# CHAPTER 10 — 地形

## A. IDENTITY

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **Number** | 10 of 13 |
| **Chinese title** | 地形 |
| **Pinyin** | *dì xíng* |
| **Gloss** | 地 ground / earth · 形 form, configuration, shape — the same 形 as Chapter 4's 軍形 |
| **Standard English title** | "Terrain" (Giles); "Configurations of Terrain" (Sawyer) |
| **Sharper alternative** | **"Ground Does Not Lose Battles"** |

*Why the alternative.* The chapter is filed under terrain and read as a terrain manual, but its second half is an audit of six defeats in which terrain appears **nowhere**. Sun Tzu builds a taxonomy of ground and then withdraws it as an excuse: 非天之災，將之過也 — not Heaven's calamity, the general's fault. The title should name the removal, not the taxonomy.

---

## B. THE CORE STRATEGIC DICTUM

> **Ground settles nothing on its own. It multiplies whatever a commander has already built, and it returns the account: every army broken on a slope was broken before it reached one.**

**Unpacking (≈195 words).** Chapter 10 has two halves that most readers treat as one, and the join is the whole argument. The first half classifies six configurations — 通 accessible, 挂 entangling, 支 deadlocked, 隘 narrow, 險 precipitous, 遠 distant — and gives each a rule. Three of those rules contain an order to attack (通: 以戰則利; 挂: 敵無備，出而勝之; 支: 令敵半出而擊之); the other three are about arriving first, filling the gap, or declining to move at all. The second half classifies six ways armies are destroyed — 走 flight, 弛 slackness, 陷 collapse, 崩 ruin, 亂 disorder, 北 rout — and not one of them is caused by ground. They are caused by force committed against odds already known, weak officers over strong troops, strong officers over weak troops, a senior subordinate who fights his own quarrel under a commander who never gauged him, a chief too lax to be understood, and a commander who cannot read what he is facing. Terrain is put in its place in the same chapter that catalogues it: 夫地形者，兵之助也 — ground is an **aid to military action**, not the action.

**Where the popular reading is wrong.** The internet reads this chapter as "get the high ground" and stops. That misses that it is the chapter where Sun Tzu tells a general to **disobey his sovereign** — fight when ordered not to, refuse when ordered to — and where he warns that a commander who loves his men but cannot command them has bred 驕子, spoiled children, and owns useless troops. It also misses that 助 is not a demotion to irrelevance: the same chapter says 不知地形之不可以戰，勝之半也 — not knowing the ground costs you half of victory. Ground is worth exactly half, and the general owns the other half and the blame for both. Chapter 10 is not about hills. It exists to take away the two most popular defences: bad ground and bad orders.

## C. THREE KEY LINES

### C1 — terrain put in its place

| | |
|---|---|
| **Chinese (trad.)** | 夫地形者，兵之助也。料敵制勝，計險阨遠近，上將之道也。 |
| **Pinyin** | *Fú dì xíng zhě, bīng zhī zhù yě. Liào dí zhì shèng, jì xiǎn è yuǎn jìn, shàng jiàng zhī dào yě.* |
| **Literal** | "Now, terrain is an aid to military action. To gauge the enemy and secure victory; to reckon the precipitous and the constricted, the far and the near — this is the way of the superior general." |
| **Modern** | "Ground helps. It does not decide. Measuring your enemy, and measuring how steep, how tight, how far — that is the job, and it is the general's job, not the ground's." |
| **Confidence** | **High** on 夫地形者，兵之助也 and 上將之道也. **Translation caution:** 兵 here is *warfare / the army*, not "a soldier." Giles's "the soldier's best ally" is a 1910 artefact and must not be reproduced or argued from; 助 means auxiliary to the general's work, not negligible — read it against 不知地形之不可以戰，勝之半也 and the chapter's closing 知天知地，勝乃可全. **Medium** on the middle clause: editions print 計險**阨**遠近 and 計險**隘**遠近 (both *è / ài*, both "constricted"), and some witnesses read 計險**易**遠近, paralleling Chapter 1's 險易. Treat the character as an editorial variant and name the edition. (The reading 陋 was carried in an earlier draft of this packet with no witness attached and has been struck.) |

### C2 — the removal of the alibi

| | |
|---|---|
| **Chinese (trad.)** | 凡此六者，非天之災，將之過也。 |
| **Pinyin** | *Fán cǐ liù zhě, fēi tiān zhī zāi, jiàng zhī guò yě.* |
| **Literal** | "In all these six cases, it is not a calamity of Heaven; it is the fault of the general." |
| **Modern** | "None of these six is misfortune sent from Heaven. Every one of them is a failure committed by the general." |
| **Confidence** | **High** on the sense; **medium** on the exact characters. The 十一家注 line and most modern editions read 非**天**之災; the 武經七書 line reads 非**天地**之災. Print the variant you are quoting and caption the edition. The introduction to the six is likewise unstable across editions — 故兵有走者 / 凡兵有走者 — and the head-word of the list should be captioned, not smoothed. |

### C3 — the general who is worth having

| | |
|---|---|
| **Chinese (trad.)** | 進不求名，退不避罪，唯人是保，而利合於主，國之寶也。 |
| **Pinyin** | *Jìn bù qiú míng, tuì bù bì zuì, wéi rén shì bǎo, ér lì hé yú zhǔ, guó zhī bǎo yě.* |
| **Literal** | "Advancing, he does not seek renown; withdrawing, he does not evade blame; it is only the people he protects, and the advantage accords with the ruler — he is the treasure of the state." |
| **Modern** | "He attacks without wanting the credit and withdraws without minding the blame. What he is protecting is the people, and it happens to serve the throne. That man is the treasure of the state." |
| **Confidence** | **High** on the line. **Medium** on one character: many editions read 唯**民**是保 rather than 唯**人**是保. The 民 → 人 substitution is the standard Tang naming taboo on 李世**民** (Emperor Taizong) working through the transmission; both readings are legitimately in print. **This affects the gloss, not only the graph:** if 民 is the earlier reading, the object of protection is *the populace*, not the soldiery — so render "the people (民; 人 in the taboo-affected line)" and never "the men." One further variant to caption: 而利合於主 / 而利於主. |

*Also load-bearing, held back for the Codex and the node:* 戰道必勝，主曰無戰，必戰可也；戰道不勝，主曰必戰，無戰可也 (fight though the sovereign forbids it; refuse though he commands it) — and the chapter's closing 知彼知己，勝乃不殆；知天知地，勝乃可全. Note the wording: Chapter 3 has 知彼知己，**百戰不殆**; Chapter 10 does not merely append sky and ground to that couplet, it **rewrites its second half** to 勝乃不殆 before extending it. Some editions close 勝乃**不窮** rather than 勝乃可全. Do not caption this as "the famous line, extended."

## D. THE VIGNETTE

### **THE BONES AT YAO**
**Winter 628/627 – summer 627 BCE (Lu, Duke Xi, years 32–33). The twin ridges of Yao (殽/崤), between the Qin heartland and the Zhou royal domain — the defiles east of modern Sanmenxia, Henan.**
**Duke Mu of Qin (秦穆公) · Jian Shu (蹇叔), his old counsellor · Meng Ming Shi (孟明視), commanding · Xian Zhen of Jin (先軫) · Duke Xiang of Jin (晉襄公).**

The message comes from Qi Zi (杞子), one of three named Qin commanders left garrisoning Zheng with Feng Sun (逢孫) and Yang Sun (楊孫). It is the sweetest thing Duke Mu has ever read. *They have put me in charge of the key to their north gate. Bring an army up quietly and the state is yours.*

Jian Shu is old enough to be tactless. Wearing an army out on a march to strike something far away is not a thing he has heard of. The men will arrive spent; the defender will be rested and waiting; a thousand *li* is not a distance anyone crosses in secret. *The army will labour and get nothing for it, and it will turn resentful.*

The Duke does not answer the argument. He sets it aside and calls Meng Ming, Xi Qishu and Bai Yibing to take the army out through the east gate.

Jian Shu stands at the roadside as the column passes and weeps in front of it, which in this century is a serious act. *Mengzi. I will see this army go out. I will not see it come in.*

The reply comes back from the Duke, along the road, in front of everyone. *What would you know? If you had died at a middling age, the trees on your grave would be an armspan around by now.*

Jian Shu's son is in the column. He weeps a second time, for him, and tells him where the Jin will be waiting. There are two ridges at Yao: on the southern one lies the tomb of Gao, sovereign of Xia; on the northern one King Wen once sheltered from wind and rain. *You will die between them. I will come and gather your bones.*

Spring. The column passes the north gate of the Zhou capital. The armoured men to left and right take their helmets off and drop down in the correct show of respect to the King's city — and then three hundred chariot crews vault straight back aboard, showing off. A boy of the Zhou house named Wangsun Man watches it done and says, flatly, that this army will lose. *Light, and without manners. Light men plan badly. Men without manners are careless. Careless, unable to plan, and going into broken ground.*

At Hua, a Zheng merchant named Xian Gao, on the road to trade at Zhou, meets the column head-on. He understands what he is looking at before it understands him. He goes to the Qin commanders with four cured hides carried in front and twelve oxen behind and greets them as expected guests — *my ruler heard you were coming and sent me out to provision your following* — and puts a rider on the road home at a gallop.

The key to the north gate is worth nothing now. Meng Ming cannot besiege Zheng and cannot stay. The army destroys the small state of Hua, where it is already standing, and turns for home.

Home is west, and west is the ridges.

In Jin, Duke Wen lies newly dead and unburied. Xian Zhen wants the ambush. Luan Zhi objects: Qin once did Jin a kindness, and the lord is not yet in the ground. Xian Zhen answers with Heaven, with kinship, and with ritual — never with a force ratio. Qin ignored Jian Shu and worked its people out of greed; Heaven is handing it to us, and a gift from Heaven is not refused. Qin did not mourn our lord, and it has gone against our own clan; a state without ritual is owed no favour. *Let an enemy go for one day and you have bought trouble for generations.* The new Duke Xiang has his white mourning dyed black so that he can wear it into a line. The Jiang Rong are called up at speed.

Fourth month, the day *xinsi*. The Qin column is in the defile. There is no room. Chariots cannot turn, the flanks are stone, the head of the column cannot see the tail and neither can reach the other. It is not a battle in the sense the column expected. It is a lid coming down.

All three commanders are taken alive.

Then Jin gives them away. Wen Ying — Duke Xiang's principal mother, and a daughter of Qin — asks for them: send them home to be executed there, and let her lord's ruler have the satisfaction of doing it with his own hands. The Duke agrees. Xian Zhen comes to court and asks after the prisoners and is told. Fighting men took them on the field, he says, and a woman talked them loose in a morning; we have thrown away what the army won and grown our enemy, and we have not long left. He turns away without a bow and spits on the floor. A rider goes out after them. He reaches the river, and they are already in the boat.

Outside the Qin capital, Duke Mu comes out to the suburbs in white and waits at the roadside for what comes back. He faces the returning army and weeps in front of it. *I went against Jian Shu, and so I brought this shame on you three. The fault is mine.* He does not replace Meng Ming.

Ground did not destroy the Qin army. Ground was present when a decision taken in a warm hall a thousand *li* west arrived to be paid for.

**Record vs. legend.** The *Zuozhuan* (Duke Xi, years 32–33) carries all of the above: Qi Zi's message; Jian Shu's opposition and the Duke's setting it aside (公辭焉); the army called out through the east gate; Jian Shu's weeping at the roadside; the Duke's reply about the grave-trees (中壽，爾墓之木拱矣) sent **after** the weeping, not in answer to the strategic argument; Jian Shu's second weeping over **one** son in the column (蹇叔之子與師); the two ridges and 余收爾骨焉; the helmets and 超乘者三百乘 at the Zhou north gate with Wangsun Man's verdict; Xian Gao as 鄭商人弦高將市於周, with 乘韋先，牛十二, at 滑; 滅滑而還; Xian Zhen's grounds (天奉我也 · 縱敵患生 · 秦不哀吾喪而伐吾同姓); 子墨衰絰; the defeat 夏四月辛巳; the capture of all three; Wen Ying's request (使歸就戮於秦，以逞寡君之志) and the release; Xian Zhen's 不顧而唾 and the chase to the river; and Duke Mu's 孤違蹇叔，以辱二三子，孤之罪也 and 不替孟明.

**The word for what happened.** The annals do not say Qin lost a battle; the graph is 敗 (*bài*) — an army *broken*. Chunqiu, Xi 33: 晉人及姜戎敗秦師于殽. The vignette stages that as a lid closing because that is what the graph means, not as a stylistic choice.

**On the dating.** The army leaves in the **winter** of Xi 32; the Zhou north-gate incident is 三十三年春; the battle is 夏四月辛巳 — **summer**. On-screen the campaign must read *winter 628/627 – summer 627 BCE*, never "spring 627."

**Same campaign, not the same narrative.** *Shiji* 5 (秦本紀) covers these events but is not a corroborating duplicate. There, Baili Xi **and** Jian Shu jointly oppose the expedition; Meng Ming is described as Baili Xi's son; the march is placed in the spring of Duke Mu's thirty-third year rather than the previous winter; 滑 is called a Jin border town rather than an independent statelet; and the helmet-vaulting and the four hides are both absent — Shiji's Xian Gao is a trader driving twelve oxen to sell at Zhou (販賣賈人持十二牛將賣之周). Use *Zuozhuan* for the detail we stage, and never let Shiji colour arrive on screen wearing a *Zuozhuan* citation.

**Legend or later rhetoric.** The celebrated line that "not one horse, not one wheel returned" (匹馬隻輪無反者) is *Gongyang*, and it is not extra colour on the same story — it is a rival annihilation tradition, flatly incompatible with the fact both *Zuozhuan* and *Shiji* record, that all three commanders were taken alive and released. The identification of Jian Shu's sons as 西乞術 and 白乙丙 is later and contested; the *Gongyang* tradition has **both** 百里子 and 蹇叔子 sending sons, a different family tree entirely. And every speech quoted here — Jian Shu's, the Duke's, the boy's, Xian Zhen's — is *Zuozhuan* set-piece oratory, composed to the standards of its own genre. The events are recorded. The dialogue is literature.

## E. VISUAL CUE

**Environment.** Not a ravine. Chapter 7 already owns the enclosed dry defile at the last hour before dawn and does not lend it. Chapter 10 is a **terraced loess tableland** at first light — the stepped yellow-earth country of western Henan: flat plateau surfaces stacked one above another, separated by hard vertical drops, receding to a horizon of more of the same. The argument here is *elevation profile*, not corridor. The six configurations are six **steps and aspects of one escarpment system**, laid across the sheet as six isocontour densities, so the reader learns the taxonomy by walking the terraces and reading the drops: 通 the broad top surface with two open approaches; 挂 a step whose face goes down easily and comes back up only once; 支 a facing pair with a sunken draw between them, punishing whoever enters it first; 隘 a re-entrant narrowing to a single gate; 險 a scarp with a lit crown; 遠 the far tableland across a plain too wide to reach. No army is drawn at the top of the chapter. There is only ground, and the ground is dry. Paper fibre θ is set **across** the fall lines, so ink cannot travel the terrace edges.

**Dominant ink behaviour — gravity.** This is the chapter where **G, the grain field, is literally the terrain gradient.** Wet ink released anywhere runs downhill, pools on the flat surfaces, and refuses the risers: K∥ tracks the fall line at the top of the permitted band, K⊥ at the bottom. The reader's own marks perform the tactical analysis before the reader does.

**The 隘 gate — specified, not hand-waved.** In the narrowing re-entrant, the 4-octave FBM edge displacement on the 0.02 < D < 0.28 band of each facing wall is driven by a sampler whose **spatial frequency is modulated inversely by the gap**: ω = ω₀ / max(d, ε), where d is the distance in UV units between the two opposing isocontours. As the gate closes, displacement on both margins packs into tight tooth-like serration until the two bands clamp and the channel is shut. One scalar frequency term; no wave equation, no interference, and **no "beat frequency"** — the reader sees the walls getting *sharper* as they get closer, which is the whole tell.

**Particle system — on the surface, never in the air.** 飛白 chaff only: fibre flecks scraped off the tooth wherever a stroke outruns its reserve. Implemented as a 2-D compute particle set **constrained to the substrate plane (z = 0)**. Spawn at brush coordinates where velocity v > v_thresh and reserve R → 0; slide along the lateral wind vector **W**; decelerate against paper friction over 0.4–0.8 s of scene time; halt; deposit a permanent +0.005 micro-speckle to local D. **Nothing is airborne, and nothing is lifted.** Chapter 7 owns 塵 — grain-derived dust raised by a marching column — and Chapter 10 has no column at the top of the chapter and no dust: everything that moves here was scraped off the paper by the reader's own stroke. (Reserved and not used: embers — Chapter 12 owns fire; frost — Chapter 11 owns crystallisation.)

**Lighting.** One raking source at ~7° elevation from frame right. One scarp face is lit to paper `#E8E2D4`; the opposite falls to ink `#1B2321`. This is not mood: 高陽, the high sunny side, is prescribed by the text for **two** of the six configurations — 通形 (先居高陽，利糧道) and 險形 (必居高陽以待敵) — and the chapter's key advantage is therefore rendered as *literal illumination* with no colour spent at all. Gold appears exactly once, as a hairline on the Historian's citation rule.

**Camera.** **24mm** for the entire terrain passage and nothing else — the world without people, and the width as punishment. One unbroken crane, roughly forty seconds of scene time, rising from the floor of a draw up the lit scarp until the whole terrace stack reads at once as a section drawing. It cranes and it tracks; it **never cuts**, because nothing in this passage is a decision the reader made. **There is no 180mm anywhere in Chapter 10.** The opposing force is never inserted, never compressed, never shown — the chapter's argument is that the enemy is not the variable, and withholding the enemy lens is how that argument is made in the grammar rather than in the caption. (The 180mm allocation for this pair of chapters is spent once, in Chapter 11, across the Zhang.) Letterbox holds 2.39:1 until the node.

**The six calamities.** They are not a new environment. The camera returns to the *identical* plate — same terrace, same light, same fibre — six times, and six differently-drawn armies fail on it in six different ways. Terrain is the constant in the equation; only the strokes change. The 崩 pass is the one to build first, and it needs two moving parts, not one: a subordinate stroke leaving the formation on its own vector at its own velocity, going feibai as it goes — **and a command stroke that never reaches for it**, because the text's 崩 is 大吏怒而不服，遇敵懟而自戰，將**不知其能**: the chief did not know what that man was. Draw the ignorance as well as the anger.

**THE ONE IMAGE.** *One 淡墨 contact on the crown of the lit scarp — a single touch by the reader, no annotation, no UI.* The wet pigment advects down **G** along the steepest fall line with K∥ at the top of the permitted band, and comes to rest where ‖∇h‖ → 0: the saddle at the foot of the re-entrant. It pools. Local M there rises to ≈0.85 and stays high while everything around it dries, which makes that pool the one place on the sheet where further ink can still spread — the one place a force can still be committed without leaving a hard edge. *The reader put a mark on a crest and the substrate put it where the ambush goes.* This is not the paper being wise. It is gradient descent on a height field, run at reading speed, in the only medium this site has.

**Audio.** Wind off the terrace edges, panned hard and constant, its filter opening as the crane rises, so height is audible before it is visible. No drums until the node loads. At the top of the crane — the moment the whole stack is legible at once — room tone ducks and does not return for four seconds, and what fills the hole is nothing at all. There is no enemy to hear, because this chapter has not got one.

## F. TACTIC DECISION NODE — 挂形 / ENTANGLING GROUND

*(Letterbox tightens 2.39:1 → 2.76:1 on LOAD. Brush audible. 行草 on the operative term. House style: the graph is **挂** throughout this document, matching the recension quoted; **掛** is not used.)*

**THE SITUATION — Commander's voice.**
> You hold the crest. His camp is in the bowl below, and his lines are loose — no pickets forward, cook-fires still burning past the hour. The face between you is one-way: fast down, and only once. **可以往，難以返.** His water is in the bowl at his feet. Yours is two ridges back down the reverse slope.

**OPTION A — GO DOWN.**
Commit the main body downhill now, at speed, on the unreadiness you can see. Take the camp before he can dress a line.

**OPTION B — STAY UP.**
Hold the crest, put a screening force across his water, and make him climb. Spend days.

**SIMULATED VISUAL CONSEQUENCE — A.**
The camera cuts (you caused it) to **24mm** on the floor of the bowl. Your formation is released as one loaded 濃墨 stroke down the fall line, and the Law does what it was built to do: the stroke accelerates along G, outruns its finite reserve, and arrives **feibai** at roughly 60% of the descent — dry flecks where the mass was. The node does not tell you in advance which of two plates you have bought.

*If 無備 was real.* The camp's ground is dry and unsized. Your flecks take a hard, final edge on it and his cluster falls below D < 0.05 within two beats: you have the camp. You also have this. The crest you left is deposited over by your own descent at high D, monotonic, uphill — **the reserved white you were holding above the bowl is off the sheet permanently** — and you finish the node standing in the lowest, wettest ground on the plate with your supply running back over a face you cannot re-ink. Roughly **+0.05** page density, and the whole of your high ground, for a camp.

*If 無備 was displayed.* The camp is already wet: high M, low D, prepared. Your stroke blooms on contact and its edge is gone inside two seconds of scene time. The descent line then dams against the terrace riser behind you and spills sideways into the draws as broken, striated feibai smears — an army disassembling against the ground it came down, still recognisable, no longer a formation. Roughly **+0.09**, a tide line across the bowl that stays on the sheet to the end of the site, and no route home that is not through your own dried ink.

**SIMULATED VISUAL CONSEQUENCE — B.**
No cut. The camera holds and tracks laterally, and the cost arrives as a clock rather than an event. Your crest line stays 淡墨 — contingent, still able to bleed — but a crest line must be **re-drawn every beat to stay legible**, and every re-inking is a deposit: holding costs **+0.008 per beat, for as long as you hold.** Below, his camp's M decays from 0.62 toward 0.20 as your screen sits on his water, and his edges harden as they dry. Three probing strokes climb toward you, each going feibai on the gradient, each stopping short and leaving a tide line lower than the last — three bright permanent scars on the face, a staircase of his failures, drawn in his ink.

Then your own bill. You are on a dry height with your water two ridges back: from the fourth beat your crest deposit begins going **feibai in place, without moving** — the only site in the whole work where exhaustion is generated by time instead of velocity. And on the sixth beat, if he still has a road, he takes it: his cluster leaves the bowl intact, unattacked, and legible, and what remains on the plate is your thickened crest, your accumulating tide lines, and an empty bowl. About **+0.05** and five beats of the chapter's clock, to hold a hill with nothing under it.

**HISTORICAL VERDICT.**

*Sun Tzu says:* 挂形者，敵無備，出而勝之；敵若有備，出而不勝，難以返，不利 — on entangling ground, if the enemy is **genuinely** unready, go out and beat him; if he is ready, you go out, you fail, and you cannot get back. The whole fork rests on one perceptual question the ground cannot answer for you: is 無備 real, or displayed? Note what the chapter does **not** say here. 險形者，我先居之，必居高陽以待敵 — *hold the height and make him come* — is the rule for a **different configuration**, and it appears in this node as the rival doctrine Option B is borrowing, not as the rule for the face you are standing above.

*A downhill strike where the unreadiness was real.* **Mount Dingjun, 219 CE.** Liu Bei reached the mountain first and held it — 我先居之, from the winning side. Xiahou Yuan came up to contest ground already occupied. He split his force in half to reinforce Zhang He on the eastern perimeter and went out with light troops to repair burned abatis (鹿角) on the southern perimeter, and Fa Zheng, standing on the height beside Liu Bei, said: now. Huang Zhong came off the crown with the drums going and Xiahou Yuan, a senior Wei commander, was killed in the field. *Sanguozhi* Wei 9 (Xiahou Yuan), Shu 6 (Huang Zhong), Shu 7 (Fa Zheng). **Correct the story that is usually told about this battle:** Xiahou Yuan was not a man who held the height and threw it away. He was below, divided, and holding a spade, contesting a hill someone else had already taken.

*Holding the height, and losing anyway.* **Jieting, 228 CE.** Ma Su was Zhuge Liang's own appointed vanguard commander, and what the record charges him with is 違亮節度 — departing from Liang's dispositions. Wang Ping's account: 舍水上山，舉措煩擾 — he left the water and went up the hill, and his handling was fussy and disruptive. Zhang He's: 謖依阻南山，不下據城 — he took his stand on the southern hill and would not come down to hold the walled position — 郃絕其汲道，擊，大破之. *Sanguozhi* Shu 5 (Zhuge Liang), Shu 9 (Ma Liang), Shu 13 (Wang Ping), Wei 17 (Zhang He); *Zizhi Tongjian* 71. **Height is not the doctrine. Arriving first, holding water and staying is the doctrine** — and 高陽 in 必居高陽以待敵 means the sunny side of a position you can supply, not simply the top of something.

*And note what this is not.* Jieting is not the chapter's 崩. 崩 requires 大吏怒而不服，遇敵懟而自戰 — a senior subordinate refusing his chief in anger and fighting his own battle — and Ma Su was the man Liang put on that ground. The nearest slot is 亂 (將弱不嚴，教道不明), and the honest position is that Sun Tzu's six do not have a compartment for every defeat: do not force one. The sources also record what Ma Su **did**, not what he was thinking; the reasoning usually attributed to him is later, and the vignette and node must not put a motive in his mouth.

*The Historian's closing note, at M ≈ 0:* Both men belong to Chapter 10's second half, not its first. Neither was beaten by ground.

## G. CODEX MODE ENTRY — 10 地形

*(16:9. Orthographic. Ink frozen at current density. Grotesque, ragged right, 62–68 characters. Insert plates remain 2.39:1, inset like a slip on a desk.)*

**DICTUM.** Ground settles nothing on its own. It multiplies whatever a commander has already built, and it returns the account: every army broken on a slope was broken before it reached one.

- **Six grounds, and three of the six carry an order to attack.** 通 accessible (先居高陽，利糧道 — take the sunny height first, keep the supply road: 以戰則利) · 挂 entangling (easy in, hard out — 敵無備，出而勝之, but if he is ready you fail and cannot return) · 支 deadlocked (do not move first — 引而去之，令敵半出而擊之: draw him and strike the half that has emerged) · 隘 narrow (get there first and **fill it**, 盈之以待敵; if he has filled it, do not follow) · 險 precipitous (先居之，必居高陽以待敵; if he holds it, 引而去之) · 遠 distant (勢均 at long range: do not offer battle). **先居高陽 is prescribed for two of the six, not one.** The verbs are occupy, fill, decline, withdraw — and, three times, attack.
- **Six defeats, and not one is caused by terrain.** 走 flight — *夫勢均，以一擊十*: forces otherwise matched, and he throws one against ten · 弛 slackness — 卒強吏弱 · 陷 collapse — 吏強卒弱 · 崩 ruin — 大吏怒而不服，遇敵懟而自戰，**將不知其能**: a senior man fighting his own quarrel under a chief who never gauged him · 亂 disorder — 將弱不嚴，教道不明，吏卒無常，陳兵縱橫 · 北 rout — 將不能料敵，**以少合眾，以弱擊強**，兵無選鋒: he cannot read the enemy, he engages a superior force with an inferior one, and he sends no picked edge in first. 非天之災，將之過也.
- **The chapter licenses disobedience, then charges for it.** 戰道必勝，主曰無戰，必戰可也 — fight when victory is certain and the sovereign forbids it; refuse when it is not and he commands it. The price of that licence is 進不求名，退不避罪: you may not take the credit, and you may not duck the blame. And the closing formula is not the famous one repeated — Chapter 3's 知彼知己，百戰不殆 is rewritten here as 知彼知己，勝乃不殆 and then extended: 知天知地，勝乃可全.

**APPLY IT.** When an undertaking collapses, audit the chain of command before you arraign the circumstances. Ask which of the six it was: force committed against odds nobody disputed (走); authority that could not reach the ranks (弛); ranks that could not carry the authority (陷); a senior man fighting his own quarrel under a chief who never gauged him (崩); a chief too lax to be understood (亂); or a commander who could not read what he faced and sent no picked edge in first (北). If none of the six fits, say plainly that you were unlucky. It will not fit often.

# CHAPTER 11 — 九地

## A. IDENTITY

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| **Number** | 11 of 13 |
| **Chinese title** | 九地 |
| **Pinyin** | *jiǔ dì* |
| **Gloss** | 九 nine · 地 ground. The longest chapter in the book. |
| **Standard English title** | "The Nine Situations" (Giles); "Nine Terrains" (Sawyer) |
| **Sharper alternative** | **"Distance From Home"** |

*Why the alternative.* The nine are not nine kinds of scenery — but neither are they nine points on one ruler, and the packet must not pretend otherwise. **Four are depth bands**: 散地 dispersive (your own soil), 輕地 light (just inside his), 重地 serious (deep, his cities behind you), 死地 death (the far bank). **Five are terrain-quality and access categories** that interleave with those bands rather than extending them: 爭地 contested, 交地 crossing, 衢地 junction of three states, 圮地 bad going, 圍地 hemmed-in. The taxonomy is mixed. The *argument* is not: the chapter's engine is 深入則專 — the deeper in, the tighter they hold — and its real subject is what that single number does to men who have not been told they are on it. "The Nine Situations" makes it sound like a filing cabinet. Name the axis the argument runs on, and footnote that four of the nine sit on it directly.

---

## B. THE CORE STRATEGIC DICTUM

> **Courage is a property of ground, not of men: take away every road but the one through the enemy, and you will get from ordinary soldiers a ferocity you could never have ordered out of them.**

**Unpacking (≈205 words).** Each of the nine grounds carries a one-line instruction, and the instructions are of two different kinds. Four say *do not be here*: 散地則無戰, 輕地則無止, 爭地則無攻, 交地則無絕. Five say *this is how you operate while you are here*: 衢地則合交 (make your alliances), 重地則掠 (forage), 圮地則行 (keep moving through), 圍地則謀 (scheme your way out), 死地則戰 (fight). The chapter then runs the whole list a second time in the first person — 散地吾將一其志, 輕地吾將使之屬, 圍地吾將**塞其闕** (*hemmed in, I will seal my own gap*), 死地吾將**示之以不活** (*on death ground I will show them they are not going to live*) — and it is that second list, not the first, where the commander stops describing ground and starts describing what he does to his own men. The mechanism underneath is behavioural: 深入則專 — go deep and men concentrate; men near home drift homeward. Cohesion is not loyalty, it is arithmetic performed by frightened people, and the chapter proves it with a hard image: men of Wu and Yue, hereditary enemies, crossing in one boat, meeting a storm, and helping each other as a left hand helps a right.

**Where the popular reading is wrong.** "Burn the boats" has been laundered into a motivational technique — manufacture urgency, remove your escape route, unlock your potential. Sun Tzu is not describing self-improvement. He is describing what a commander does to **other people**, and he says so without flinching: 愚士卒之耳目，使之無知 — keep the troops' eyes and ears in ignorance; 犯之以事，勿告以言 — hurl them at the task and do not tell them the plan; 若驅群羊 — like driving a flock of sheep. Note also the asymmetry, which the book states twice over: Chapter 7's 圍師必闕 (leave *his* surrounded army a gap) set against this chapter's own 圍地吾將塞其闕 (seal *my* gap when I am the one hemmed in). 死地 is ninth of nine because it is the worst place on the list. It is not a technique. It is the bill.

## D. THE VIGNETTE

### **THREE DAYS' RATIONS**
**Winter, 208–207 BCE. The north bank of the Zhang River (漳水), and the siege lines around Julu (鉅鹿) — modern Pingxiang County, Hebei.**
**Xiang Yu (項羽) · Song Yi (宋義), his nominal commander-in-chief · Ying Bu (英布, 當陽君黥布) and General Pu (蒲將軍) · Wang Li (王離) and Zhang Han (章邯), for Qin.**

Forty-six days at Anyang. That is what the campaign has been.

Song Yi holds the seal and the theory: let Qin and Zhao exhaust each other at Julu, then walk in on whichever is left. It is not a stupid theory. It is being executed in cold and heavy rain, by men eating taro and beans, in an army that has stopped believing it is an army. Song Yi's son has been appointed chancellor to Qi; the father rides out with him as far as Wuyan, and there is a long banquet there, and wine at it.

At dawn Xiang Yu goes into the commander's tent to make the morning report and comes out holding Song Yi's head. He tells the assembled officers the man was in correspondence with Qi against Chu. Nobody contests it. They make him acting commander-in-chief before the body is cold.

He sends Ying Bu and General Pu across the Zhang first with twenty thousand between them — enough to prove the crossing, not enough to lift the siege. The Zhao commander inside Julu sends again, begging. So Xiang Yu takes the whole army over the water.

And on the far bank he has the boats sunk.

Then the cooking-pots and the steamers, broken. Then the shelters they have just finished building, burned. Each man is issued three days' food and nothing else. There is no baggage train behind him now, because there is no behind. The purpose is stated plainly and it is not morale: the men are to be shown that they will certainly die, so that not one of them keeps a thought of coming back.

What they are walking toward is not a field army. It is a system. Zhang Han has built a 甬道 — a walled corridor of rammed earth, a supply artery with its own ramparts — running grain up to Wang Li's siege lines around Julu. Qin is not besieging a city so much as plumbing one. Xiang Yu goes for the corridor. Nine times Chu goes in against the walled road, and cuts it, and holds it cut, and the grain stops.

Round the city sit the armies of the other feudal lords: ten fortified camps and more, in place for weeks, not attacking, because they have eyes and can count. When the Chu assault goes in, they climb up onto their own ramparts and watch it from there.

What they see is the point of the chapter, and it is not tactics. It is a body of ordinary men — recently mutinous, recently starving, forty-six days bored — put by their own general in a position with exactly one exit, fighting in a manner the watchers can only describe in terms of noise. The shouting of Chu shakes the sky. One soldier of Chu is worth ten. The lords on the ramparts are frightened while winning.

Wang Li is taken alive. Su Jiao is killed in the field. She Jian, offered surrender, sets a fire and burns himself in it rather than take terms.

Afterwards Xiang Yu summons the lords who watched. They come through the gate of his camp on their knees, shuffling forward, and not one of them lifts his eyes to the man in the tent. From this day the lords at Julu are his — 諸侯上將軍, supreme commander of the allied lords — and they attach themselves to him.

These men were not persuaded. They were not briefed. 若驅群羊 — driven like a flock, out and back, none of them knowing where. He did not raise their morale. He deleted the alternatives and collected what was left.

**Record vs. legend.** *Shiji* 7 (項羽本紀) is the source for the narrative above: 留四十六日不進 at Anyang; 天寒大雨，士卒凍飢 and 食芋菽; 遣其子宋襄**相齊**，身送之至**無鹽**，飲酒高會 — the son goes to Qi as **chancellor** (相), not to a governorship, and the banquet is at **Wuyan**, on the road east, not in the halted camp; the killing of Song Yi in his tent and Xiang Yu's elevation to 假上將軍; 遣當陽君、蒲將軍將卒二萬渡河 — the advance crossing is under **two** named commanders, Ying Bu and 蒲將軍; 悉引兵渡河，皆沉船，破釜甑，燒廬舍，持三日糧，以示士卒必死，無一還心; 九戰，絕其甬道，大破之; 虜王離，殺蘇角; 涉閒不降楚，自燒殺; 諸侯軍救鉅鹿下者十餘壁，莫敢縱兵 and 諸將皆**從**壁上觀; 楚兵呼聲動天 and 楚戰士無不一以當十, with 諸侯軍無不人人惴恐; 入轅門，無不膝行而前，莫敢仰視; and 項羽由是始為**諸侯上將軍**，諸侯皆屬焉. The *Zizhi Tongjian* follows it under the third year of Qin Er Shi.

**Corrections this packet has had to make, and the screen must carry.** The boats were **sunk** (皆沉船), not left beached: there is no attested moment of men eating within sight of their hulls, and any such image belongs to us and must be labelled as staged, not narrated as record. What Xiang Yu gained on the day was the submission of the lords **present at Julu** — Zhang Han's Qin field army was still in being and surrendered separately, months later; do not write "commander of every anti-Qin force in the empire." And the phrase for watching from the wall is *Shiji*'s 從壁上觀; the familiar four-character 作壁上觀 is a later idiomatic freeze of it, exactly as 破釜沉舟 is a freeze of 皆沉船，破釜甑.

**Uncertain, contested or later.** The month of the main action is not given in the *Benji* narrative; Xu Guang's 集解 places the **advance crossing** in the eleventh month of Qin Er Shi's third year, which is firmer than a blanket "some time that winter" but is commentary, not text. **Which Anyang** is meant is disputed — the son's posting is to Qi and the banquet is at Wuyan, well to the east — so do not pin it on a map without saying so on screen. 九戰 is textually unsettled: the mainstream reading is 九戰，絕其甬道, some editions punctuate 九絕其甬道, the parallel account in the Zhang Er–Chen Yu biography has 數絕甬道 and divides the work among the allied lords differently, and "nine" may in any case be idiomatic for *many*. Xiang Yu's age at Julu is arithmetic, not testimony: *Shiji* gives 初起時年二十四 — twenty-four when he first rose, in 209 BCE — and the birth year of 232 and the familiar "about twenty-six at Julu" are later reckoning built on that one sentence. And the kneeling scene, with its perfect cinematic geometry, is Sima Qian writing roughly a century after the event, with a novelist's instincts and a case to make about Xiang Yu.

## E. VISUAL CUE

**Environment.** Not a place — a **traverse**. One continuous lateral move, right to left, across a single enormous sheet, in which the ground under the army changes nine times without a single cut. The nine grounds are nine bands of *substrate*, not nine landscapes: the paper's own sizing S, tooth and warmth shift band by band as the army moves away from the right-hand edge of the sheet, which is home. The right deckle edge is 散地. The left deckle edge is 死地, and past it the substrate simply stops — raw torn fibre, then nothing, because there is no paper on the other side of the river.

**Dominant ink behaviour — cohesion as a function of depth.** The army is one cluster of high-D strokes, and its **bleed radius shrinks as it travels left**. Specified, not asserted. On top of the substrate grain field, Chapter 11 adds a **centripetal term** toward the cluster centroid **c** = (1/N)Σxᵢ:

> **G_total(x) = G_paper(x) − κ(u)·(x − c)/‖x − c‖**

where u ∈ [0,1] is the traverse coordinate with home at u = 0. κ(0) = 0, so in 散地 the cluster bleeds outward normally — loose, feathered, wide, spreading toward the near edge, a formation with somewhere to go. κ(u) rises monotonically with depth while K∥ and K⊥ both fall, so band by band the isocontour tightens, peripheral feathering is drawn back into the body, and in the last band the cluster is a single dense object with almost no bleed at all. That is 深入則專, expressed as a scalar and a sign.

**This is not Chapter 10's behaviour inverted, and the two must never be described as the same shader with the arrow flipped.** Chapter 10 runs pigment *down a height field the commander did not choose*; Chapter 11 pulls pigment *toward a centre the commander created*. Different term, same equation. If a Chapter 11 pass ever samples a terrain gradient, or a Chapter 10 pass ever computes a centroid, the shaders have been crossed.

**Substrate event — crystallisation, and it is not a particle system.** The Zhang crossing is in winter, and the chapter's signature mark is the 水痕 tide line, which per the Law is a **deposit in the accumulation pass**, not a sprite emitter and not a "seed crystal." Specification: the nine grounds are nine bands along the traverse coordinate u. At each of the eight boundaries u_k, paper sizing **S** steps upward by ΔS = +0.07 across a one-texel column. That step drives ∂M/∂t through zero along the column, and the tide-line deposit fires there, committing a hard, bright, permanent rule the full height of the sheet into the water-mark buffer. Result: **eight bright scars behind the army and none in front of it** — each one a border the reader crossed and cannot recross. Frost-bright, hard-edged, no bloom, no glow, nothing airborne. (Not used here: Chapter 10's surface chaff; Chapter 12's embers.)

**Lighting.** Flat, cold, high-cloud — a single very large soft source with no direction, because there is no sunny side on this sheet and no advantage to be had from where you stand. Ink reads at its coolest, faintly green at full density per the pine-soot ramp. **The only warm value in the chapter is the reserved white of home behind you, and it gets smaller.** Vermilion is spent exactly once, for one frame, at the point of the crossing — a single seal-press mark, unblended, beading proud of the surface: an irrevocable commitment and blood, the same pigment, the thesis stated as a material fact.

**Camera.** **24mm** for the traverse, per jurisdiction — but the *move* belongs to this chapter and to no other. Chapter 10 cranes, and rises, and reads the ground from above. Chapter 11 refuses height: this is a **ground-level dolly at ankle elevation**, running alongside the column's feet for the entire nine bands without a cut. Frozen mud, the underside of a load, the stride shortening band by band, the reserved white of home going out of frame behind a heel and not coming back. No faces — the doctrine forbids them and the dolly makes them unnecessary. The only camera events are one **85mm** insert on the named factor at the crossing (the broken 釜甑, and three days of ration in a pair of hands) and one **180mm** compression across the water onto the Qin siege wall, which we never approach and never enter. **That 180mm is the only one in Chapters 10 and 11 combined:** the enemy lens is spent here, once, on a wall. Letterbox holds 2.39:1, tightens to 2.76:1 for the node, and — the single permitted flourish — the top and bottom mounting-silk bars sit *closer together for the rest of Act III* after this chapter. The scroll has been remounted. It is not opening again until the ending.

**Sinking the boats — the mechanic.** The boats are strokes on the sheet, drawn, dried, real. The reader cannot erase them, because **D is monotonic**; that is not a limitation here, it is the point. Choosing to sink them **floods the region to 濃墨 at D = 0.95** — near-saturation, deliberately not 1.0. The boats do not disappear; they stop being legible *as boats*. And they are not gone from the surface either: while they dried, those strokes wrote themselves into the paper's normal and roughness maps as raised, sized ridges. In the final lighting pass the 7° raking source catches that relief, so beneath the matte black the hull contours read as **specular relief** — visible only because the light is low, and only to someone looking. The line of retreat becomes a solid black bar: still there, permanently there, embossed, and no longer information. You did not remove the option. You made it unreadable, in ink, in front of everyone, and it will be on the final scroll.

**THE ONE IMAGE.** *The reader's own line of retreat saturated past legibility into an unbroken black bar — and, only where the raking light crosses it, the embossed ridge of a hull still standing proud in the surface of the paper. The option is not gone. It is illegible, and it is under your hand.*

**Audio.** Drum (鼓) only, no metal (金), for the entire second half of the chapter: the withdraw-and-return signal has been taken out of the vocabulary, and the reader should register its absence before they can name it. Room tone drops to almost nothing at 死地. The Commander does not speak, because the Commander has **no voice**: the order to sink the boats arrives as the dry crack of a seal press, then one heavy drum stroke going out across the ice, then dead room tone. **No 古琴 anywhere near the Commander** — the decaying 古琴 partial belongs to the Text and to nothing else, and spending it on an order would be precisely the channel-mixing the manifesto calls a failure of command.

## F. TACTIC DECISION NODE — 死地 / DEATH GROUND

*(Letterbox 2.39:1 → 2.76:1 on LOAD. Vermilion is armed for this node and for no other node in Act III.)*

**THE SITUATION — Commander's voice.**
> You are across, and deep. His siege wall is ahead and the water is behind, and the transports that brought you are still at the shingle. Rations: three days. The Zhao inside the city will not last a fourth. **What your men do tomorrow depends on what is behind them tonight, and what is behind them is yours to set.**

**OPTION A — 焚舟破釜. TOTAL IRREVERSIBILITY.**
Sink every hull. Break every pot and steamer. Burn the shelters. Three days' food, whole army forward, nothing held back, nothing explained.

**OPTION B — 去梯示死. THE FORTIFIED BRIDGEHEAD.**
Sink the assault transports only — the men going forward have no boat — and entrench a disciplined guard on the ford behind them. Han Xin's shape at Jingxing: a line that cannot retreat, resting on a bank that is held.

**SIMULATED VISUAL CONSEQUENCE — A.**
One vermilion seal press — the only full-saturation frame in the chapter — and then the camera **cuts** (you caused it) to the **85mm** insert as the hull strokes flood to D = 0.95 and become a bar. The bar dries in six seconds of scene time and deposits a tide line along its whole upper edge: a permanent bright rule between the army and the water, legible from here to the end of the site. Then the cluster does what the entire traverse has been preparing — κ at maximum, K∥ and K⊥ bottoming out, the isocontour hardening into one object — and it goes **through in a single beat**, at the top of the permitted velocity, tearing to feibai as it goes. The Qin wall at 180mm does not fight: its D falls below 0.05 in one beat and the men become weather.

**What the sheet keeps:** the black bar; its tide line; one vermilion seal; feibai exhaustion scattered across the whole plain west of the crossing, dry and permanent; and **+0.09 page density in a single node**, the largest single expenditure in Act III. You have no reserve behind the crossing and no second attempt. Act IV opens with measurably less white than it would otherwise have had, and the ending will show you the number.

**SIMULATED VISUAL CONSEQUENCE — B.**
No cut; the camera stays at ankle height and keeps dollying. The assault's κ still rises — those men have no boat either — but a third of your D is standing on a bank behind them, so the stroke that goes in carries two-thirds of the mass. The wall does not break in one beat. It takes three: three separate advances, each stalling, each depositing a tide line where it stopped, so the field between the water and the wall ends the node ruled with **three parallel bright scars instead of one clean bar**.

**What the sheet keeps:** a wider, paler, longer-running stain across roughly twice the area for a comparable total cost (**+0.08**); three stalled tide lines instead of one; and **time** — three beats of the chapter's clock, which is three more beats of siege for the men inside Julu, who are on this sheet too, and who are drying. **What you keep:** the ford, garrisoned and legible, and a margin of reserved white at the right-hand edge that Act IV can still spend. **What you have bought and may not want:** if the third advance also stalls, the guard on the ford is the only wet mass on the plate, the front has nothing behind it but a door, and κ collapses to zero from the rear forward — the formation stops being one object and comes apart along the bank, into the water, in the direction of the thing you kept for it.

Neither branch is free and neither is scripted. A is one beat, one bar, and no future. B is three beats, twice the stain, a bank you still hold, and a third roll of the dice you may not want to make.

**HISTORICAL VERDICT.**

*Sun Tzu says:* 投之亡地然後存，陷之死地然後生 — and, unromantically, 死地則戰. But read the ninth item as an item. Four of the nine grounds are instructions for **not being there** (無戰, 無止, 無攻, 無絕); four more are instructions for **operating while you are** (合交, 掠, 行, 謀). 死地 is where both kinds run out. The mechanism the chapter gives for it is 犯之以事，勿告以言 — hurl them at the deed, do not tell them the plan — and 愚士卒之耳目, keep their eyes and ears ignorant. That is not courage. It is the controlled removal of another man's options without his consent. And the tell is an asymmetry the book states in two places at once: Chapter 7 insists 圍師必闕 — when you surround him, leave him a gap — while this chapter says 圍地吾將**塞其闕**, *when I am the one hemmed in I will seal my own gap*, and 死地吾將**示之以不活**, *on death ground I will show them they are not going to live*. **You close your own exits, and you always leave his open.** No reading of this chapter that survives that pair is a motivational reading.

*Where A was taken.* **Julu, winter 208–207 BCE.** Xiang Yu sank the boats, broke the pots and steamers, burned the shelters, issued three days' rations, cut Zhang Han's 甬道 and held it cut, took Wang Li alive, and ended with the feudal lords who had spent the battle watching from their ramparts submitting to him as 諸侯上將軍 (*Shiji* 7). Note the limit of the winnings: the lords **at Julu**. Zhang Han's field army was intact and surrendered separately, later.

*Where B was taken, and worked.* **Jingxing, 205/204 BCE.** Han Xin drew his line up with the Mianman River (綿蔓水) at its back — his own officers thought it a textbook blunder — precisely so that it could not break; and at the same time he held back two thousand light horse, who took the emptied Zhao camp and replaced its banners with Han red. The line that could not retreat and the force kept in hand were **one plan**, which is exactly what Option B is. Asked afterwards, he **paraphrased** this chapter rather than quoting it: *Shiji* 92 gives him 陷之死地而後生，置之亡地而後存 — the two clauses inverted from the received order, 置 for 投, 而後 for 然後 — and he cites alongside it a line, 右倍山陵，前左水澤, that is not in the received Chapter 10 or 11 at all. Caption it as a loose citation from memory, not as a verbatim quotation. He then added the part everyone forgets: he was commanding levies he had not trained, men he described as pulled off the marketplace. Death ground was not his preference. It was the only way he could make strangers hold a line.

*Where a withdrawal was ordered and could not be un-ordered.* **The Fei River, 383 CE** — and this belongs here as the failure mode of B, not as B itself. Fu Jian was **not** a commander prudently keeping a line of retreat; his plan was the opposite manoeuvre, 半渡而擊: agree to Jin's request that Qin pull back from the bank, let the Jin army get half across, and take that half with cavalry while it was in the water. The withdrawal began, and then it could not be stopped. Zhu Xu (朱序) — a Jin officer serving in the Qin army under duress, not an anonymous panicking rear-ranker — shouted from behind that Qin had lost, and the rear ranks, who had been ordered to move backwards and told nothing else, believed him. The retreat became a rout in Qin's own rear areas, and the campaign and eventually the state came apart (*Jinshu*; *Zizhi Tongjian*, Jin annals, under 383). **A movement your men cannot be told the reason for is a movement they will interpret.** 犯之以事，勿告以言 has a price, and the price is that an army kept ignorant supplies its own explanation at the worst available moment.

*The Historian's closing note, at M ≈ 0.* *Shiji* says Xiang Yu was twenty-four when he first rose, in 209 BCE; Julu is two years on, and every stated age for him at Julu is arithmetic laid on that one sentence. In 202 BCE, at the Wu River (烏江, *Wūjiāng* — not 吳, *Wú*), a ferryman offered him the crossing. He refused it, gave his horse away, fought on foot until he had taken more than ten wounds, and cut his own throat (*Shiji* 7). Whatever death ground is, it is not a method that keeps paying. It is a place, and this chapter is mostly a list of eight other ones.

## G. CODEX MODE ENTRY — 11 九地

*(16:9. Orthographic. Ink frozen. Vermilion disarmed in Codex — a seal shown flat on the desk is a record, not a commitment.)*

**DICTUM.** Courage is a property of ground, not of men: remove every road but the one through the enemy and ordinary soldiers will produce a ferocity no order could have obtained.

- **A mixed taxonomy on a single argument.** Four of the nine are depth bands: 散地 dispersive (your own soil — *do not fight*) · 輕地 light (just inside his — *do not halt*) · 重地 serious (deep, his cities behind you — *forage where you stand*) · 死地 death (*fight*). Five are terrain quality and access: 爭地 contested (good for whoever holds it — *do not assault it*) · 交地 crossing (both can move freely — *do not let your columns be cut*) · 衢地 junction of three states (*make your alliances here*) · 圮地 bad going, mountain, forest, marsh (*keep moving through*) · 圍地 hemmed-in (*scheme your way out*). Four instructions are about **not being there**; five are about **operating while you are**. The axis the argument runs on is depth: 深入則專.
- **The mechanism is jeopardy, not loyalty.** Wu and Yue hated each other and rowed like one body in a storm. Nothing about the men changed; the boat did. It is also why the chapter is candid about deceiving one's own army — 愚士卒之耳目，使之無知 · 犯之以事，勿告以言 · 若驅群羊, driven out and back, none of them knowing where.
- **The asymmetry is the ethics, and the book states it twice.** Chapter 7: 圍師必闕 — leave *his* surrounded army a gap. This chapter, of itself: 圍地吾將塞其闕 — *hemmed in, I will seal my own* — and 死地吾將示之以不活. You close your exits; you leave his open. 死地 is ninth of nine because the other eight were instructions for being somewhere else. Burning the boats is not the strategy. It is what remains after the strategy.

**APPLY IT.** Do not admire the boats going down until you have audited how the army came to be standing on the far bank with three days of food. Ask who else was in the boats, and whether they were asked. Destroying your own line of retreat is not vision; it is the penalty clause on every calculation made before the crossing, and it is paid by people who were never shown the arithmetic.

## APPENDIX — PRODUCTION FLAGS

**Ratified against the manifesto.**
- Both chapters sit in **Act III (THE FRICTION)**, mean page density ceiling **0.42**. Ch. 11's death-ground node is the single largest ink expenditure in the act (≈ +0.09 on branch A, ≈ +0.08 on branch B, spread over twice the area) and must be budgeted against Chapters 7–10, not against the whole site.
- **No blue** anywhere in either chapter. Ch. 11's winter is cold by *value and grain*, never by hue: high-key flat light, pine-soot ink at its green-cool end of the ramp, and the warm reserved white of home receding. If a colourist reaches for a cool grade here, the shot is rejected.
- **Vermilion appears once per chapter, maximum.** Ch. 10: not at all in the terrain passage; permitted only on the branch of the node where Option A meets a prepared camp. Ch. 11: exactly one seal-press frame at the crossing. Accent coverage ≤ 3% of frame, full saturation ≤ 1%.
- **180mm is used exactly once across both chapters** — Chapter 11, across the Zhang, onto the Qin siege wall. **Chapter 10 has no enemy insert at all**, by design: withholding the enemy lens is how Chapter 10 argues that the enemy is not the variable. Do not restore it.
- **Camera moves are differentiated, not just focal lengths.** Ch. 10 is a rising crane that reads ground from above; Ch. 11 is an ankle-height dolly that refuses height for nine bands. Two chapters running the same 24mm lateral track would be one chapter shot twice.
- **Rout language is not boilerplate.** "Identity lost without darkening" is the Law's definition and belongs in the Law. Each chapter must describe its own failure in its own physical terms: Ch. 9 remixes at a ford; Ch. 10 dams against a terrace riser and spills into draws as striated feibai; Ch. 11 loses κ from the rear forward and comes apart along a bank. If two chapters describe a rout in the same sentence, one of them has not been written.
- **Distinctness check against neighbouring chapters.** Ch. 7 (軍爭) owns the enclosed dry defile at pre-dawn and grain-derived airborne dust — **neither is available to Ch. 10**. Ch. 9 (行軍) owns rain, riverbanks-as-camp and the reading of signs. Ch. 10 owns *terraced elevation* and gravity (G = terrain gradient) under raking directional light, with surface-bound chaff and nothing airborne. Ch. 11 owns *cohesion* (a centripetal κ(u) toward the cluster centroid) and substrate-step crystallisation under flat light. Ch. 12 owns embers and the only fire. If Ch. 10's ink ever runs inward, or Ch. 11's ever samples a height field, the shaders have been crossed.

**Vignette non-collision.** Ch. 10 uses **Yao / 殽, winter 628/627 – summer 627 BCE** (*Zuozhuan*, Xi 32–33) — not on the reserved anchor list and not claimed by any other chapter. Ch. 11 uses **Julu, winter 208–207 BCE** (*Shiji* 7) in preference to the obvious Jingxing anchor, because Julu is the fullest surviving account of a commander destroying his own line of retreat in front of his own men — **not** because it "enacts" a *Sunzi* line. It does not: the *Sunzi* text has boats burned, the *Shiji* has boats sunk and 釜甑 broken, the four-character wording of each is unstable or later, and the direction of influence between the passage and the story is not established. **Jingxing is retained and promoted** to Option B's historical verdict, where Han Xin's own loose citation of this chapter does more work than a second narrative would.

**Open textual questions for the Historian's apparatus.**
1. 唯**人**是保 vs 唯**民**是保 (Ch. 10) — Tang naming taboo. Decide house style, footnote the other, and **gloss the object as "the people," not "the men."**
2. 計險**阨** / 計險**隘** / 計險**易**遠近 (Ch. 10) — editorial variants; the first two are near-synonymous, the third parallels Ch. 1's 險易. Name the edition on the plate. (陋 has been struck: no witness was ever cited for it.)
3. 非**天**之災 vs 非**天地**之災 (Ch. 10) — a real split between the commentarial and 武經七書 lines. Print one, caption both. Same treatment for 故兵有走者 / 凡兵有走者 and 利合於主 / 利於主.
4. 勝乃可全 vs 勝乃不窮 (Ch. 10 close). Also: state on screen that Ch. 10 **rewrites** Ch. 3's 百戰不殆 as 勝乃不殆 before extending it. It is not the same sentence with two clauses added.
5. **Chapter 10 has no Yinqueshan witness at all.** The slips' 木牘 lists a 地形 title; no 地形 slip text survives. Never let the HUD, the Historian or a caption imply the slips corroborate this chapter's wording. Chapter 11 *is* on the slips and diverges from the received text; quote Ch. 11 as received-recension wording and say so.
6. 焚舟破釜 (Ch. 11) must be captioned as received-text wording with the edition named, never as universal or as certainly pre-Qin. 破釜沉舟 must never be captioned as a *Sunzi* line: the *Shiji* has 皆沉船，破釜甑, and the idiom is later than both. Same apparatus for 同舟**而**濟 (text) vs 同舟**共**濟 (later idiom), and for 從壁上觀 (*Shiji*) vs 作壁上觀 (later idiom).
7. Julu's precise month is not fixed in the *Benji* narrative; Xu Guang's 集解 dates the **advance crossing** to the eleventh month of Qin Er Shi year 3. On-screen date should read **"winter, 208–207 BCE"**, with the commentary cited as commentary if used. Which **Anyang** is meant is disputed and must not be pinned to a map silently. 九戰 has a punctuation variant (九戰，絕其甬道 / 九絕其甬道) and a divergent parallel (數絕甬道, Zhang Er–Chen Yu).
8. Jingxing is dated 205 BCE in some reckonings and 204 BCE in others. If it appears on screen, print both or print the regnal year.
9. Xiang Yu's age is derived, not attested: *Shiji* gives 初起時年二十四 (209 BCE). Any figure for Julu must be labelled as reckoning.
