# CHAPTER 1 — 計

## A. IDENTITY

| field | value |
|---|---|

| Alternate title | **始計** — the popular 武經七書 title. Usable on the site, but must be labelled 武經七書, never attributed to 十一家注. Cao Cao's commentary and the Yinqueshan title-tablet both give 計. |

*Why the alternative is sharper:* "Laying Plans" implies a room of men imagining futures. 計 is not imagination — it is arithmetic. 廟算, the operation this chapter is named for, produces a tally with a result that can come out short. Giles's title makes the chapter sound like a strategy offsite. It is a balance sheet.

The Historian may note *once*, and only in Codex apparatus, that **Xu Shen's *Shuowen* analyses 計 as 从言从十** — speech beside ten. That is Xu Shen's 會意 reading of the graph, not paleography and not the chapter's argument. Do not say "the argument is legible in the graph."

## B. THE CORE STRATEGIC DICTUM

> **The battle does not decide the war; it audits a count you already made, and it charges for the errors.**

Sun Tzu opens by refusing the reader's appetite. Before any manoeuvre he names five 事 — 道 (the alignment of the people's will with the ruler's), 天 (season, weather, time), 地 (distance, ground, danger, life and death of terrain), 將 (the commander's five qualities), 法 (organisation, chain of command, supply administration) — then converts them into seven comparative questions asked of *both* sides, and says the outcome is knowable from the answers. The chapter closes on 廟算: many counts win, few counts lose, and no count at all does not merit discussion.

**Where the popular reading is wrong.** Three ways.

*First,* 兵者，詭道也 is neither the chapter's thesis nor a footnote, and this packet previously got it wrong in the second direction. It is a **definitional 也-judgement**, grammatically parallel to 勢者，因利而制權也 in the same chapter, and Cao Cao glosses it 兵無常形，以詭詐為道 — war has no fixed form; it takes deceit as its way. It is therefore a **second principle of the chapter**, standing beside the count and illustrated by the clauses that follow it. What Giles gets wrong is scope, not rank: "All warfare is *based on* deception" makes 道 a foundation, when 道 here is *way, method* — the manner in which arms are conducted, not the ground the count rests on. Deception corrupts the enemy's arithmetic; it does not substitute for yours. A liar with a bad count is still a liar.

*Second,* 道 is not "moral law" or virtue; Giles's translation has misled a century of readers. The text defines it on the spot: 道者，令民與上同意也 — *the Way is what makes the people share the ruler's intent.* That is a measurable political fact, closer to legitimacy or cohesion than to righteousness, and the anti-Giles point stands on that clause alone. The continuation is a **crux and must be flagged, not used**: the received text reads 故可以與之死，可以與之生，而不畏危(也), while Yinqueshan is reported to read 民弗詭也 ("the people will not go against him"). Commentators already suspected the 危/詭 relation. Do not build the site's psychology of 道 on "they will die with him without fear" — that clause is not textually settled.

*Third,* this is not "planning." 廟算 produces a number. The chapter's mood is not visionary; it is actuarial, and its threat is not defeat but *innumeracy*.

## C. THREE KEY LINES

**1. The opening sentence of the book.**

- **Chinese (copy-text):** 兵者，國之大事，死生之地，存亡之道，不可不察也。
- **Pinyin:** Bīng zhě, guó zhī dà shì, sǐ shēng zhī dì, cún wáng zhī dào, bù kě bù chá yě.
- **Literal:** "War: the great affair of the state; the ground of death and life; the road of survival and extinction. It cannot but be examined."
- **Modern:** War is the state's largest undertaking. It is the terrain on which people live or die and the road on which a country continues or ends. You are not permitted to look away from it.
- **Confidence:** High on the copy-text. Some editions open 孫子曰：兵者… . **Variant to display, not to suppress:** Yinqueshan is reported to read 兵者，國之大事**也**，死生之地… . The *sense* of this sentence is the most stable in the book; the *wording* is not identical across witnesses, and the site must not conflate the two claims.

**2. The second principle.**

- **Chinese:** 兵者，詭道也。
- **Pinyin:** Bīng zhě, guǐ dào yě.
- **Literal:** "War is a way of deceiving."
- **Modern:** Warfare is a practice of misrepresentation.
- **The received expansion, inseparable from it:** twelve clauses — 能而示之不能，用而示之不用，近而示之遠，遠而示之近，利而誘之，亂而取之，實而備之，強而避之，怒而撓之，卑而驕之，佚而勞之，親而離之 — closed by a maxim, 攻其無備，出其不意。
- **Confidence:** High on characters in the copy-text. **Three cautions.** (a) These twelve are *not* a typology of feints: 能而示之不能 is a feint, but 實而備之 (if he is solid, prepare against him) and 強而避之 (if he is strong, avoid him) are ordinary prudence, and 攻其無備，出其不意 is a closing maxim, not an item. Call this "the received expansion of 詭道," never "fourteen feints." (b) Yinqueshan's list is shorter, and the three clauses 卑而驕之，佚而勞之，親而離之 are **absent** from the slips as transcribed — which matters, because those are exactly the clauses the site was previously using as its Chengpu verdict. (c) Cao Cao's gloss 兵無常形，以詭詐為道 is the oldest reading and is why this line is a principle, not an appendix.

**3. The chapter's actual conclusion.**

- **Chinese:** 夫未戰而廟算勝者，得算多也；未戰而廟算不勝者，得算少也。多算勝，少算不勝，而況於無算乎！
- **Pinyin:** Fú wèi zhàn ér miào suàn shèng zhě, dé suàn duō yě; wèi zhàn ér miào suàn bù shèng zhě, dé suàn shǎo yě. Duō suàn shèng, shǎo suàn bù shèng, ér kuàng yú wú suàn hū!
- **Literal:** "He who, before fighting, reckons in the temple and wins, has obtained many tallies. He who reckons and does not win has obtained few. Many tallies, victory; few tallies, no victory — and how much more so with none at all!"
- **Modern:** The tally is taken before the war, and the war confirms it. A wide margin wins, a thin margin loses, and a commander who never ran the numbers does not need a battle to tell him what he is.
- **Confidence:** High on sense. **Graph flag:** the traditional-character build should prefer **筭** — the rod graph, written in Yinqueshan and in the Song 十一家注 — over the later standard 算; whichever is chosen must be fixed once and used consistently. The pun on *reckoning* and *counting-rod* is carried by the graph and should survive to screen. Editions vary between 而況於無算乎 and 而況無算乎 — negligible. **The temple rod-count as a staged ritual with rods laid on a mat is commentarial reconstruction, not a stage direction in the Sunzi**; it may be depicted, but the Historian must not narrate it as reportage.

**Supporting canon for the HUD ledger (not display lines):**
五事 — 一曰道，二曰天，三曰地，四曰將，五曰法。 *(Yinqueshan is reported to read 經之以五, without 事 — flag, medium.)*
七計 — 主孰有道？將孰有能？天地孰得？法令孰行？兵眾孰強？士卒孰練？賞罰孰明？吾以此知勝負矣。 *(These are seven rhetorical 孰 questions; the ordering above is the copy-text's. Yinqueshan is reported to differ in the first question — 主孰德 for 主孰有道 — and to carry an additional clause 順逆，兵勝也 in this stretch. Flag both, medium; verify against facsimile before display.)*
道者，令民與上同意也。 *(High. This is the clause the site defines 道 from.)*

### NINETY LI OF GIVEN GROUND
**Chengpu, in the fields of Wey. Fourth month, 632 BC.**
*Duke Wen of Jin (Chóng'ěr) · Xiān Zhěn 先軫 · Hú Yǎn 狐偃, styled Zǐfàn 子犯 · Xū Chén 胥臣 · Luán Zhī 欒枝 · Chéng Déchén 成得臣, styled Zǐyù 子玉, of Chu · King Cheng of Chu*
*(Personal names in pinyin; 字 courtesy names marked as such on first use and used plainly thereafter.)*

Seven hundred chariots stand in column and none of them is moving forward.

The *Zuozhuan* gives the muster as a formula and not as a scene: 晉車七百乘，韅、靷、鞅、靽 — seven hundred Jin chariots, breast-strap, trace, belly-band, crupper. Four words for four pieces of leather, which is how a chronicle says *nothing on this field is unaccounted for*. Then the Duke climbs the ruins of the old Shen settlement to look at his army, and says the one thing he says all morning: *young and old, they observe the forms; they can be used.*

He gives the order to withdraw.

Ninety li. Three days' marches — three 舍 — back from ground his officers have already surveyed and liked. They protest, because the shame is arithmetic too: our ruler is retreating from a subject, from Ziyu, a Chu minister, and the men can see it. Zi Fan answers for the Duke, and the answer is not about honour. *An army in the right is strong; an army in the wrong is old.* Once, in exile, this Duke sat at King Cheng's table in Chu and was asked what he would give in return. He said: if our armies ever meet, I will withdraw three stages. He is not paying a debt. He is buying the one factor he cannot manufacture — 道, the thing that lets a man send farmers at a wall of bronze and have them go.

And the ninety li is a lever with two ends. Every li Jin gives up, Chu takes; and Chu is taking it away from its own grain, away from its king, who has already sent word from Shen that this campaign is finished — that the Jin lord has been abroad **nineteen years**, that heaven has given him the years and cleared away what harmed him, and that what heaven has set in place is not a thing a minister overturns. Ziyu reads the message and asks to fight anyway. The king, angry, **withholds the main royal army and sends him a reinforcement instead**: 西廣, a royal 廣 unit; 東宮, the heir's troops; and the six 卒 of the Ruo'ao clan. It is not the Chu order of battle — Ziyu will still take the field in three armies, with Chen and Cai levies on his right — but it is a general advancing ninety li past his king's word, in a temper, with the household's men rather than the state's.

Because that is the other half of the count, and it was made in a tent months ago. Xian Zhen had said: hold the ruler of Cao, cut up the lands of Cao and Wey, give them to Song; let Song take bribes to Qi and Qin and have *them* ask Chu to stand down. Chu will refuse — Chu loves Cao and Wey. *喜賂怒頑* — delight them with bribes, enrage them with obstinacy — *and can there fail to be a battle?* The count had two states in it that had not yet chosen, and Xian Zhen made their choice a purchasable item. This morning the *Chunqiu* entry for the battle reads 晉侯、齊師、宋師、秦師及楚人戰于城濮: the Jin marquis, **the armies of Qi, Song and Qin**, against the men of Chu. Song is a principal, not an extra; the *Zuozhuan* names 宋公, 齊國歸父 and 崔夭, and 秦小子憖 in the field on the 戊辰 day.

On the day, the Jin army forms up north of Shen (莘北 — the old 有莘 settlement, Shēn; not 新). Chu camps with its back to a mound.

Then the lying begins, and it is very cheap. Xu Chen, on the Jin lower wing, has his chariot horses dressed in tiger skins and drives them at the Chen and Cai contingents holding the Chu right — allied levies, the least committed troops on the field, chosen deliberately. Horses that have never smelled tiger break; Chen and Cai break; the Chu right dissolves before the main lines touch. On the other wing Hu Mao raises two great pennants and pulls them back, the sign of a commander's standard leaving the field, while Luan Zhi's carts drag cut brushwood through the dust and run. It is a rout made out of dry sticks and one afternoon's wind. The Chu left goes after it at the gallop, stretches, and Xian Zhen and Xi Zhen take it in the flank with the ducal companies of the centre while the Hu brothers close the other jaw.

Both Chu wings are gone. Ziyu holds his centre together and pulls it off the field intact, which is the only reason the word for it is defeat and not annihilation.

For three days the Jin army sits in Chu's camps and eats Chu's stores, and the smoke off the cooking pits is the last thing in frame.

**Record vs legend.** The *Zuozhuan* (Lu, Duke Xi, years 23–28) records: the promise of three stages made in exile; the withdrawal of ninety li and the officers' protest; Xian Zhen's plan to buy Qi and Qin with Cao and Wey's land; King Cheng's order to disengage, his *nineteen years* remark, and his angry despatch of 西廣、東宮 and the Ruo'ao six 卒 only; the seven hundred chariots and the harness formula; the Duke reviewing the army from the ruins of Shen; the tiger skins; the two pennants and the dragged brushwood; the flank attack; the Jin army eating Chu's grain for three days; and, afterwards, Ziyu's death at Lian'gu when he is asked what he will say to the old men of Shen and Xi whose sons he spent — on which news Duke Wen says, in five characters, that now nobody can poison him. Duke Wen is hegemon before the year is out.

What is literary shaping is the *dialogue*. The *Zuozhuan* was compiled generations later and gives every actor a finished speech, including Duke Wen's dream the night before of King Cheng pinning him down and sucking out his brains, which Zi Fan reads as auspicious. Treat the speeches as the tradition's argument about the battle, not as transcript. The site of Chengpu itself is disputed between candidate locations in modern Shandong and Henan. Note also that the *Zuozhuan* is a **battle narrative** — tiger skins, pennants, dragged brushwood, the ducal companies striking across — and the site's line that "nobody out-fought anybody" is the site's thesis about the battle, not the chronicle's account of it; if it is used, it must be marked as ours.

## E. VISUAL CUE

**Environment — THE COUNTING HALL, AND THE SHEET IT SITS ON.**
An ancestral hall before dawn: rammed-earth floor, bronze standing lamps, a low mat, and on it a sheet of paper the size of a door with nothing on it. This is the whitest the site will ever be (D ≈ 0.020). The world outside — armies, a river, a season — exists only as pale marks the Historian is *about to* make. The hall is not a place; it is the sheet seen from close enough to be architecture.
*Material-culture note, art direction only:* the substrate is rendered as xuan, which is the site's paper throughout and is **anachronistic for 632 BC and for the Warring States**. This is a declared convention of the project, like the letterbox; it is never presented as period reconstruction, and the Historian never says the word.

**Dominant ink behaviour — 淡墨 ONLY, AND NOTHING DRIES.**
Every mark in Chapter 1 is dilute, high-moisture, still moving. Nothing in this chapter is committed, so nothing has a tide line until the node. M sits at 0.60–0.70 across the whole frame — static humidity, quiescent, no flow field: the fog of war rendered as the paper simply being *damp*, everywhere, all chapter. (Chapter 6 owns driven moisture and flow; Chapter 1 owns still damp. The distinction is stated in `canon-04-06.md` and must hold.) The reader's first lesson in the Ink Law is that they can watch a mark still deciding what it will be.

**Particle system — PAPER FIBRE, NOT SMOKE.**
No airborne ink (banned). What moves in the light is paper lint: short cellulose fibres lifted off the sheet's tooth by lamp convection, drifting on the fibre-direction vector field θ so the *air itself previews the anisotropy* before any ink demonstrates it. Sheet-bound, low, never rising above lamp-collar height; no glitter, no god-rays. Second emitter: counting rods, 籌/筭, being set down — no particles, but each placement displaces three or four fibres. Both run at the standard non-Chapter-12 particle scale and rendering path defined in `direction-tech.md` (particle scale 1.0 / 0.5 / 0.22 by tier); this packet does not set counts.

**Lighting — TWO LOW WICKS, CROSSING.**
Two guttering oil lamps set low along the mat's edge, spaced, so their grazing shadows **cross** the paper tooth rather than raking it in one direction. The tooth still reads as landscape, but as a woven landscape with two shadow directions and soft, doubled penumbrae that breathe with the wicks. This is deliberately *not* the single hard raking source: Chapter 5 owns the raked lighting model, Chapter 4 owns shadowless, Chapter 6 owns value inversion. Chapter 1 owns interior, warm, doubled and unsteady. Volumetric only in the 30cm above the mat. Colour temperature warm but strictly within the ink/paper ramp — no gold on screen until the seventh comparison is answered, and then one hairline.

**Camera — 85mm HELD, 40mm FOR THE LEDGER, ONE 180mm.**
Opens at 85mm on the named factor: five slow lateral tracks, one per 事, each ending on a single character being written in stroke order. Cuts to 40mm only when the Historian handles the rods. The chapter's single 180mm shot is its last: Chu's camp fires compressed across a valley, unreachably far, the first appearance of the lens that will never be shared with the reader's own force. Letterbox 2.39:1 throughout, tightening to 2.76:1 at the node.

**THE ONE IMAGE THEY REMEMBER — THE DIFFUSION RACE.**
The seven comparisons are written as fourteen pale strokes in two vertical columns, right-to-left: Jin's answers and Chu's, paired. They are drawn identically, at identical density, in identical ink. Then nothing is announced. The strokes simply begin to wick, and one column travels visibly faster along the fibre than the other — because the paper under it is wetter, because the ground was better prepared.

*Constraints, so this does not become an ink blot.* The race is driven by a **non-uniform background moisture buffer**, not by a difference in the strokes: Jin's column sits on high-M ground, Chu's on markedly drier ground, and the two columns' own D and load are identical. Bleed is capped: total isocontour displacement is held to a small fraction of the character's stroke width so that **both columns remain fully legible at the end of the shot** — the winner reaches the margin, it does not dissolve. Moisture decays fast enough that the leading column pins a crisp tide line at the margin rather than continuing to spread. **The answer to "who wins" arrives as a diffusion rate, not a number**, and the reader has to watch for four seconds to see it. No score, no highlight, no jade. Just one column reaching the margin first.

## F. TACTIC DECISION NODE — "THE NINETY LI"

**Situation (Commander, 行草 on the operative term):**
> Ziyu is coming and he is angry. In exile, at another man's table, you promised this ground away; your officers can count the li, and they can count what it costs to be seen retreating from a subject. Give it, or hold it.

**OPTION A — WITHDRAW 退避三舍. Yield ninety li.**
Fall back three marches. Concede the surveyed ground, keep the count.

*Simulated visual consequence:* The camera cranes back on rails. **Your mark does not retract — D is monotonic and nothing lifts.** What you lay is a *new* stroke, thin and 淡墨, curling back along the outside of your own advance and depositing three small camp blotches where the marches end. The forward recon stroke stays exactly where it was, permanently, as a pale dry hairline you have abandoned — the reader can see the ground they gave, because the ink for it is still on the page. As the crane completes, moisture at the three camps crosses zero and **three tide lines crystallise: bright, hard-edged, permanent** (manifesto §3.6), the first 水痕 the reader ever sees, and they are marks of ground *given*, not taken. Chu's mark, following, lengthens ninety li and thins with distance — you have traded your ink for his. At the frame edge the three allied strokes (Qi, Song, Qin) go from 淡墨 to **gold**: unratified intention becomes order. *Render note:* gold is leaf, not ink. It is composited in the post pass as an un-bloomed overlay on the stroke geometry, and its contribution to the ink field's D accumulator is zero — the strokes do not darken, they change material. Gold that blooms is a bug (manifesto §4). **Ink spent: +0.008. Page density after: 0.028.**

**OPTION B — HOLD. Fight on the ground you surveyed.**

*Simulated visual consequence:* Hard cut — you caused it — to 濃墨 at the ridge line. The largest single deposit in Act I, and it is all yours. The 180mm shot does not change: the enemy does not move, because he does not have to. The three allied strokes stay **pale**, never ratified, still 淡墨 at the margin where you left them; they are on the sheet but they are intention, and intention does not hold a flank. **HISTORICAL VERDICT (revealed at M ≈ 0, never before):**

*What the record says.* *Zuozhuan*, Duke Xi 28: Duke Wen of Jin withdrew three 舍 — ninety li — from ground his officers had surveyed, and won. The chronicle gives the Jin reasons in its own vocabulary, and they are not Sun Tzu's: 報施, repaying what was given; 信, keeping faith with a promise made in exile; 師直為壯，曲為老, an army in the right is strong and one in the wrong is old; and 喜賂怒頑, Xian Zhen's plan to buy Qi and Qin by delighting them with bribes and enraging them with Chu's obstinacy. **Cheng Dechen — Ziyu — demanded the battle** against King Cheng's instruction, was reinforced only with 西廣, 東宮 and the Ruo'ao six 卒, advanced anyway, lost both wings to a tiger skin and a bundle of brushwood, extracted his centre, and died at Lian'gu.

*What the site may claim, and must mark as its claim.* Chengpu is 632 BC. The received *Sunzi* is centuries later, and the three clauses that read most like a gloss on the ninety li — 卑而驕之，佚而勞之，親而離之 — are **received-text only and absent from the Yinqueshan slips**. Mapping this battle onto Chapter 1's exam question is *our* pedagogy; it is not what the chronicle says happened, and the Historian says so in one line. Note also that Ziyu's actual choice was to *pursue* into given ground, not to hold surveyed ground — he is not the site's Option B, and the packet must not label him as one. What survives without argument is the shape: one commander spent pride to buy position and allies, the other spent position and allies to keep pride, and the *Zuozhuan* gives Duke Wen's reaction to the news of Ziyu's death in five characters that are not noble.

## G. CODEX MODE ENTRY — 計 · THE COUNT

**DICTUM.** The battle does not decide the war; it audits a count you already made, and it charges for the errors.

- **Five fields, then seven questions, then a number.** 道 天 地 將 法 — cohesion, season, ground, commander, administration — asked of *both* sides. The chapter's output is a margin, not a plan.
- **道 is not virtue.** The text defines it in one clause: 令民與上同意也 — the people share the ruler's intent. Cohesion is a quantity. Measure it or lose it. (What follows that clause is a textual crux; the site flags it and does not lean on it.)
- **詭道 is a second principle, not an appendix.** War takes deceit as its way, and the twelve received clauses after it show how — Cao Cao: 兵無常形，以詭詐為道. But deception spoils the enemy's arithmetic; it does not repair yours. The chapter holds both, and a reader who takes only this one has a method and no number.

**APPLY IT.** The temple tally is not a forecast of victory; it is an audit of deficits, and it is taken *before* the army is raised because afterwards there is nothing to be done with the answer. Name the quantity on which everything else in the enterprise rests — the one that, being short, makes every other provision irrelevant. If you cannot name it and say how short it may run before the thing fails, you have not counted. You have marched.

# CHAPTER 2 — 作戰

## A. IDENTITY

| field | value |
|---|---|
| Number | 2 of 13 |
| Chinese title | **作戰** |
| Pinyin | **Zuò Zhàn** |
| Literal | "doing battle" / "putting the war into operation" — the mobilisation phase |
| Standard English | **Waging War** (Giles; and Griffith 1963 uses the same title). Also *On Waging Battle* (Ames 1993), *Waging War* (Sawyer 1993). |
| Sharper alternative | **THE PRICE OF A DAY** |

*Why the alternative is sharper:* "Waging War" promises campaigns and gets a reader ready for movement. There is almost no movement in this chapter. It is about money, grain, oxen, glue, lacquer, chariot axles, and the fact that all of them are consumed per day whether or not anything happens. The chapter's unit is not the battle. It is the **day**.

## B. THE CORE STRATEGIC DICTUM

> **An army is a fire, and what it burns is not the enemy — it is your own country, at a fixed rate, every day it stays lit.**

Sun Tzu itemises before he argues: a thousand fast chariot-teams, a thousand heavy waggons, a hundred thousand men in armour, provisions carried a thousand li, and the costs at home and at the front — envoys, glue and lacquer, the upkeep of vehicles and armour — a thousand in gold a day, *and only then* can a hundred-thousand-man army take the field. Everything after that is consequence: blunted weapons, spent keenness, a treasury that runs dry, and neighbouring rulers who arrive to profit from the exhaustion. The chapter's remedies are two, and both are ugly: end it fast, and eat the enemy's food.

**Where the popular reading is wrong.** It is quoted as "speed wins wars." Sun Tzu never says a quick war is a *good* war. He says 兵聞拙速，未睹巧之久也 — one has heard of clumsy speed, but never seen skilled protraction. 拙 means *crude, unskilful*. He is conceding that a botched fast war beats an elegant slow one, which is a fiscal judgement, not a tactical preference.

**And here the chapter is categorical, and the site does not get to soften it.** 夫兵久而國利者，未之有也 — there has never been a case of a protracted war benefiting a state. 故兵貴勝，不貴久 — what is valued in war is winning, not lasting. There is no clause anywhere in Chapter 2 licensing a long war on the grounds that it has been paid for. Any reading that arrives at "if you can afford time, time is not the enemy" has repealed the chapter to save an anecdote. **Chapter 2 forbids protraction. Full stop.** The vignette below is staged as a *tension with the chapter*, never as its proof.

Second, 因糧於敵, feeding off the enemy, is not thrift. The text sets the exchange at twenty to one, and the difference is made up by peasants, on both sides, who are not consulted. Third, the chapter ends by calling the general who understands this 民之司命, the arbiter of the people's fate. That is not a compliment about heroism. It is a claim about who is spending whose harvest.

## C. THREE KEY LINES

**1. The itemised bill.**

- **Chinese:** 凡用兵之法，馳車千駟，革車千乘，帶甲十萬，千里饋糧，則內外之費，賓客之用，膠漆之材，車甲之奉，日費千金，然後十萬之師舉矣。

- **Literal:** "In the use of arms: a thousand teams of fast chariots, a thousand leather-covered waggons, a hundred thousand men in armour, grain carried a thousand li; then the expenses within and without, the cost of envoys, materials of glue and lacquer, the upkeep of chariots and armour — a thousand gold a day. Only then may a host of a hundred thousand be raised."
- **Modern:** Field a hundred thousand men and you have already bought a thousand chariot-teams, a thousand waggons, a supply road a thousand li long, and a daily bill of a thousand in gold for diplomacy, adhesives and maintenance. That is the entry fee. It is charged before anyone has fought.
- **Confidence:** High on the copy-text. Note: 駟 is a four-horse team, so 千駟 is a thousand *teams*, not a thousand vehicles — most English versions blur this. **On 金:** 千金 is a Warring States monetary denomination reckoned as a weight of metal. It is money, and the site should not pretend otherwise; what the site refuses is *conversion* — never give it in yuan, dollars, or a modern equivalent. "A monetary weight of metal" is the formula.

**2. The concession that is misread as doctrine.**

- **Chinese:** 故兵聞拙速，未睹巧之久也。夫兵久而國利者，未之有也。
- **Pinyin:** Gù bīng wén zhuō sù, wèi dǔ qiǎo zhī jiǔ yě. Fú bīng jiǔ ér guó lì zhě, wèi zhī yǒu yě.
- **Literal:** "So in war one has heard of crude haste; one has never seen skill in duration. There has never been a case of a protracted war benefiting a state."
- **Modern:** Clumsy and quick is a thing that happens. Clever and slow is not. No country has ever got richer from a long war.
- **Confidence:** High. 拙速 is the load-bearing pair and must not be softened to "swift victory" — the word is *unskilful*. The second sentence is the chapter's categorical prohibition and must never be quoted without it.

**3. The exchange rate.**

- **Chinese (copy-text graph):** 故智將務食於敵，食敵一鍾，當吾二十鍾；𦮼稈一石，當吾二十石。
- **Pinyin:** Gù zhì jiàng wù shí yú dí, shí dí yī zhōng, dāng wú èr shí zhōng; [jì/qí] gǎn yī dàn, dāng wú èr shí dàn.
- **Literal:** "So the wise general takes pains to feed off the enemy. One zhong of the enemy's grain is worth twenty of ours; one dan of his fodder-stalks, twenty of ours."
- **Modern:** A competent general eats the enemy's harvest. One measure taken from him is worth twenty shipped from home.
- **Confidence:** High on sense and on 食敵一鍾，當吾二十鍾. **Medium on the fodder graph and its reading.** The 十一家注 copy-text writes **𦮼**, and Cao Cao supplies a sound-gloss, 𦮼音忌 (*jì*), identifying the plant with 萁, bean-stalk. Later printings therefore display 萁稈 (*qí gǎn*) or, treating the gloss as a lemma, 忌稈. These are **one graph plus its commentary**, not three independent edition-readings with a single agreed pronunciation; do not print "all read *qí gǎn*." Display 𦮼 (or 萁 in a fallback build) and carry Cao Cao's 音忌 in the apparatus. **Do not render this glyph at display size until the build's font coverage is fixed.**
  **On measures:** the text has **石**. Its reading here is *dàn*, and 擔 is a later way of writing that reading — say so in the apparatus; never print 擔 as if it were Sunzi's graph. 鍾 (zhōng) and 石 (dàn) are dry measures of the period; give them as measures, never as litres or kilos.
  **On the twenty-to-one figure:** 一鍾當吾二十鍾 is the text. The familiar sermon that *nineteen of every twenty measures are eaten by the porters and draught animals on the road* is **later logistics commentary**, not Sunzi's stated reason — and Han-period examples run far worse than 20:1. Keep the ratio; mark the explanation as commentary when it is used.

## D. THE VIGNETTE

> **Gating.** Codex apparatus and post-commit Story material. Not legible before the node resolves.
> **Framing, mandatory.** This episode is presented as a **counter-case in tension with Chapter 2**, and the Historian says so before it starts. Wang Jian's 堅壁 — sitting behind a rampart refusing battle — is the direct opposite of 拙速, and the chapter would not endorse it. What the episode *does* illustrate, cleanly, is the first half of the chapter: 日費千金，然後十萬之師舉矣 — the bill precedes the army — and the cost of a commander who quotes a discount to get a commission.

### SIXTY MYRIADS
**The court of Qin at Xianyang; then the orchard country of Pinyang; then a stockade on the Chu frontier. Conventionally 225–224 BC.**
*King Zheng of Qin · Li Xin · "Meng Tian" (see flag) · Wang Jian · Xiang Yan of Chu*

The king asks a simple question and gets two prices.

*How many men do you judge sufficient to take Chu?* Li Xin is young and decorated. With a few thousand men he ran the fleeing Crown Prince Dan of Yan down at the **Yan River (衍水** — the graph is 衍, not the state 燕; later the 太子河 in Liaoning**)**, and *Shiji* 73 says he broke them there and got Dan. He answers like a man quoting a job: not more than two hundred thousand. Then the old one. Wang Jian has taken Zhao. He has taken Yan's capital. He says: without six hundred thousand it cannot be done.

Six hundred thousand is not a number, it is a sentence about the state. Wang Jian will later put it in one phrase — 空秦國甲士, *empty Qin of its armoured men* — and that phrase is **his**, spoken to justify himself, not the *Shiji*'s narration. It means the granaries of the Wei valley opened at once, the ox-carts of four conquered kingdoms turned south, the conscription of a generation, and a war that cannot be quietly abandoned if it goes badly, because there is nothing left at home to abandon it *to*. The king hears an old man being frightened. *General Wang has grown old — how timid he has become.* Li Xin is bold, and his figure is a third of the other. The commission goes to Li Xin. Wang Jian pleads illness and goes home to his orchards at Pinyang.

The cheap war goes well, and the *Shiji* gives it no duration at all: Li Xin breaks the Chu forces at Pingyu, his colleague breaks them at **Qǐn (寢** — a Chu county near modern Shenqiu, Henan; **not** Qin the state**)**, Li Xin then takes **鄢郢 (Yan-Ying, a major Chu political name whose identification is disputed)** and turns his army west to join him at Chengfu. And behind him the Chu army does the only thing the ledger did not price. It follows — 荊人因隨之, *the men of Chu accordingly followed them* — three days and three nights **without halting to make camp**. It is a pursuit that would destroy an army in any account of war concerned with courage, and is instead a wager that the Qin column's rear has outrun its own grain. It has. Chu comes into the Qin camps at the end of it, breaks into two of them, kills seven commanders of the rank of duwei, and the army of Qin runs.

The king rides to Pinyang himself. Whatever he says there, the *Shiji* renders as an apology: *I did not use the general's plan, and Li Xin has indeed disgraced the armies of Qin.* Wang Jian repeats his price without adjustment. Not one man fewer than six hundred thousand.

He gets them. The king sees him off in person at **Bashang**, and there, at the head of the entire armed strength of the state, Wang Jian begins asking for things: good fields, houses, gardens, fish ponds, for his sons. The king laughs at him. Then the column moves, and **once it has reached the pass** he sends **five separate parties of messengers** back down the road to ask for more. His own officers are embarrassed — *is the general not overdoing this?* Wang Jian's answer is the coldest sentence in the episode: the King of Qin is harsh and does not trust men; he has now emptied the kingdom of soldiers and handed them to me; if I do not ask for farms for my grandchildren, he will sit in his palace and wonder what I *do* want.

Then he crosses into Chu, fortifies, and **refuses battle for a long time — 久之, and the *Shiji* does not say how long.**

Chu, hearing that Wang Jian has come with everything, calls up everything. Two enormous armies are in the same country and one of them is behind a rampart with the gates shut. Chu offers battle. Qin does not come out. Chu offers battle again. Qin does not come out. Wang Jian washes his men, feeds them well, eats what they eat, and waits. Eventually he asks what the troops are doing with themselves and is told: throwing stones and jumping for distance. *Then they can be used.*

Nothing else in this story is a manoeuvre. Six hundred thousand men are a machine for converting grain into patience, and the patience is the weapon. Chu, unable to get the battle it needs and unable to feed a full mobilisation forever, breaks camp and moves east — and a marching army is a stroke that thins. Wang Jian follows and hits it, and at **Qí-south (蘄南** — 蘄 is a Chu county south of modern Suzhou in Anhui; **not** the state of 齊**)** the Chu general Xiang Yan is killed. **A little over a year later — 歲餘 — the King of Chu is a prisoner and the state is Qin's counties.** The 歲餘 is the interval between the victory and the capture. It is not the length of the sit-down, and nothing in *Shiji* 73 measures that.

**The honest reading.** Li Xin quoted a discount to win a commission and it cost Qin an army. Wang Jian quoted the true figure in front of the king, out loud, and refused to open lower. That is Chapter 2's first half, exactly. **What follows it is not.** Wang Jian then fought precisely the kind of war the chapter says has never benefited a state, and won — and the site's position is that this is a real counter-example, not a hidden endorsement. The chapter's rule holds as a rule; Wang Jian is what the rule looks like when a state large enough to break it does. The reader is told this and is not told it is fine.

**Record vs legend.** *Shiji* 73 (biographies of Bai Qi and Wang Jian) records: the two estimates and the king's remark about Wang Jian's age; the initial victories at Pingyu, Qǐn, Yan-Ying and Chengfu; the three-day-and-night Chu pursuit that broke into two camps and killed seven duwei; the king's journey to Pinyang; the send-off at Bashang, the requests for estates, the five parties of messengers sent back after reaching the pass, and Wang Jian's stated reason; 堅壁 and the refusal to fight for 久之; the stone-throwing and jumping; the eastward move; Xiang Yan's death at 蘄南; and 歲餘 to the capture of King Fuchu.

Flags. **(1) The colleague's name.** *Shiji* 73 gives 蒙恬, Meng Tian; Liang Yusheng and later critics argue this should be 蒙武, since Meng Tian does not appear as a field commander in the parallel tables at this date. Present the name with the doubt attached; do not cast Meng Tian as a fact. **(2) The Yan campaign.** *Shiji* 73 credits Li Xin with getting Prince Dan at the 衍水; the basic annals (ch. 6) instead have the King of Yan send Dan's head, and credit the taking of Ji and Dan's head to Wang Jian's Yan campaign. Do not stage a live capture-and-return. **(3) The number.** Warring States troop figures reach us through the states' own claims and later compilation; 600,000 as a single field army is regarded by most modern historians as inflated, perhaps greatly. The *ratio* Wang Jian insisted on — three times Li Xin's estimate — is the part that carries weight. **(4) Xiang Yan's end is contradicted inside the *Shiji* itself** and the site must not settle it: ch. 73 has him killed at 蘄南 and the Chu king captured 歲餘 afterwards; ch. 6 has the Chu king captured in the twenty-third year, Xiang Yan then enthrone the Lord of Changping, and Xiang Yan take his own life in the twenty-fourth. **(5) Dates.** 225 / 224 / 223 BC is a conventional harmonisation of these two accounts, not a *Shiji* statement. Give it as conventional.

## E. VISUAL CUE

**Environment — THE ROAD, AT 1:1, AS TWO RUTS.**
There is no battlefield in Chapter 2 and no camp. There is a **supply road**, and it is the entire chapter. The sheet is turned so its fibre runs with the road, and the camera sits directly over it. **The road is not one organic brushstroke** — Chapter 3 works on a single continuous stroke and the two must not read the same at thumbnail size. It is **two deeply scored parallel cart ruts**, laid dry and rough, at ox-cart gauge, with a rubbed unmarked crown between them; at intervals, **stamped rectangular depot marks** sit square across the pair, mechanical against the ruts' wander. A corridor of friction, not a piece of calligraphy. Scale is the punishment here, so the environment's only content is *length*.

**Dominant ink behaviour — 飛白, EARNED AT THE FRONT OF THE STROKE.**
This is the chapter where the reader learns that a brush has a finite reserve. **The rule, stated exactly, because the packet previously broke it:** 飛白 is generated *at the leading edge of deposition*, where drawing velocity outruns the brush's remaining load (manifesto §3.4). It is a property of ink being laid, not of ink that has been laid. **Nothing already on the page ever un-lays itself**; D is monotonic and a black texel cannot become paper. So the road is drawn *forward across the chapter*: laid at full 濃墨 at the home-valley end, and as the pan advances the newly-laid ruts break up into fibre-wide voids until the brush is running on tooth alone. The reader watches the mark run out of ink going *away* from home, which is also what happened.

The rear of the road ages by **M, not by D**. Behind the pan, moisture falls out of the laid ruts, bleed halts, edges harden, tide lines crystallise across the depot stamps, and the core darkens the 3–6% that drying costs. The supply line does not thin; it **sets** — it stops being able to move, stops being able to absorb anything, and goes permanent and brittle while the front of the stroke starves. Fraying forward, fossilising behind. Both are legal, both are physical, and neither erases a pixel.

**Particle system — CHAFF, AND THE GOLD THAT IS NOT A PARTICLE.**
Threshing chaff and road dust: sheet-bound, low and heavy, never rising above axle height, drifting along the road's own direction vector with alpha modulated by the paper grain so the flakes read as fibre rather than as sprites. No god-rays, no glitter, no airborne ink. Standard non-Chapter-12 particle path and per-tier scale per `direction-tech.md`; counts and draw primitives are that document's to set, not this one's.
The expenditure counter is the opposite of a particle: **gold as hairline rule only**, one thin dry stroke ruled across the ledger per day of scene time, accumulating in the frame's lower margin, composited un-bloomed in the post pass with zero contribution to D. By the end of the chapter the margin is a comb of gold lines and the reader has not been told a number once.

**Lighting — FLAT NOON, NO SHADOW TO HIDE IN.**
Top light, near-zero elevation offset, minimal modelling. The only chapter shot without atmosphere: no mist, no volumetrics, no dusk. Deliberately unbeautiful — a ledger is not lit, it is read. The single exception is the last shot, where a low sun finally crosses the road and the fraying reads as topography.

**Camera — 24mm, PUNITIVE, ONE UNBROKEN PAN.**
The chapter's spine is a single pan-and-scan along the road at 24mm, on rails, at a constant rate, **for longer than is comfortable** — the length of the supply line is measured in the reader's own seconds. No cut is permitted during it. 40mm inserts for the Historian on the ledger, glue pot and lacquer, axle grease. **No 180mm shot in this chapter at all**: the enemy is not the subject, the road is. Letterbox 2.39:1, held; the pan is the only motion.

**THE ONE IMAGE THEY REMEMBER — THE ROAD RUNNING OUT.**
Seen from directly above: **two ruts, running the full length of the pan, laid solid at the home end and breaking to dry-brush and then to nothing but paper tooth before they reach the far end** — and the front, the whole reason for them, is off the end of the road and never arrives in frame. The army is not shown. The road is the army. The reader watches a mark run out of ink over the course of a minute and understands that this is what a campaign is.

## F. TACTIC DECISION NODE — "THE PRICE"

**Situation (Commander):**
> The king has asked your number and he is holding a cheaper one. Say two hundred thousand and the commission is yours today. Say six hundred thousand and you will be called an old woman in front of the court — and if it is granted, the state has nothing left, and you will be the man holding all of it.

**OPTION A — TWO HUNDRED THOUSAND. Take the commission.**
Move now, move light, live off the country, finish before the treasury notices.

*Simulated visual consequence:* The gold comb in the lower margin stays short — six rules, no more — and the page stays pale, the cleanest the reader will feel in Act I, and it feels like winning. The ruts are laid fast: velocity outruns the brush's reserve almost immediately and they go 飛白 within their first third, ragged, gorgeous, hollow. Then a second stroke — not yours — crosses them at right angles and does not stop. Where it crosses, **water remixes**: your remaining moisture carries your ruts' identity into grey, which is the only way a form dies in this world without darkening (manifesto §3.8), and the reader sees a rout here for the first time. Two depot stamps go with it. Nothing lightens; you simply cannot tell any more which mark was yours. **Ink spent: +0.009 laid, +0.006 in the crossing. Page density after: 0.043 entering from Ch1 Option A, and roughly two fifths of your own mark is no longer readable as yours.**

**OPTION B — SIX HUNDRED THOUSAND. Refuse the discount.**
Name the true figure. Take the insult. Do not move until it is granted.

*Simulated visual consequence:* The gold comb consumes the entire lower third of the frame in a single ruled burst — the largest expenditure event in the first three chapters, and it happens **before anything military occurs**. In exchange the ruts are laid at 濃墨 and *stay wet for the whole chapter* — M held at 0.55, never drying, because they are being continuously reloaded. The enemy's mark, at the far margin, is the one that goes dry-brush and breaks. You win by being the mark that is still wet. **Ink spent: +0.012, front-loaded, and there is no path in the build where it is refunded. Page density after: 0.040.**

**HISTORICAL VERDICT (revealed at M ≈ 0):**

*The chapter.* 日費千金，然後十萬之師舉矣 — the bill precedes the army, not the other way round. And 兵貴勝，不貴久 — what is valued is winning, not lasting; 夫兵久而國利者，未之有也 — no protracted war has ever profited a state. Note what this does *not* say. It does not say move fast for its own sake, and it does not license a slow war on the grounds that it has been funded. **The chapter has no clause permitting protraction at any price.**

*The record.* **Li Xin chose A**, conventionally in 225 BC. *Shiji* 73 records early victories at Pingyu, Qǐn, Yan-Ying and Chengfu, then a Chu pursuit of three days and nights without halting to camp, which broke into two Qin camps, killed seven duwei, and put the army of Qin to flight. **Wang Jian chose B.** He held his price, was granted six hundred thousand, took his estates-and-messengers insurance against a suspicious sovereign, fortified in Chu and refused battle for **久之 — an unstated length of time** — struck the Chu army the moment it broke camp to move east, and Xiang Yan was killed at 蘄南; **歲餘** afterwards the King of Chu was taken. *Shiji* 6 tells the end differently, and the site does not choose between them.

*The site's line.* B is right about the **price** and wrong about the **clock**, and Chapter 2 says so. The general who names the true number before mobilising is doing the chapter's work. The general who then sits for an unmeasured season is doing something the chapter has explicitly never seen benefit a state — and got away with it because Qin could afford a thing that has ruined every state that could not. The reader should leave this node knowing the difference between a rule and an exception, and knowing which one they are.

## G. CODEX MODE ENTRY — 作戰 · THE PRICE OF A DAY

**DICTUM.** An army is a fire, and what it burns is not the enemy — it is your own country, at a fixed rate, every day it stays lit.

- **The unit is the day, not the battle.** A thousand in gold per day for a hundred thousand men, spent on glue, lacquer, axles, envoys and grain, before a single engagement. The bill starts when the army forms, not when it fights.
- **拙速 is a concession, not a slogan.** Crude and quick beats clever and slow. Sun Tzu is not praising speed; he is pricing duration — and he closes the door on it: no long war has ever profited a state.
- **Twenty to one.** A measure of the enemy's grain is worth twenty of your own. The ratio is Sunzi's; the usual explanation — that the road ate the other nineteen — is later commentary, and the site marks it as such. Either way it is a description of what is about to happen to somebody's farm.

**APPLY IT.** A halted host eats what a marching host eats. Before reckoning what any undertaking might gain, reckon the daily cost of it merely standing still, and multiply that by the number of days you cannot yet name. If the prize will not repay a season of standing, you were beaten before you deployed — and if you cannot say how many days of standing you can afford, you have not raised an army. You have lit a fire in your own granary.

# CHAPTER 3 — 謀攻

## A. IDENTITY

| field | value |
|---|---|
| Number | 3 of 13 |
| Chinese title | **謀攻** |
| Pinyin | **Móu Gōng** |
| Literal | "planning the attack" / "attacking by scheme" |
| Standard English | **Attack by Stratagem** (Giles). Also *Offensive Strategy* (Griffith 1963), *Planning the Attack* (Ames 1993), *Planning Offensives* (Sawyer 1993). |
| Sharper alternative | **TAKING IT WHOLE** |

*Why the alternative is sharper:* "Attack by Stratagem" makes the chapter sound like a chapter about tricks. The word the chapter actually turns on is 全 — *whole, intact, undamaged*. Take the enemy's state whole; his army, his brigade, his company, his squad. Breaking them is listed second every time, at **five** scales. The chapter is not about clever attacks. It is about the difference between acquiring a thing and destroying it, and about the fact that a wrecked prize is an expense.

## B. THE CORE STRATEGIC DICTUM

> **Excellence is not taking the city by storm; it is receiving the city whole. A siege announces that your mind failed three months before your ladders arrived.**

The chapter sets out a ladder of four instruments in descending order — 伐謀 attack his plans, 伐交 attack his 交, 伐兵 attack his army, 攻城 attack his cities — and then explains why the bottom rung is so far below the others: siege engines and approach ramps take three months apiece, and a commander who loses patience sends his men up the wall like ants and kills a third of them without taking the place. Above the ladder sits the standard by which all four are judged: winning a hundred battles out of a hundred is not the finest thing; subduing the enemy's forces without fighting is. Then a ratio table (ten to one, surround; five to one, attack; two to one, divide; equal, engage; fewer, withdraw; outmatched, avoid), then the three ways a sovereign wrecks his own army from the capital, then five conditions for knowing victory in advance, and finally the line everyone can quote.

**Where the popular reading is wrong.** 知彼知己，百戰不殆 is sold as a promise of victory. It is not. 殆 means *endangered, imperilled* — knowing both sides means you will not be *at risk* in a hundred engagements; it says nothing about winning them. Sun Tzu's ladder is not moral either: 伐謀 outranks 攻城 because of cost and yield, not because bloodlessness is virtuous — the chapter's own ratio table tells you to surround, attack, divide and engage when the numbers allow, and 全 is a preference about *what you get to keep*, not a scruple. And the half of the chapter nobody quotes is aimed at your own side: a ruler who orders an advance or a retreat without knowing the army cannot make it, who administers the army as if it were the civil service, or who appoints commanders without understanding the chain of command, produces 亂軍引勝 — he confuses his own army and hands the victory away. Half of "know yourself" means knowing who above you is about to lose the war for you.

## C. THREE KEY LINES

**1. The standard.**

- **Chinese:** 是故百戰百勝，非善之善者也；不戰而屈人之兵，善之善者也。
- **Pinyin:** Shì gù bǎi zhàn bǎi shèng, fēi shàn zhī shàn zhě yě; bù zhàn ér qū rén zhī bīng, shàn zhī shàn zhě yě.
- **Literal:** "Therefore a hundred battles and a hundred victories is not the finest of the fine; subduing the enemy's soldiers without fighting is the finest of the fine."
- **Modern:** An unbeaten record is not excellence. Bending the enemy's army to your will without a battle is excellence.
- **Confidence:** High. This is the line the whole site is built on and it is textually secure. 屈 is *to bend, to make submit* — not "conquer."

**2. The ladder.**

- **Chinese:** 故上兵伐謀，其次伐交，其次伐兵，其下攻城。攻城之法，為不得已。
- **Pinyin:** Gù shàng bīng fá móu, qí cì fá jiāo, qí cì fá bīng, qí xià gōng chéng. Gōng chéng zhī fǎ, wéi bù dé yǐ.
- **Literal:** "So the highest use of arms attacks plans; next, attacks 交; next, attacks troops; the lowest attacks cities. The method of attacking cities is for when there is no alternative."
- **Confidence:** High on the characters. **The second rung is a live interpretive split and the site must show it, not pick silently.**
  - **Cao Cao (the oldest commentary): 交，將合也** — 交 is the *joining* of forces; 伐交 is striking the enemy as his contingents converge, before they are one army. Giles's "prevent the junction of the enemy's forces" follows this school; it is not a modern howler.
  - **Du Mu, Wang Xi and the later commentarial mainstream** read 交 as *relations, alliances* (間其交, drive a wedge between his associations) — the diplomatic reading.
  - **The site chooses the diplomatic reading for Chapter 3** because that is the rung the Zhang Yi vignette occupies, **and says out loud that it is a choice.** The Historian carries Cao Cao's gloss in the apparatus. A reader who leaves this chapter thinking "junctions" is a mistranslation has been misled by us.

**3. The line everyone quotes, at its true strength.**

- **Chinese:** 知彼知己，百戰不殆；不知彼而知己，一勝一負；不知彼，不知己，每戰必殆。
- **Pinyin:** Zhī bǐ zhī jǐ, bǎi zhàn bù dài; bù zhī bǐ ér zhī jǐ, yī shèng yī fù; bù zhī bǐ, bù zhī jǐ, měi zhàn bì dài.
- **Literal:** "Know the other, know yourself: a hundred battles, no peril. Not knowing the other but knowing yourself: one victory, one defeat. Knowing neither: every battle certainly imperilled."
- **Modern:** Know him and know yourself and a hundred engagements will not endanger you. Know only yourself and you will win half. **Know neither and every battle is certainly dangerous** — which is not the same as certainly lost, and the difference is the whole chapter.
- **Confidence:** High on characters and sense. **Flag:** many editions print 知彼知己**者**，百戰不殆 with the nominaliser 者; both readings circulate and neither changes the meaning. Note also that 不殆 (*not endangered*) is frequently mistranscribed in popular quotation as 百戰百勝 (*a hundred victories*) — which is the phrase this very chapter has already dismissed twelve lines earlier. The misquotation inverts the chapter.

## D. THE VIGNETTE

> **Gating.** Codex apparatus and post-commit Story material. Not legible before the node resolves.

### SIX LI
**The court of Chu at Ying. 313 BC.**
*King Huai of Chu · Zhang Yi of Qin · Chen Zhen · King Huiwen of Qin*
*(Conventional identification: Ying at Jiangling on the Yangzi until 278 BC; some scholars place it on the Han instead. Give it as conventional.)*

The most expensive sentence of the fourth century BC is spoken by a man with a grudge and no army, in a room, without a weapon in it. Years earlier, at a Chu minister's feast, a jade disc went missing and Zhang Yi was the poor man in the room; they flogged him. He asked his wife afterwards whether his tongue was still in his head. She said it was. He said then it would be enough.

Qin wants Qi. Qin cannot have Qi, because Chu and Qi are joined — the vertical alliance, the north–south chain that exists for exactly one purpose, which is to make the state in the west unable to eat the states in the east one at a time. Every soldier Qin would need to break that chain by force is a soldier Qin would rather spend elsewhere. So Zhang Yi goes south to Ying instead, and he goes with an offer.

Close your passes against Qi, he says. Break the treaty. Do it publicly. And Qin will hand over the land of Shang and Yu — six hundred li of it, the Dan valley, the ground between us — and our two states will be brothers.

Six hundred li for a signature. The king is delighted. The court comes forward to congratulate him and the thing has the shape of a gift.

One man does not come forward. Chen Zhen has served in Qin and knows what the offer is worth. His counsel is not *refuse* — it is subtler, and it is Chapter 3 in a sentence: **break with Qi in appearance and stay joined in fact**; send a man north with Zhang Yi; when the land is actually in our hands, break with Qi then, and it will not be too late. Keep the alliance alive until the payment clears.

The king's reply is one of the most quoted refusals in the tradition. He tells Chen Zhen to shut his mouth and wait for the land to arrive. Then he takes the chancellor's seal off his own belt and puts it in Zhang Yi's hands, and loads him with gifts, and the room approves.

And then he over-performs. Closing the passes is not enough; the rupture must be *personal*, unmendable, loud, so that Qin can be in no doubt of his good faith. The order goes out for a man to be sent north to stand in front of the King of Qi and curse him.

The envoy leaves the hall. The seal of the chancellor of Chu is on the belt of a man from Qin, walking the other way, and neither of the two things Chu has just given away cost Qin anything at all.

**What happens next, in the record.** Zhang Yi reaches Xianyang, falls from his chariot on arrival, is injured, and does not come to court for three months. The land does not arrive. King Huai concludes that Qin thinks the break with Qi insufficient — 儀以吾絕齊為尚薄邪, *does Zhang Yi think my break with Qi was not thorough enough?* — and **only now** is the insult mission sent north. Qi, abused by an ally for nothing, turns and makes terms with Qin. Only then does Zhang Yi come to court, and when the Chu envoy asks for the six hundred li he expresses surprise: 臣有奉邑六里 — *I have an appanage of six li; I should be glad to present it to His Majesty's attendants.* Six li. Not six hundred.

King Huai mobilises. In **312 BC** the armies meet at **Danyang (丹陽)** and the Chu army is destroyed. He calls up what is left, drives north-west as far as Lantian, is beaten again, and has to turn round when Han and Wei come down into Chu from the north as far as Deng. Qin takes Hanzhong — which was not on offer in any negotiation, and which *Shiji* 5 sizes at **six hundred li**, the exact figure Chu was promised and never received.

Count what Qin spent. An envoy's travel. Three months of a man staying at home with an injury. Two sentences, one at each end of the affair, and the difference between them was a single numeral.

**Record vs legend.** The episode is told across *Shiji* 70 (biography of Zhang Yi), *Shiji* 40 (hereditary house of Chu) and *Shiji* 5 (basic annals of Qin), and the three accounts do not agree. The site cites **ch. 70** as its base and names the divergences.

- **The flogging over the jade disc and the exchange with his wife about his tongue** open ch. 70 and read as anecdote, not record.
- **The insult mission is ONE mission, and it comes *after* the three-month absence**, prompted by the king's own question about whether the break was thorough enough. There is no earlier, second trip; a previous draft of this packet invented one. **Source split on who goes:** ch. 40 names a 勇士, Song Yi (宋遺); ch. 70 has a warrior go to Song and *borrow Song's credentials* (至宋，借宋之符) before cursing the King of Qi. Name the split; do not merge it.
- **The six li.** Ch. 70: 臣有奉邑六里 — a personal appanage. Ch. 40 instead describes a surveyed stretch, 從某至某，廣袤六里, and does not call it a fief. Pick one and cite the other; do not blend "fief" and "survey line" into a single record.
- **The chariot fall.** Ch. 40: 詳醉墜車, feigned drunkenness. Ch. 70: 詳失綏墮車, feigned loss of the mounting-strap. Different details, same three months.
- **Danyang.** Chs. 5, 40 and 70 name the battle 丹陽; the Qu Yuan biography (ch. 84) has 丹、淅. The location of 丹陽 is itself a commentarial dispute (Dan-water school versus Hanzhong and others), and should be given as disputed.
- **The captives.** Ch. 5: 庶長章 (Wei Zhang, holding the rank 庶長) 擊楚於丹陽，虜其將屈匄，斬首八萬 — Qu Gai captured, eighty thousand heads, **no figure of seventy and no rank 執珪**. Ch. 40: 虜…屈匄、裨將軍逢侯丑等七十餘人 — Qu Gai and the deputy general Feng Houchou and **more than seventy others of 裨將軍 rank**. Ch. 70: 殺屈丐 — Qu Gai **killed**, not captured — and 秦齊共攻楚, Qin and Qi attacking Chu *together*, which makes Qi a co-belligerent rather than merely a lost ally. The formula "**seventy nobles of 執珪 rank**" is **Zizhi tongjian** wording (列侯執珪), not any of these three *Shiji* chapters, and 執珪 is a Chu jade-tablet noble rank — **never render it "executive rank," which is a modern administrative invention.** Quote ch. 40's 裨將軍七十餘人, or the *Tongjian*'s 執珪, and say which.
- **The eighty thousand** is a victor's claim recorded by the victor's successors.
- **The persuasion itself** survives in parallel *Zhanguo ce* versions with different details and speakers; both it and the *Shiji* draw on a Warring States genre of rhetorical set-pieces in which persuaders are given perfect lines.
- **"Shang and Yu"** is reasonably secure as the Dan river valley in the borderland of modern southern Shaanxi and western Henan; the precise extent of "six hundred li" is not.
- **King Huai's death is a different decade and a different king.** He went to Qin under safe-conduct in **299 BC**, was detained, and died there in **296** — under King Zhaoxiang, some thirteen years after Danyang and long after Huiwen was dead. It is not the last beat of this story; it is a later story about the same weakness, and the site must not run them together.

## E. VISUAL CUE

**Environment — THE SEAM, SEEN NEARLY EDGE-ON.**
Two allied forces drawn as **one continuous stroke**: Chu and Qi are not two marks that touch, they are a single unbroken deposit that happens to have two thick lobes. The land between them — the alliance itself — is the narrow wet bridge of the stroke's waist. **The camera is not overhead.** Chapter 2 owns the plan view; Chapter 3 sits almost in the plane of the sheet, so the near lobe is a foreground mass, the far lobe is a background mass, and the waist runs *away* from the reader into depth. There is no city in this chapter and no fortification of any kind. Chapter 3's argument is the seam and nothing else.

**Dominant ink behaviour — SEPARATION WITHOUT DEPOSIT.**
Chapter 3's whole demonstration is that you can change the enemy's shape without adding density. The alliance-stroke is wet and connected. A single vermilion mark placed *on the seam* acts as a chemical dam: the moisture bridge stops travelling, ∂M/∂t crosses zero along the waist, and a **tide line crystallises across it — bright, hard-edged, permanent**, exactly as the Ink Law defines 水痕 (manifesto §3.6). The one stroke is now two strokes. Nothing lightened; nothing was added; the enemy simply stopped being a single object. Vermilion never blends and beads proud of the fibre, so the seal sits between the two receding fronts as the only colour in the frame.
**Nothing in this chapter touches S.** Sizing is Chapter 4's mechanic and Chapter 4 owns its reveal outright (`canon-04-06.md`: "this is the chapter that owns it"). Chapter 3 contains no sized region, no alum, no negative-space architecture, and no ink-refusal silhouette. A previous draft of this packet spent Chapter 4's central image one chapter early; it has been removed entirely.

**Particle system — VERMILION SHEAR, AND NOTHING ELSE.**
The only emitter is **dry cinnabar dust shearing off the broken seal wafer**: a few dozen hard, opaque flakes lifting from the stamp as the wax cracks, drifting a short distance along the seam and settling. Sheet-bound, no bloom, unblended, at the far edge of the 3% colour cap. **Chapter 3 does not run a crystallisation emitter at the drying front** — that emitter belongs to Chapter 4, where accumulated tide-line crystal draws the fortification the reader was never shown, and it must debut there. Chapter 3 is the quietest particle budget on the site, deliberately: the chapter about not spending should not be visually generous.

**Lighting — ONE SHAPED WEDGE, THE REST AMBIENT.**
Late low sun through a doorway: a single hard-edged **wedge** of light lying across the sheet, with the seam falling inside it and both lobes falling partly outside. This is a *shaped* source, not a grazing rake — Chapter 5 owns raking, Chapter 4 owns shadowless, Chapter 1 owns crossed interior wicks, Chapter 6 owns value inversion. The paper tooth reads only inside the wedge; the tide line, when it forms, throws a real micro-shadow along the lit edge and is invisible where the waist runs out of the wedge, which is the shot. **No specular pass, no PBR, no highlight on raw paper** — un-inked paper in this engine is flat and matte, and the previous draft's "subtle change in how the fibre catches light" is struck.

**Camera — 85mm ON THE SEAM, 180mm ACROSS, ONE OF THE THREE MATCH CUTS.**
The chapter's home lens is 85mm on the named factor, and the named factor here is a *gap between two friends*. 40mm for the Historian on the envoy's tally-sticks and the fief deed. 180mm once, across the valley, at the enemy — and it holds long enough to be uncomfortable. **This chapter spends one of the site's three permitted match cuts on ink:** the six-hundred-li territory, written large, match-cutting on the stroke of 六 to the same character at one-hundredth the scale on a personal fief deed. That match cut *is* the vignette's punchline and should be budgeted here rather than anywhere in Act I.

**THE ONE IMAGE THEY REMEMBER — ONE STROKE BECOMING TWO, WITH NO INK ADDED.**
Hold on the waist of the alliance-stroke. A single small vermilion mark is set at the seam — the only colour in the frame, well under the 3% cap. The wet bridge stops travelling. A tide line crystallises across it, edge to edge, bright and hard, in about two seconds; the fronts recede a hair on either side and pin. The camera does not move. **The enemy's army is now two armies, the page is no darker than it was, and nobody has fought.** That is the entire book in one shot, and it belongs to Chapter 3.

## F. TACTIC DECISION NODE — "SIX HUNDRED LI"

**Situation (Commander):**
> Qin offers six hundred li of the Dan valley if you close your passes against Qi. Your court is already congratulating you and the chancellor's seal is in your hand. In the outer court, Qi's own envoy is waiting on an answer he was promised today — Qi wants your border districts as the price of staying joined, and has let it be known that Qin has made Qi an offer too. Take Qin's, and you are paid for a treaty you were going to be squeezed out of anyway. One old adviser wants you to sign nothing until the surveyors have walked the boundary — which means keeping faith, today, with an ally who is at this moment extorting you.

**OPTION A — TAKE IT. Break with Qi now, publicly, and be seen to.**
Close the passes. Send the seal. Pre-empt Qi before Qi pre-empts you.

*Simulated visual consequence:* **Vermilion seal press** — the Commander's only "yes," and it is audible before it is visible. The alliance-stroke's waist stops travelling and a tide line crystallises across it, and because the connecting bridge was the darkest wet region in frame, the composition *appears* to lighten. It has not; D never falls. The illusion is produced by the camera pulling out to 24mm, and it feels exactly like gaining ground. Then the promised territory is written into the frame — and the scale bar corrects, by a factor of one hundred, in a single match cut. The 24mm shot resolves: your mark is alone, in a great deal of white, and **the white you were reading as your gain is your isolation.** The Qi lobe has dried out of contact and cannot be re-wetted for the remainder of the site. **Ink spent: +0.007. Page density after: 0.047 entering from Ch1 A / Ch2 B** — and the isolation is a permanent state flag carried into Chapters 4–13.

**OPTION B — WAIT. Break in appearance, stay joined in fact.**
Send an officer north with the envoy. Let the passes look closed. Sign nothing the boundary survey has not confirmed — and pay Qi's price to keep the seam wet one more season.

*Simulated visual consequence:* One small vermilion mark is spent on ambiguity, and it does not go on the seam. The camera **tracks laterally along the border, slowly, the length of the waist** — the only lateral move in the chapter — while the mark stamps onto a bamboo tally in the Historian's lower margin rather than onto the ground. On the sheet, a single 淡墨 wash is drawn across the waist at high water: **M is driven back up to ~0.70 and the bridge starts moving again**, and the two lobes bleed gently toward one another and hold there. No cut. No crane. No new commitment. The only lasting feedback is that the Qi lobe stays *wet* for the rest of the chapter and into the next, still faintly travelling toward you, which is what an alliance looks like when it is still alive. **Ink spent: +0.002 — a rise of two thousandths, the smallest consequence any node in the site produces. Page density after: 0.042.** The reward for the correct answer is that the page does almost nothing, and the reader has to decide whether they can live with that.

**HISTORICAL VERDICT (revealed at M ≈ 0):**

*The chapter.* 上兵伐謀，其次伐交 — attack his plans; failing that, attack his 交. Note the position on the ladder. Breaking a coalition is the *second* instrument, above armies and far above walls, and its cost is an envoy's travelling expenses. Note also — and this is the site's chosen reading, with Cao Cao's 將合也 standing beside it in the apparatus — that here the rung is being climbed *for* Qin, *by* Chu, at Chu's expense. And note what the chapter promises the reader of Option B: 知彼知己，百戰不殆. Not "you will win." You will not be *endangered*. That is the entire prize, and it looks like nothing.

*The record.* **King Huai of Chu chose A** in 313 BC. He closed the passes and handed over the chancellor's seal; three months later, hearing nothing, he sent a man north to curse the King of Qi and make the break irreversible; Qi came to terms with Qin; and Zhang Yi then offered him six *li* of a private appanage. In 312 BC he attacked Qin without allies and lost his army at Danyang — Qu Gai taken (or killed; the *Shiji* splits), more than seventy officers of 裨將軍 rank with him in ch. 40, eighty thousand heads claimed — and Qin took Hanzhong, six hundred li of it. He attacked again, lost again at Lantian, and had to withdraw when Han and Wei came into his rear.

**Chen Zhen argued for B** and was told to be quiet and wait for his land. **That is where the record stops, and the site stops with it.** Option B was never run. We do not know that Qin would have handed over Shang and Yu if Chu had waited — almost certainly it would not have — and "Chen Zhen was right" is the tradition's moral about the episode, not a completed experiment. What can be said without inventing anything: the man who proposed keeping the alliance alive until the payment cleared was overruled, the alliance was destroyed for free, and the state that destroyed it paid for the destruction with an envoy's expenses. Being the only person in the room who was doing Chapter 3 bought Chen Zhen nothing, which is also part of the lesson.

## G. CODEX MODE ENTRY — 謀攻 · TAKING IT WHOLE

**DICTUM.** Excellence is not taking the city by storm; it is receiving the city whole. To scale a rampart is to announce that your mind failed three months before your ladders arrived.

- **The ladder is a cost curve, not a morality.** Plans, then 交, then armies, then walls. Each rung down multiplies what you spend and reduces what you get to keep. Siege is the bottom because three months of engines, three months of ramps, and a third of your men dead on the wall is what a failure of foresight actually costs.
- **交 is contested, and we tell you which side we took.** Cao Cao reads it as the *joining* of the enemy's forces; the later commentators read it as his alliances. The site works the second reading, because that is what Zhang Yi did at Ying — and it prints the first.
- **全 before 破, five times over.** 國 軍 旅 卒 伍 — state, army, brigade, company, squad. Take it whole; breaking it is the inferior option at every scale. A ruined prize is an expense you now own.
- **不殆, not 百勝.** "Know the enemy and know yourself" promises freedom from *peril*, not a hundred wins — and the chapter has already said, twelve lines earlier, that a hundred wins is not excellence anyway. The popular version of this quotation contradicts the chapter it comes from.

**APPLY IT.** Before you advance against an opponent's front, find the third party whose indifference is currently protecting his flank — the ally who has not yet been asked, the neighbour with a grievance, the supplier who has no stake in the outcome. Reckon what it would cost to make that party merely neutral, and set it beside the cost of the assault, in grain and in men who will not come back. If the first number is smaller, the frontal attack was never a plan. It was anger, given a budget.
