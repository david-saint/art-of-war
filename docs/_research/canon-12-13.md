# CANON PACKET — CHAPTERS 12 & 13

**孫子兵法 · ACT IV — THE INSTRUMENTS (器)**
Governed by `/docs/_research/manifesto.md` v1.0. Act IV ink ceiling: **mean page density 0.55.**

Modern readers file these two chapters as appendices — the manifesto's §9 uses the word, and this packet keeps it, but only as a description of *our* reader's habit. The Chinese commentarial tradition does not file them that way: 用間 is the received climax, the chapter every commentator from Cao Cao onward treats as the book's argument arriving, and the Yinqueshan slips carry both chapters as part of the thirteen. Act IV's job is to restore the reader's sense of proportion to the tradition's: **the most destructive instrument placed beside the cheapest one.** Fire returns nothing you can hold. A man who will tell you where the enemy is costs a hundred, once. The reader arrives here having spent eleven chapters of ink, and Act IV tells them what the cheap thing was.

# CHAPTER 12

## A. IDENTITY

| | |
|---|---|
| **Number** | 12 of 13 |
| **Chinese title** | 火攻 |
| **Pinyin** | *Huǒ Gōng* |
| **Literal** | fire · attack |
| **Standard English** | "The Attack by Fire" (Giles) · "Incendiary Attacks" (Sawyer) |
| **Sharper alternative** | **THE WEAPON YOU CANNOT RECALL** |
| **Alt. runner-up** | "The Almanac and the Temper" — the chapter is a weather calendar bolted to a warning about a ruler's mood, and the join is the whole point |

**Why the sharper title.** "The Attack by Fire" reads as a manual heading and lets the reader file the chapter under *techniques*. But the chapter is not really about ignition; it is about what you have signed. Most instruments in the book can be halted: a march countermarched, a siege lifted, a formation re-shaped, a plan withdrawn while it is still 淡墨. Fire, once lit, continues to act on its own schedule and by the wind's consent rather than yours. The chapter itself pairs it with water — 水可以絕，不可以奪, *water can cut off, but it cannot take* — so fire is not the only auxiliary here, and we should not claim it is the only irreversible thing in the book. What it *is* is the clearest demonstration of irreversibility the book stages. That is precisely why the chapter ends — with no transition at all — on the ruler who goes to war angry. Anger and fire are the same object in the last paragraph: both cheap to start, both conditional on wind, and neither recallable. The chapter is about irreversibility, and the fire is the demonstration.

## B. THE CORE STRATEGIC DICTUM

> **Fire is aimed by the weather and not by you — and an instrument that goes on working after you have stopped must never be started by a man who is angry, because the anger will pass and the country will not.**

**Unpacking (≈195 words).** Chapter 12 is built in three movements that most readers only read two of. First, an *enumeration* — 一曰火人，二曰火積，三曰火輜，四曰火庫，五曰火隊: men in camp, stored grain, the baggage train, the arsenal, and a fifth whose reading is unsettled. It is a list, not a moral ranking. Second, a *discipline* — fire is legal only under stated meteorological conditions, with rules for each of five ways the fire can develop, including the counter-intuitive one: if the camp burns and the enemy stays calm, **wait**, because a quiet camp on fire is a camp that expected the fire. Third, the sober coda about anger.

**Two things the packet will not claim.** **(1) The mansions.** The moon standing in 箕 Sieve, 壁 Wall, 翼 Wing, 軫 Cross-bar is often waved away as mystical flavour and just as often over-corrected into "pure meteorology." Neither is honest. Moon-in-mansion timing is correlative astronomy, and the wind-association of 箕 is old — the 洪範 tradition already pairs 箕星 with wind. The text asserts wind on these four dates; the commentaries assert it too and do not derive it. We say that, and stop. Chapter 13's refusal of 鬼神 sits in *tension* with this chapter; it does not retroactively secularise it. **(2) The coda is not a moral appendix.** Readers treat "do not fight in anger" as a pious sign-off pasted onto a tactics chapter. It is the chapter's conclusion. Fire is the proof that some acts have no undo; anger is the state in which humans reliably commit such acts; therefore the chapter on the irreversible instrument is the correct place to legislate the ruler's temper.

## C. THREE KEY LINES

### C.1 — The dates for wind

| | |
|---|---|
| **Chinese (trad.)** | 發火有時，起火有日。時者，天之燥也；日者，月在箕、壁、翼、軫也。凡此四宿者，風起之日也。 |
| **Pinyin** | *Fā huǒ yǒu shí, qǐ huǒ yǒu rì. Shí zhě, tiān zhī zào yě; rì zhě, yuè zài Jī, Bì, Yì, Zhěn yě. Fán cǐ sì xiù zhě, fēng qǐ zhī rì yě.* |
| **Literal** | "Setting fire has its season; raising fire has its day. The season: the sky's dryness. The day: the moon in Sieve, Wall, Wing, Cross-bar. All these four mansions are the days of rising wind." |
| **Modern** | "There is a season for lighting fires and a date for lighting them. The season is when the air is dry; the date is when the moon stands in the Winnowing-Basket, the Wall, the Wing, or the Chariot-Board. On those four, the wind gets up." |
| **Confidence** | **High** on the four characters 箕壁翼軫 and their identification as four of the twenty-eight 宿 *xiù* lunar mansions. **Medium** on any claim about *why* these four: the received commentaries assert the wind correlation without deriving it, and the association of 箕 with wind is already traditional (cf. 洪範) rather than observational. The Historian states that the text asserts it. He does not vouch for it, and he does not call it a weather service. |

### C.2 — Windward, and the two winds

| | |
|---|---|
| **Chinese (trad.)** | 火發上風，無攻下風。晝風久，夜風止。 |
| **Pinyin** | *Huǒ fā shàng fēng, wú gōng xià fēng. Zhòu fēng jiǔ, yè fēng zhǐ.* |
| **Literal** | "Fire on the windward side; do not attack from the leeward. A day wind lasts long; a night wind stops." |
| **Modern** | "When the fire is upwind, do not attack downwind. A wind that gets up in daylight will hold; one that gets up at night will drop." |
| **Gloss (not the lemma)** | *Never attack into your own fire* — a fair paraphrase of the prohibition, but it is a gloss and the on-screen Text must carry the negative clause, not a positive order to attack. The sentence issues no attack command. |
| **Confidence** | **High.** Standard received text. The second clause is a diurnal-wind maxim — the kind of empirical-sounding rule the book is full of (cf. chs. 9–10), not a uniquely "scientific" moment. Note that it cuts against any vignette that needs a night wind to hold: on the text's own account, a night wind is the one that drops. |

### C.3 — The coda: anger is a fire

| | |
|---|---|
| **Chinese (trad.)** | 主不可以怒而興師，將不可以慍而致戰。合於利而動，不合於利而止。怒可以復喜，慍可以復悅；亡國不可以復存，死者不可以復生。 |
| **Pinyin** | *Zhǔ bù kě yǐ nù ér xīng shī, jiàng bù kě yǐ yùn ér zhì zhàn. Hé yú lì ér dòng, bù hé yú lì ér zhǐ. Nù kě yǐ fù xǐ, yùn kě yǐ fù yuè; wáng guó bù kě yǐ fù cún, sǐ zhě bù kě yǐ fù shēng.* |
| **Literal** | "A ruler may not raise an army out of wrath; a general may not bring on a battle out of pique. Move if it accords with advantage; stop if it does not. Wrath can turn back to joy, pique back to pleasure — but a perished state cannot be made to exist again, and the dead cannot be made to live again." |
| **Modern** | "No head of state sends an army because he is furious. No commander gives battle because he has been slighted. Move when it pays; stop when it does not. Your temper will improve by morning. The country will not come back, and neither will the men." |
| **Confidence** | **High.** Among the most stable passages in the transmission. |

**Two further lines held in reserve for the Historian's margin (neither is one of the three):**

1. 故以火佐攻者明，以水佐攻者強。水可以絕，不可以奪。 — "Fire as an auxiliary to attack is *clarity*; water as an auxiliary is *force*. Water can cut off; it cannot take." This is the line that stops us claiming fire is the book's only unrecallable instrument. **Confidence: high** on the text, **medium** on 明/強 as "clarity/force" — the pair is glossed variously.
2. 夫戰勝攻取，而不修其功者凶，命曰費留。 — "To win the battle and take the objective and then fail to 修其功 is calamitous; the name for it is *fèi liú*." **Confidence: medium on the gloss.** 修其功 is read variously as *consolidate the achievement*, *reward the meritorious*, or *follow through on the campaign's purpose*; 費留 is likewise contested (Giles took it as stagnation). The Historian must state that the phrase is disputed rather than pick one silently.

## D. THE VIGNETTE

### **ONE BUNDLE OF STRAW, EACH MAN**
**Yiling and Xiaoting, on the Yangtze below the gorges · summer, 222 AD**

*Dateline note for the Historian, on screen: the sources do not agree to the month. 先主傳 puts the smash in the sixth month and "some ten days" after a portent; 文帝紀 and* Zizhi Tongjian *69 put the Wu victory in the intercalary month, after a stalemate that ran through the sixth. Summer 222 is the honest dateline; the discrepancy is worth a hairline citation rather than a silent choice.*

He had been on the river for a year by then.

Liu Bei took the imperial title in the fourth month of 221 and marched east in the seventh — three months crowned, prosecuting a family matter with a state. Guan Yu's head had gone north to Cao Cao in a box. Zhang Fei never made it out of camp: murdered by two of his own officers, who took his head downriver as a gift to Wu. He is sixty-two by Chinese reckoning, about sixty-one by ours, and he has come down the river to kill Sun Quan.

Men tell him not to. Qin Mi argues the season and the omens against it and is put in prison for it. Zhao Yun tells him the enemy is Cao Wei and that Wu can wait — that one reaches us through Pei Songzhi's citation of the *Yun biezhuan*, not the main text. The *Zizhi Tongjian* says the remonstrators were many. Zhuge Liang is not among them in the third-century record; the scene where he pleads is Ming-dynasty novel. Liu Bei comes anyway, and Wu gives him the ground.

Lu Xun is forty by Chinese reckoning, about thirty-nine by ours, and the Sun-family veterans do not want to take his orders. He married Sun Ce's daughter; his reputation in that camp is for administration; Han Dang and the rest have been killing people since before he could shave. What the sneer leaves out is that he already has a Jingzhou war behind him — in 219 he held the Lukou command against Guan Yu's rear, wrote the flattering letter that pulled Guan Yu's garrisons north, and took Zigui, Zhijiang and Yidao with his own troops. Sun Quan gives him the staff of authority and full command. His first order is to retreat. His second is to retreat further. He gives up the passes, gives up several hundred li of river, and stops on ground of his own choosing, and then does the thing that is actually difficult: for something close to half a year, he does nothing at all.

His generals petition. Liu Bei puts Wu Ban's detachment down on the open plain as bait, so plainly that Lu Xun will not discuss it. *He came in fresh and fast,* he tells them. *You do not meet that. You let the river summer work.*

The river summer works. Shu has come a long way into country with no width in it — five and six hundred li of advance in the Wu memorials, and in Cao Pi's dispatch a camp line reckoned at seven hundred li and more, which at a Han li of roughly four hundred metres is somewhere between a hundred and fifty and a hundred and eighty miles. By midsummer Liu Bei's men have been standing under armour on rock since spring. He brings the men up out of the boats. Lu Xun writes it down as it happens and sends it to Sun Quan almost cheerfully: *he has abandoned the boats for the land road and is making camp everywhere; I have examined his dispositions; there will be no surprise.* Because the terrain gives Shu no room to concentrate, the positions string along the only line available: palisades and linked camps — 樹柵連營 — dozens of them, from Wuxia and Zigui down to the Yiling border. Later they will count forty-odd of them broken.

In Luoyang, Cao Pi reads the dispatch describing this and puts it down. *Liu Bei does not understand war. Seven hundred li of camps to hold off an enemy?* And then he quotes, not Sun Tzu, but a general military taboo: *he who takes his ground across plain and marsh and broken country is captured by his enemy — this is a thing soldiers avoid.* He tells his court to expect news from Wu. It arrives in seven days.

Lu Xun tries one camp first, fails, and is told he has spent men for nothing. He says: now I know how to break them.

He orders every soldier to carry one bundle of thatch — 各持一把茅 — and takes the camps by fire.

Not one fire. Not one line. The bundles go to many points at once and the whole army attacks together, 同時俱攻, in the same hour: forty-odd camps broken, the palisades burning where the tinder was carried to them, and Shu's formations pulled apart along a corridor that gave them nowhere lateral to go. What the fire did was not travel. What the fire did was arrive everywhere the same evening, and take away the one thing a strung-out army has, which is the ability to reinforce along its own length.

Zhang Nan is killed. Feng Xi is killed. Shamoke, the Wuxi Man chieftain who brought his people into somebody else's revenge, is killed. Du Lu and Liu Ning give up their commands. Fu Rong holds the rear and is called on to surrender and answers *Wu dogs — when has a Han general ever surrendered?* and dies where he is standing. Liu Bei is pushed up onto Ma'an Hill and rings the hill with what is left, and Lu Xun closes on it from four sides, and the record says the dead were counted in tens of thousands and the boats and gear and stores were gone in a single day and the bodies came down the river in numbers that blocked it.

He gets out at night. His own courier-station men burn the armour and the gongs behind him in the road to slow the pursuit, and he reaches Baidicheng with that behind him and says, to no one in particular, *that I should be humiliated by Lu Xun — is this not Heaven?*

It is not Heaven. It is drought, a gorge, a line drawn where there was no room to draw anything else, and a man who waited.

He does not come down the river again. Sunrise at Baidicheng, and the smoke is still on the water below the gorge, and the mountain air is cold, and the army is not there.

**Record vs. legend.** *Recorded* (Sanguozhi — 先主傳, 陸遜傳, 文帝紀; *Zizhi Tongjian* ch. 69): the campaign's cause and Liu Bei's personal motive; Zhang Fei's murder by subordinates before departure; Qin Mi's remonstrance and imprisonment; Zhao Yun's remonstrance (via Pei Songzhi's citation of 雲別傳); Lu Xun's appointment over senior objections and his prior 219 command against Guan Yu; the long refusal to engage; Wu Ban's bait camp on the plain; Lu Xun's memorial on the boats-to-land-road move; the linked palisaded camps and Cao Pi's recorded prediction, with the 兵忌 quotation and the seven days; the failed probing attack; the order that each man carry one bundle of thatch; the simultaneous general assault and the forty-odd camps broken; Zhang Nan, Feng Xi and Shamoke killed; Du Lu and Liu Ning surrendered; Fu Rong's refusal and death; Huang Quan's northern command cut off and surrendering to Wei; Ma'an Hill; the night flight and the burning of gear in the road; Liu Bei's quoted remark; his death at Baidicheng in 223.

*Approximate:* the month (see dateline note); troop numbers — the usual figures are roughly 50,000 for Wu against a Shu field force of comparable or slightly smaller size; and the li-to-mile conversion, which depends on which Han li you use.

*Not in the third-century record:* Zhuge Liang remonstrating against the campaign; Shu troops jeering, stripping, and taunting the Wu palisade; thatch *roofs* on the camps (the named construction is 樹柵, palisade; 茅 is what Wu carried as tinder); "the water was unbearable" as Liu Bei's stated motive for leaving the boats — that is inference, and the surviving observation is Lu Xun's, not Liu Bei's.

*Legend:* the 700,000-strong Shu host, the vengeance-quest structure with Zhuge Liang's stone maze trapping Lu Xun in retreat, and every scene of personal duelling — all Ming-dynasty *Romance of the Three Kingdoms*.

*Aftermath, for the Historian's margin only, and stated accurately:* in the tenth month of 222, with Liu Bei still alive at Baidicheng, Sun Quan grew alarmed and sued for peace, and Liu Bei accepted, sending Zong Wei in reply. Liu Bei died in the fourth month of 223. Deng Zhi went to Wu in 223 and again in 224 to reconfirm the anti-Wei alignment, because Zhuge Liang feared Wu would drift after the death. **Jingzhou stayed with Wu.** The pre-219 arrangement was never restored, and Wu had in 221 accepted a title from Wei before breaking with it. Do not write "the alliance was rebuilt on the terms it had before."

> **Note on the declined anchor.** Red Cliffs (208 AD) is the obvious Chapter 12 vignette and it is deliberately not used here. Red Cliffs is a fire *trick* and the audience already owns it as spectacle; Yiling is a fire *doctrine* — season, dryness, patience, the ways to burn a thing, and the anger-clause coda all in one recorded episode, with the man who started the war in a rage dying of it. Red Cliffs is recommended instead as an *insert* inside Chapter 5 (勢, stored energy released) or as an eight-second Historian citation here.

## E. VISUAL CUE

**ENVIRONMENT.** Night, a river gorge, seen from a high promontory on the southern rim — Lu Xun's vantage, looking *down* into it. The gorge is not a flat band across the lower frame: it recedes in a deep S-bend, three visible reaches of it, the far one small. The corridor's walls read as folded ink at depth rather than as two flat slabs at frame left and right, and the river is a strip of reserved paper that narrows with distance and disappears round the second bend. The encampment is not one stroke. It is **forty-odd separate marks following the folds of the gorge in perspective** — short, palisade-dark, each one a little smaller than the last, laid at intervals that do not read as a line until you have followed them round the second bend and understood that they never stop. *That* is the argument: not a stroke that conducts, but a distribution that cannot reinforce itself, drawn so the reader's eye has to travel it.

*(Explicit anti-repetition note. Chapter 7's defile is a receding V at ground level, Chapter 10's is two ridge walls with the camera inside them, and Chapter 2's road is one continuous stroke seen from directly overhead. Chapter 12 must not be any of those: it is the only chapter shot from **above and outside** its own terrain, and the only one whose subject is an interval repeated in depth rather than a single mark.)*

**DOMINANT INK BEHAVIOUR — 焦墨, charring.** This is the site's only chapter where ink *changes state* rather than merely spreading, and it does so without violating monotonic D. Fire is not light and does not lighten anything. Fire is **accelerated A**: the age field runs forward at ~120× behind an advancing front, so the paper browns toward `#DCD3BE` and past it, while D at the front is driven **up** to burnt maximum — charcoal is more carbon, not less. A camp mark does not vanish; it *hardens*, loses its wet edge permanently, and its 4-octave FBM isocontour displacement is frozen at the instant of char, so each burnt boundary is jagged and final forever. Moisture is annihilated ahead of each front, which means **the fires dry the fog of war as they go** — you can suddenly see how far the enemy's dispositions run, and the reason you can see it is that they are burning. Act IV's first taste of the Chapter 13 sensation.

**Ignition is multi-point and simultaneous, and the shader must say so.** Four to eight origins seeded across the visible reaches within the same beat, not one origin and a travelling line. The reader should register *many* before they register *spreading*.

**PARTICLE SYSTEM — the only embers in the build.** Per the manifesto's refusal list, embers exist nowhere else. Spec: vermilion `#C1352B` cores at ~0.4× bloom via Shader #6's MRT mask, beading *proud* of the surface and never blending into the ink; advected by the wind field, not by gravity, and they land. Each landed ember becomes a permanent deposit — a char pit that persists in the reader's scroll to the end of the site. Ember count is capped so accent coverage stays under the 3% frame rule; the fire is mostly black and brown, with red only at the working edge.

**The smoke is not a particle system.** Smoke is 淡墨 advected by wind across the paper above the marks — a wash, obeying the Ink Law, depositing a tide line along the gorge rim that never comes out. See the pipeline addendum: it is a rendered line-source texture, not a point deposit, because forty camps cannot be expressed through eight uniform slots.

**LIGHTING.** Volumetric, multi-source, from *below and inside the frame* — the only bottom-lit shot in the site, and the sources are down in the gorge under the camera. Everything is uplit by the thing destroying it. Cliff faces catch a warm bounce that is emphatically not gold (gold is authority and expenditure; this is neither) but the paper's aged tone pushed hot. No god-rays — they are a doctrine deletion and Shader #7 is fog only.

**The reserved white takes no highlight. Ever.** Manifesto §4: the reserved white is never emissive and never a highlight, and there is no reflection buffer in the pipeline (Shader #5 is refraction only — no reflection, no Fresnel, no blue). The river is polluted the way this project pollutes anything, which is by deposit: as fires reach the banks, charred debris and soot are spawned into the water pass and carried on the flow vectors, laying **permanent dark tide lines along the sandbars and the inside of each bend** — D ≈ 0.18, monotonic, hard-edged. The reader loses the clean page to a stain that arrives downstream, on the clock, in front of them. That is a better shot than a reflection and it is legal.

**CAMERA — 24mm, per the doctrine's punitive-scale jurisdiction.** Not a lateral dolly: Chapter 7 (24mm unbroken lateral track), Chapter 10 (24mm unbroken crane) and Chapter 11 (24mm unbroken right-to-left traverse) have each already spent that move, and a fourth would read as the house style rather than as an argument. Chapter 12 is a **high oblique descent**: the camera starts on the promontory rim looking down the length of the S-bend, and over the sequence sinks and pitches — losing the far reaches behind the first fold one at a time — so the frame's information *decreases* as the fire's does not. Rails only, no roll, no orbit. One cut, fired by reader action, to **180mm**: the enemy compressed across the gorge, which per doctrine is the only lens permitted to hold them, and at 180mm you finally see that the burning marks are made of people. Then the letterbox goes to 2.76:1 for the node.

**THE ONE IMAGE THEY REMEMBER.** *Forty fires, lit at once, receding round a bend.* The reader counts the near ones, loses count at the second fold, and understands in about a second that the count was never the point: no camp on that corridor can reach the next one in time, because it was never drawn to. They understand it as a *spacing* error before they understand it as a military one — and the sentence under it is Cao Pi's borrowed taboo, not Sun Tzu's, because the record has him quoting a proverb and not this book.

### **PIPELINE ADDENDUM — CH.12** *(binding on the build; extends `direction-tech.md` §5)*

**Fire as accelerated A — added to Shader #1 (Ink field sim, GPGPU).** New uniforms: `vec2 uFireOrigin[8]; float uFireT[8]; int uFireCount; vec2 uWindDir; float uCharRate; float uFrontWidth;`. The B channel of the RGBA16F pair is re-tasked for this chapter as `B = max(tide, char)` with the top bit carrying a *char-locked* flag.

```glsl
float front = 0.0;
for (int i = 0; i < uFireCount; ++i) {
  vec2  w  = normalize(uWindDir);
  vec2  r  = uv - uFireOrigin[i];
  float dn = dot(r, w);                                  // downwind component
  float lt = length(r - dn * w);                         // lateral component
  float d  = max(dn / 3.1, -dn) + lt;                    // teardrop, not a disc
  front    = max(front, smoothstep(uFireT[i] + uFrontWidth, uFireT[i] - uFrontWidth, d));
  M        = mix(M, 0.0, smoothstep(uFireT[i] + 0.04, uFireT[i], d));  // 0.04-UV dry band AHEAD
}
A        = mix(A, min(A + uDt * uCharRate, 1.0), front);  // uCharRate = 120 × base age rate
D        = max(D, front * 0.95);                          // monotonic; char is more carbon
charLock = max(charLock, step(0.5, front));               // frozen-FBM flag, written once
```

**Freezing the burnt boundary — Shader #2 (Ink dispersion + dark rim).** The 4-octave FBM displacement is time-seeded; gate the seed on the lock so the isocontour stops evolving at the instant of char and the jagged edge is final:
`float seedT = mix(uTime, uCharTime, charLock);` — then feed `seedT` where `uTime` fed `curl()`. The displacement still applies only in the `0.02 < D < 0.28` band, and the rim still adds to **D**, not to opacity.

**Smoke as an advected wash — new source pass, not `uDeposits[8]`.** Eight point-deposit slots cannot express forty camps. Render a **512×512 R8 `smokeSourceRT`** once per frame from an instanced quad emitter (one quad per camp, ~48 instances, one draw call), writing deposit rate. Bind to #1 as `sampler2D uSmokeSource`:

```glsl
float s = texture(uSmokeSource, uv).r;
M = max(M, s * 0.85 * uDt);                                       // wash is wet on arrival
D = max(D, min(D + s * 0.03 * uDt * exp(-uSmokeAge * 0.35), 0.12)); // decays; hard-capped
```

The exponential decay plus the 0.12 clamp is what keeps a continuous line-source from saturating the frame: the whole smoke pass contributes **≤ 0.03 to Act IV mean density**, and its only permanent residue is the rim tide line where `∂M/∂t` crosses zero.

**River soot deposit — Shader #5 (water-surface distortion), no reflection added.** Soot flakes are spawned at bank contact points and advected by `uFlow`; where a flake's residence time crosses threshold it writes a deposit into the ink field at `D = 0.18`, biased to the inside of each bend where flow speed drops. No new buffer, no Fresnel, no emissive white.

**Ember budget.** Shader #6 unchanged; instance cap 24k (not 65k) because we are at 24mm on a receding corridor and per-ember screen area is small. MRT bloom mask unchanged at 0.4×.

## F. TACTIC DECISION NODE — "THE QUIET CAMP"

**Node phase spec:** LOAD (brush loads, aperture → 2.76:1) → MARK → BLOOM → DRY (4–12s scene time) → READ. Analysis is legal only at M ≈ 0.

**THE SITUATION — the Commander (行草 on the operative term, tracked small-caps jade, no voice; a drum, a slip set down):**

> Your fire is inside his camp. You did not have to reach it — you paid a man to carry it, and he is still in there.
> **The wind came up two hours after dark.** You know what the book says about a night wind.
> At the western end the palisade is going and you can hear it going. At the centre there is smoke you cannot see through and figures crossing the gaps at a walk. **You cannot tell whether that is a line forming or a line leaving.**

**OPTION A — 早應之於外. Cross now.**
Hit him while the fire is still doing your work and the wind is still lent to you. The text is explicit: when fire breaks out within, respond at once from without. You will be moving into your own smoke and you will not be able to read the ground you take.

**OPTION B — 待而勿攻. Hold on the bank.**
The text is also explicit: if fire breaks out and his troops stay quiet, wait and do not attack. But 待 costs what it costs — the night wind is the one the text says drops, and a fire that finishes is a fire that stops telling you anything.

*(Both options are literally sanctioned by different sentences of Chapter 12, and neither sentence's condition is cleanly satisfied by what the reader can see: the camp is partly loud and partly silent, and the discriminator the doctrine wants — 其兵靜 — is exactly the thing the smoke has taken away. This node has no safe answer and no reconvergence.)*

**SIMULATED VISUAL CONSEQUENCE — A. Ink cost +0.07.**

No cut. Metal, not drum.

**THE HISTORICAL VERDICT (revealed at M ≈ 0, apparatus grotesque, margin, hairline rule).**

Sun Tzu gives both instructions and puts a condition between them: 火發而其兵靜者，待而勿攻 — *if the fire breaks out and his troops are quiet, wait and do not attack.* A camp that burns without panicking is a camp that knew. But the chapter's five 變 are not a decision tree with one exit; they are a demand that you keep reading the enemy *after* your plan has succeeded, which is the moment nobody does it — and reading requires that you can see, which fire is very good at preventing.

**What Option A actually looks like in the record.** At Yiling it is **Lu Xun**, not Liu Bei. Wu carried the thatch to many camps and attacked in the same hour, 同時俱攻; the fire and the assault were one act, which is 早應之於外 executed with no gap in it at all. Forty-odd camps broken, Shu pushed onto Ma'an Hill and enveloped there, the dead counted in tens of thousands. Liu Bei is not the commander choosing Option A. **He is the camp.** (Sanguozhi 陸遜傳; *Zizhi Tongjian* ch. 69.) The one thing the Shu side did with fire that night was burn its own armour and gongs across the road to slow a pursuit — men leaving, not men counter-attacking.

**What Option B actually looks like in the record — and what it does not.** Lu Xun's famous refusal to fight is a **pre-fire** decision, and its stated reason is that you do not meet a fresh, fast army: that is not 待而勿攻, which is a rule about a camp already burning. Then, at the fire, he did the opposite of waiting. Then, afterwards, he refused to pursue to Baidicheng — with Zhu Ran and Luo Tong, against Xu Sheng, Pan Zhang and Song Qian, and for a reason that has nothing to do with this chapter: Cao Pi was massing men on his flank under the pretext of helping. Three separate acts, one of them 待而勿攻 and it is not the famous one. **Nor did the restraint save Wu by itself: Wei invaded anyway within months.** The Historian says all three and declines the counterfactual.

**And the line the node exists to set up:** whichever way the reader went, the Commander's last card is 亡國不可以復存，死者不可以復生 — laid on the ink they just spent, on dry paper, in gold.

*(16:9, orthographic, Ink Law frozen at current density. Text + Historian. Inserts remain 2.39:1, inset like a slip on a desk.)*

**火攻 · 12 · THE WEAPON YOU CANNOT RECALL**

> **DICTUM —** Fire is aimed by the weather and not by you — and an instrument that goes on working after you have stopped must never be started by a man who is angry, because the anger will pass and the country will not.

- **Five things to burn, as the text lists them:** 火人 men in camp · 火積 stored grain · 火輜 the baggage train · 火庫 the arsenal · 火隊 — a fifth whose reading is genuinely disputed (supply routes, or fire dropped onto formations). It is an enumeration, 一曰 to 五曰, not a ranking of severity, and we do not present it as one. *Flagged: the received text is unstable here and we say so.*
- **The mansions give dates, and the text does not explain them.** 箕 Sieve · 壁 Wall · 翼 Wing · 軫 Cross-bar. The text asserts wind on these four; the commentaries assert it and do not derive it; the wind-association of 箕 is older than this book. Chapter 13 will refuse foreknowledge from spirits — which sits in tension with this passage rather than resolving it, and the Historian says so instead of picking a side.
- **Fire is not the only auxiliary, and the chapter says so.** 水可以絕，不可以奪 — water can cut off, but it cannot take. Fire and water are a pair here. Fire's distinction in the argument is not that it is unique but that it is the clearest case of a thing you cannot call back.
- **The fire is not the last word; the temper is.** 怒可以復喜 — wrath turns back to joy. 死者不可以復生 — the dead do not turn back. Anger is fire with the same failure mode: cheap to start, dependent on wind, impossible to recall.

**APPLY IT —** Before you start anything that goes on working after you stop, write down the condition it needs in order to land where you aimed — the wind — and then write down who is standing where it lands if that condition changes. If your answer to *why now* is that you were made small in front of people, that is not a condition. It is a mood, and moods reverse by morning. What you are about to do does not.

# CHAPTER 13

## A. IDENTITY

| | |
|---|---|
| **Number** | 13 of 13 — last in the received order |
| **Chinese title** | 用間 |
| **Pinyin** | *Yòng Jiàn* |
| **Literal** | employing · the gap (間 in this sense — spy, to drive a wedge into — is the **falling tone**, *jiàn*, as in 間諜 and 反間; the level tone *jiān* is the "between, interval" reading. Same graph, two readings: the pun is real, the tone is not the same one) |
| **Standard English** | "The Use of Spies" (Giles) · "Employing Spies" (Sawyer) |
| **Sharper alternative** | **THE PRICE OF KNOWING** |
| **Alt. runner-up** | "A Hundred Against a Thousand a Day" — the chapter is one arithmetic comparison, repeated until it becomes an accusation |

**Why the sharper title.** "The Use of Spies" sounds like tradecraft, and the chapter does supply tradecraft: five classes with definitions, the instruction to find and buy the enemy's own agent (因而利之，導而舍之), the list of people whose names you must obtain first — 守將、左右、謁者、門者、舍人, the commander's staff, his attendants, the men who announce visitors, the men on the gate, the household servants — and the flat, terrible rule that if the operation leaks, the spy and the man he told both die. That is method, and it is a mistake to say the chapter has none. What is *unusual* is that the method is wrapped inside an invoice. A hundred thousand men in the field cost a thousand in gold a day and idle seven hundred thousand households; the man who will tell you where the enemy is costs a title, a salary, and a hundred. The chapter then names the man who will not pay it — and the word it uses is not *foolish*. It is 不仁之至, the utmost inhumanity. Chapter 13 is where the coldest book in the canon files a moral charge, and the charge is about a budget line.

**Note that 間 is also a pun the site should honour.** The graph is a gap. All thirteen chapters have been about the space between what is and what is seen; the last chapter is named for that space, in the reading that means *to work it*, and tells you it can be bought.

## B. THE CORE STRATEGIC DICTUM

> **Foreknowledge cannot be prayed for, argued from precedent, or read off any instrument — it must be bought from a person, and a commander who finds that price too high has already decided which of his own men are cheaper.**

*(The third term is deliberately non-committal. 不可驗於度 is contested: 度 as measurement/calculation, or 度 as astrological degrees. "Read off any instrument" covers both without silently picking one, and the Historian names the dispute in the margin.)*

**Unpacking (≈200 words).** Chapter 13 makes three moves. First it re-states the cost of Chapter 2 as a *ratio*: years of ruinous mobilisation to decide a single day, against the trivial sum that would have told you how the day would go. Second it forecloses every non-human route to knowledge — not from ghosts and spirits, not by analogy from precedent, not by 度 — leaving only 必取於人, *it must be taken from men*. Third it classifies five kinds of men and identifies which one is structurally decisive: the **converted spy** (反間), the enemy's agent turned, because he is the only source that can recruit and validate all four others. All five run at once and 莫知其道 — the method cannot be fathomed: 神紀, the divine skein. (Note: 莫知其道 is normally read as *the enemy cannot fathom the method*. Compartmentalisation among your own spies is a commentators' gloss on it, not the sentence's claim, and we will not put it on screen as the text's.)

**Where the popular reading is wrong — two places.** (1) People read this chapter as *espionage advocacy*, a cool coda about tradecraft. It is an ethics chapter with tradecraft in it. Its rhetorical peak is an accusation of inhumanity levelled at a stingy ruler, and its logic is that intelligence is cheap **relative to lives**, not relative to money. (2) The 死間 is routinely translated into a hero — the brave agent who sacrifices himself. The lemma will not carry that either way; see C.4 below. The site does not launder the chapter and does not over-read it.

## D. THE VIGNETTE

### **FORTY THOUSAND CATTIES, UNCOUNTED**
**Xingyang, on the Chu–Han front · 204 BC**

Xingyang is a walled town on a grain road and Liu Bang is inside it with the wrong army.

Outside is Xiang Yu, who has never lost a battle. The corridor that feeds the town has been cut. Liu Bang has offered to give up everything west of the walls if he can walk out with his life, and Xiang Yu was inclined — genuinely inclined — to take it. What stopped him was a man of about seventy-four at his shoulder saying the Han are easy now and will not be easy later.

Fan Zeng. Xiang Yu calls him 亞父, second father. He was seventy when he came to Xiang Liang six years ago and he is the only person in the Chu camp who tells Xiang Yu things he does not want to hear, and he has done it since Hongmen, where he tried to have Liu Bang killed over dinner and was overruled.

So Liu Bang, in a town that is going to fall, asks the advisor his own generals distrust most.

Chen Ping has been in Han service a year — since 205, since he crossed at Xiuwu — and in that year Liu Bang's own generals have formally accused him of taking bribes and of sleeping with his brother's wife, and his recommender answered the charge by saying he had not brought a saint, he had brought a man who could win. Chen Ping tells Liu Bang a small truth about the enemy: *Xiang Yu is courteous, he is generous with his manners, and he is stingy with land. He has perhaps five men who matter — Fan Zeng, Zhongli Mei, Long Ju, Zhou Yin — and every one of them has done more for him than he has been paid for. He is also, by temperament, a man who suspects.* Give me gold, Chen Ping says. Give me a great deal of gold and do not ask me for the accounts.

Liu Bang has forty thousand catties brought up out of the treasury of a besieged town. No scribe is called. No tally goes onto a slip. The chests are opened where they stand and Chen Ping takes the weight of it away unweighed, and the one line the record keeps of the transaction is that Liu Bang let him do as he liked and did not ask what went out or came in.

The gold goes through the lines and becomes rumour, which is what gold is for. In the Chu camp men begin to hear that Zhongli Mei and the rest have won everything and been given nothing, and mean to come to terms with Han and split Chu between them. The rumours are not aimed at the generals. They are aimed at the one listener guaranteed to be receptive.

Xiang Yu, who suspects, sends an envoy into Xingyang.

What follows takes about ninety seconds. The envoy is received with the full banquet — the 太牢, ox and sheep and pig, the ceremonial maximum, carried in. Then the host performs astonishment: *I thought you were the Second Father's man. You are King Xiang's.* The dishes are taken out again. Coarse food is brought in. The envoy is left in the room with it.

He rides back and reports the insult, as he must. And Xiang Yu — who has been told nothing, only *shown* a shape — completes the picture out of his own material, and begins to strip Fan Zeng of authority.

The old man understands immediately and does not argue. *The empire's business is settled. Your Majesty may handle it himself. I ask leave for my bones, to go home to the ranks.* He is granted it.

The cart goes out through the Chu gate in the morning with one old man in it, and nobody stops it, and nobody is sent after it, and the camp behind it is quiet. He does not reach Pengcheng. An abscess opens on his back on the road and he dies of it in transit, and Xiang Yu is left with his suspicions and no one at his shoulder to correct them.

**Record vs. legend.** *Recorded* (Shiji ch. 56 陳丞相世家, ch. 7 項羽本紀, ch. 8 高祖本紀; Han Shu parallels): Fan Zeng's age of seventy when he joined Xiang Liang, and his standing as 亞父 from Hongmen onward; Chen Ping's crossing to Han in 205 and the accusations against him with Wei Wuzhi's defence; the grant of forty thousand catties of gold with no accounting of what went in or out; the counter-intelligence campaign naming Fan Zeng, Zhongli Mei, Long Ju and Zhou Yin; the envoy, the 太牢 and the substituted coarse meal; Fan Zeng's loss of authority, his request for his bones, and his death of a back abscess on the road.

*Attribution note the Historian should make:* the banquet is narrated in **both** Shiji 7 and Shiji 56, with the performance staged by the Han side and the stratagem credited to Chen Ping; the packet's earlier caveat that Sima Qian admits Chen Ping's stratagems were secret (奇計或頗祕，世莫能聞也) applies to the **unnamed remainder** of the six 奇計, not to this one, which is narrated twice. It is Sima Qian's account of a 反間 operation, written a century later with a novelist's ear for a scene. Treat it as the best available account. Do not treat it as a transcript, and do not claim it is a reconstruction of something he says he did not have.

*Also the packet's own dramatisation, and marked as such:* "beaten by a plate of food" is our line, not the record's. The record gives a dismissal and a carbuncle on the road, in that order, and lets the reader draw the arrow.

*Removed from this vignette, and where it went:* the Ji Xin decoy — the yellow-canopied carriage out the east gate at night, two thousand women in armour, Liu Bang away west to Chenggao, Ji Xin burned alive when the carriage is opened. It is recorded (Shiji 7 and 8; Shiji 56 attaches the women's sortie to Chen Ping) and it is a magnificent episode and it does not belong here. It is a different operation, it fast-forwards the scene out of the room, and it invites exactly the misreading the Historian then has to spend a paragraph undoing: **Ji Xin is not a 死間.** He is a decoy who knows precisely what he is doing and volunteers for it; the lemma at C.4 describes manufactured falsehood reaching the enemy through a channel, and does not describe him. Give Ji Xin twelve seconds in the Historian's margin as an inventory citation with that distinction attached, and keep him out of the vignette.

*And do not write the aftermath as arithmetic:* Xiang Yu died in 202 at Gaixia and then at the Wu river, of encirclement, of Han Xin's manoeuvre, of logistics and of four years. He was not "out-purchased." What the gold bought was one advisor's removal in 204, and the honest claim is that specific, and it is enough.

## E. VISUAL CUE

**ENVIRONMENT — a dark tent at midnight, and the deliberate bracket with Chapter 1.**

Chapter 1 is an interior. It is the ancestral counting hall before dawn: rammed earth, bronze standing lamps, a low mat, a door-sized sheet of paper with nothing on it, one raking key at ~8°, cold, expansive, empty. Chapter 8 is the bare sheet itself under a raking light, with no terrain, no army and no sky. **Chapter 13 must not repeat either, and the previous draft of this packet claimed uniqueness it did not have.** What it should do instead is *close the bracket on Chapter 1 and be its physical opposite*: the count at the beginning, the invoice at the end; a cold empty hall at dawn, and now a hot small tent at midnight.

So: a field tent, oiled silk, heavily textured, dark-lacquered frame, the walls close enough to be inside the 40mm's field on three sides. A single low table. A lamp on it. A stack of bamboo slips bound with cord, lying flat — the same slips the HUD has been made of for twelve chapters, now seen at arm's length instead of at the edge of the frame. **Behind and around the table there is no reserved white**: there is unlit oiled silk, warm-dark, with the weave reading. The only paper in the shot is the paper that has been *bought* — the slips, the letters, the ledger. Chapter 1 gave the reader an enormous clean sheet and asked what they would spend. Chapter 13 gives them no sheet at all and shows them what a sheet costs.

**DOMINANT INK BEHAVIOUR — ink that dried before you got here.** This is the inversion the whole build has been saving. Everywhere else the reader watches ink go down wet and bloom. Here every mark is *already* at M = 0 on arrival: hard-edged, tide-lined, oxidised, finished. Nothing blooms in Chapter 13, because information is the one substance that arrives having already happened. Two specific behaviours:

1. **Verso bleed.** Writing appears on the *back* of a slip — a mark whose source is on the other side of the material, soaked through and mirrored. The reader reads reversed characters. This is the only place in the site where ink is seen from the wrong side, and it is the chapter's whole idea made physical. Spec below; it is not a metaphor and the build must be able to produce it.
2. **The great drying.** On the reader's action, accumulated fog held at M 0.55–0.70 since Chapter 1 collapses toward M = 0 in one continuous 6-second pass. Nothing new is drawn. What changes is that the ink already there becomes *legible*. Per the manifesto: drying should feel like power, and like a door closing on someone else. **How much of the scroll dries depends on which agent the reader ran** — see §F. Both branches dry something. Neither branch withholds the pass.

**PARTICLE SYSTEM — none, and the absence is deliberate.** No embers (Chapter 12 spent them), no dust, no smoke, no motes in the lamplight. Chapter 8 is also particle-free, and this packet is not going to pretend otherwise; what is distinct here is the *reason*. Chapter 8's stillness is the room going quiet around a sheet. Chapter 13's is that nothing in this tent is in motion because everything in it has already happened somewhere else. The only thing that moves besides the reader's hands is **gold leaf, and it does not float** — per the Ink Law, no floating ink, no particle smoke, and gold that blooms is a bug. Gold appears as five discrete flakes laid on the table by hand, one per class of agent, thin and dry, catching the lamp at `#E8CE72` for two frames as each is set down. Five flakes is the entire particle budget of the chapter. They are the hundred in gold, and they are physically dwarfed by the slips they buy.

**LIGHTING — one lamp, in frame, occludable, and it transmits.** Not the only interior light source in the site (Chapter 1 has bronze standing lamps) — the distinction that matters is that this one is a **practical the action can block**, and that it is the only light in the project used *transmissively*: the money shot depends on a bamboo slip being thin enough to backlight. Contrast is highest in the site — deepest ink `#0B0F0E` against the lit face of a slip — because Chapter 13 is where ambiguity stops being available. There is no fog inside the tent to soften anything, and that is the point of the lighting design.

**CAMERA — 40mm, the Historian's lens, held level and human-scaled.** The only chapter shot predominantly in the Historian's jurisdiction, because here the site's textual honesty and its argument are the same thing: the received thirteen chapters are the recension we read, the 1972 Yinqueshan Han-tomb slips are why our progress scroll is bamboo, and the object being filmed *is* the artefact. The move is a slow crane **down** — from a level 40mm read of the table, descending until the lens is nearly at table height and the slips stand up like a palisade between the reader and the lamp. Chapter 6 also cranes down, continuously, for a whole chapter; the difference is that Chapter 6's crane never stops because water never stops, and this one stops dead at table height and stays there. One cut, on reader action, to 85mm for the named factor: a single slip, held. **180mm is not used in this chapter** — for the first time the enemy has no distance to be held at, because you are holding his handwriting. (Chapter 8 also declines 180mm; there the reason is that the enemy is not in the chapter at all. Here he is in the room.)

**THE ONE IMAGE THEY REMEMBER.** *A single bamboo slip lifted into the lamp until the light comes through it from behind — and the enemy's characters, soaked through the grain, read backwards.* Held for four seconds. Then the reader notices the hand holding it up to the light is theirs, in their own sleeve, and that the ink on the cuff is a deposit they made in Chapter 6 and cannot wash out. ### **PIPELINE ADDENDUM — CH.13** *(binding on the build; extends `direction-tech.md` §5)*

**`VersoBleedMaterial` — the slip seen from the wrong side.** `THREE.ShaderMaterial`, `side: THREE.DoubleSide`, on the existing instanced slip meshes. It rides Shader #4's MSDF path with one extra sampler; **no new pass, no new render target.**

Uniforms: `sampler2D uFrontSDF; sampler2D uBambooFibre; float uSlipThickness; vec3 uLightPos;`

```glsl
vec2  uvF   = gl_FrontFacing ? vUv : vec2(1.0 - vUv.x, vUv.y);   // mirror on the back face
float sd    = texture(uFrontSDF, uvF).r;
float ink   = 1.0 - smoothstep(-fwidth(sd), fwidth(sd), sd - 0.5);  // analytic AA, per doctrine
float fib   = texture(uBambooFibre, vUv * vec2(1.0, 18.0)).r;       // grain runs the slip's length
float trans = exp(-uSlipThickness * 4.2 * mix(0.6, 1.4, fib));      // fibrous attenuation
float back  = float(!gl_FrontFacing);
float d     = mix(ink, ink * trans, back);                          // back face never brighter
```

Two rules keep it inside the Ink Law. The back face is **strictly attenuated** — a verso mark can never out-darken its recto, and it never lightens the substrate. And when a slip is set down, its `d` stamps into the ink field through Shader #1's `uDeposits`, exactly as glyphs do, so a verso mark is a real deposit on the reader's scroll and not a decal on a mesh.

**The great drying — one uniform, one tween, no scene reloads.** Per `direction-tech.md` §1 the `<InkField />` singleton never unmounts and owns D/M/S/A for the whole read; only `<ActiveChapter/>` unmounts. There is therefore nothing to coordinate across twelve viewports: **the scroll is one resident 2048² field, and the drying is one uniform crossing it.**

Add `uniform float uGlobalDrying;` (default 0) and `uniform sampler2D uDryMask;` to **Shader #1** and **Shader #7**. On commit:

```js
gsap.to(u.uGlobalDrying, { value: 1.0, duration: 6.0, ease: 'power2.out' });
```

```glsl
// #1, after the normal M-decay step:
float mask = texture(uDryMask, uv).r;              // 1.0 everywhere for 反間; enemy-side only for 死間
M = M * (1.0 - uGlobalDrying * mask);
// tide-line deposit stays in the loop, so the drying WRITES scars rather than erasing anything.
// D is untouched: nothing lightens, we only stop hiding it.

// #7 (height fog), M is already read from the ink field:
float effM = texture(uInk, uv).g * (1.0 - uGlobalDrying * mask);
fog = 1.0 - exp(-h(z) * (0.22 + 0.9 * effM));
```

`uDryMask` is a 256² R8 authored per branch and uploaded once at commit. Because fog and moisture are the same variable by design, one tween resolves the fog of war and the wet paper together, everywhere, without a single scene-graph touch — and the "whole site becomes legible" claim is then literally true of the field the reader is handed at the ending.

## F. TACTIC DECISION NODE — "HIS MAN, IN YOUR TENT"

**Node phase spec:** LOAD → MARK → BLOOM → DRY → READ. Aperture tightens to 2.76:1 at LOAD. Analysis is legal only at M ≈ 0. Branches do not reconverge.

**Design note, and a correction to the previous draft.** The earlier version of this node offered *execute him* against *turn him*, which is not a decision — the chapter has just spent four hundred characters arguing that the turned man is the hinge, and no attentive reader picks the axe. Worse, it withheld the great drying from Option A, which makes the site's climax a reward for the correct answer: **fake agency, and the manifesto names it as such.** The node now runs the two doctrines the chapter actually places side by side, both of them legal, both of them expensive, both of them resolving.

**THE SITUATION — the Commander (no voice; a brush loading, a slip set down, one drum):**

> You caught his agent four days ago. He has been eating your rice and he has told you nothing, which is the only honest thing anyone in this camp has done this week.
> The gold is on the table, and it is not only gold: it is rank, and a salary, and a place in this state. You will be asked where all three went.
> **He is more use to you pointed back at the man who sent him than he is in your ground. The question is what you point him at — the truth, or a thing you have made.**

**OPTION A — 反間. Buy him, and send him back as himself.** *(因而利之，導而舍之)*
Pay him past the point of comfort. Give him one real thing so his masters keep him in the room. He recruits your local, names your inward man, validates your survivor. You get the whole apparatus and you get it working continuously — and you have opened a channel you will never again be certain which way is being used.

**OPTION B — 死間. Send him back carrying something you wrote.** *(為誑事於外)*
Manufacture the matter. Let it reach him the way things reach a man who is listening. He carries it across intact and it is believed for exactly as long as it takes to be tested. You have bought one specific enemy decision and nothing else — and the channel closes behind him, hard, in the manner the chapter's name implies and the lemma declines to spell out.

*(Both are the chapter's own instruments. Neither is the safe one. The discriminator is whether the reader wants a supply of knowledge or a single result, and what they are prepared to have their name on.)*

**SIMULATED VISUAL CONSEQUENCE — A. Ink cost +0.11.**
The five gold flakes go down, one at a time, with a slip set down between each. Then the great drying fires **at full mask**: M collapses toward 0 across the entire accumulated scroll — twelve chapters, every deposit and tide line the reader has made — in one 6-second pass, and the whole sheet becomes legible at once. It is the largest single change of state in the build and it is the most expensive beat in Act IV, because knowledge is not free and this build refuses to pretend it is. Two consequences the reader will not like. **(1)** In the newly dry paper, three of their own earlier decisions turn out to have been made on false readings of ground that was never there — the marks stay, monotonic, unretractable; they are simply now understood. **(2)** A single jade thread `#5C9A86` runs from the tent, out past the frame edge, toward the enemy — **with an arrowhead at both ends.** Jade means cognition only, never a thing in the world, and the two arrowheads are the honest rendering of a channel you did not build and do not own. Drum, and it commits.

**SIMULATED VISUAL CONSEQUENCE — B. Ink cost +0.03.**
Cheaper, and the cheapness is the accusation. The drying fires — but `uDryMask` covers **the enemy's ground only**. Over there, everything resolves: his dispositions, his order of march, the decision he is about to take, all of it hard-edged and readable, because you wrote the premise he is about to act on and you therefore know the conclusion. Turn the head and your own eleven chapters are exactly as fogged as they were, at M 0.62, because **a lie you planted tells you nothing about yourself.** The jade thread runs out one-way, no return arrowhead. And at the far end of it, past the frame edge, **one drop of vermilion** — `#C1352B`, ~2.5× viscosity, beading proud of the paper, not spreading, not blending, permanent, and small. It is not your seal and you did not press it. Somebody else did, on your material, at a time of their choosing. Metal, twice. Then the Text, which per doctrine is a hole in the mix.

**THE HISTORICAL VERDICT (revealed at M ≈ 0).**

Sun Tzu is unambiguous and slightly indecent about it: 五間之事，主必知之，知之必在於反間，故反間不可不厚也 — everything runs through the turned man, so there is no overpaying him. He goes further than comfort allows: 三軍之事，莫親於間，賞莫厚於間 — of the whole army, none is closer to you than the spy, and none is paid more. Read that against Chapter 2's soldiers eating their own draught animals, and notice that Sun Tzu has just told you the most trusted, best-compensated person in your army is a traitor by trade. The book does not apologise for this and neither will we.

**On the option the chapter never offers.** Killing the caught agent is not one of the five instruments, and the only executions the chapter mandates are for a leak: 間事未發而先聞者，間與所告者皆死 — if the operation is known before it moves, the spy and the man he told both die. The chapter's interest in a captured enemy agent is entirely acquisitive. That is why it is not on the table here.

**A caution on the two famous examples, which the Historian must state rather than smooth over.** Chen Ping at Xingyang in 204 BC and Fan Ju's agents at Changping in 260 BC are both called 反間 by the *Shiji*, and they are 反間 in that text's broader sense: **discord bought with gold.** Neither is the narrow thing this chapter defines — catching the enemy's own agent and running him back. At Changping the money was a thousand in gold spent inside Zhao on a rumour with two halves, and the second half is the one everyone forgets: that Qin feared nobody but the young Lord of Mafu, *and that Lian Po was easy to handle and about to surrender anyway.* Zhao replaced the general who was winning by not fighting. (Shiji 73 and 81.) At Xingyang the money was forty thousand catties, unaudited, and it cost Xiang Yu the only advisor who told him the truth (Shiji 7 and 56). **The gold did not win either battle. It selected the enemy's commander, and it removed the enemy's counsel, which is cheaper.**

**And what it is not.** Xiang Yu does not belong in Option A's slot in any form. He did not catch an agent and choose wrongly about him; he was the *target* of an operation and he discarded his own man on the strength of a staged dinner. He is what the node costs somebody, not a way of playing it.

**The chapter's last card, in gold, on dry paper, over the reader's whole scroll:** 此兵之要，三軍之所恃而動也 — *this is the essential of war, the thing the whole army relies on in order to move.* Twelve chapters have been the preface. *(The Historian adds, under the hairline: this is the close of the received recension, and the received order is not the only one the manuscript evidence allows. See Packet Notes.)*

## G. CODEX MODE ENTRY

*(16:9, orthographic, Ink Law frozen. Text + Historian. This entry is the last one and is permitted to know it.)*

**用間 · 13 · THE PRICE OF KNOWING**

> **DICTUM —** Foreknowledge cannot be prayed for, argued from precedent, or read off any instrument — it must be bought from a person, and a commander who finds that price too high has already decided which of his own men are cheaper.

- **Five classes, one hinge.** 鄉間 *xiāng jiàn* local · 內間 *nèi jiàn* inward · 反間 *fǎn jiàn* **converted** · 死間 *sǐ jiàn* doomed · 生間 *shēng jiàn* surviving. Four of them are recruited, validated and run by the third. Everything you know, you know because you turned one of his — so the converted spy is not a resource, he is the aperture. *(The graph 間 is the falling tone here. Same character as the gap; different reading.)*
- **There is method, and it is specific.** Find his agent, make it worth his while, guide him, let him go. Get the names first — the commander, his attendants, the men who announce visitors, the men on the gate, the household staff. And the rule with no comfort in it: if the business is known before it moves, the spy and the man he told both die.
- **The arithmetic is the argument.** A hundred thousand men in the field: a thousand in gold a day, seven hundred thousand households idled. A man who tells you what is on the other side of the hill: a title, a salary, a hundred. Sun Tzu does not call refusing that trade *unwise*. He calls it 不仁之至 — the utmost inhumanity. *(金 is a unit of metal whose exact identity in pre-Qin usage is unsettled; we render it "gold" consistently and say so. Giles converted it to "ounces of silver" throughout and we do not follow him.)*
- **The fourth class is not a hero, and it is not a confession either.** 死間者，為誑事於外，令吾間知之，而傳於敵間也 — manufacture the deception outside, let our own agent learn it, and he carries it to theirs. The clause says nothing about the man being unwitting, being caught, or dying. The death is in the name and in the commentators, who disagree. Where the book is cruel we cite it and let it stand; where the cruelty is the commentary's, we say whose it is.

**APPLY IT —** Take the thing you have been guessing at for months and price the guessing: the hours, the rework, the people carrying the error downstream of you. Then price the alternative — asking the one person who already knows, and paying whatever they actually want for it. If the second number is smaller and you have still not paid it, work out what the difference is buying. It is usually the right to keep believing you did not need to ask, and it is not you who pays for that.

**Open textual questions the Historian must voice, not paper over.**
1. **火隊** — the fifth fire. Reading unsettled (supply routes / tunnels / fire dropped on formations). Say so on screen. Note that if it means formations, the enumeration begins and ends with men, which is why we do not present it as a ranking.
2. **鄉間 vs 因間** — edition split on the first class of spy; the Yinqueshan slips are damaged at the graph. Say so on screen.
3. **修其功 / 費留** — glosses contested. Say so on screen.
4. **驗於度** — measurement, or astrological degrees. Contested, and consequential, because the second reading puts Ch13 in direct tension with Ch12's mansions. The dictum is worded so it does not silently pick. Say so on screen.
5. **死間** — the lemma does not contain the death, the capture, or the unwittingness; those come from the chapter's name and from commentators who disagree with each other. Quote the clause; attribute the reading.
6. **The transmission, stated correctly.** The received thirteen chapters are a **recension**, already circulating in the early Han, and the 1972 Yinqueshan Han-tomb bamboo slips from Linyi are the oldest substantial witness to it — which is why our progress scroll is bamboo and why the HUD is an artefact, not a skin. **Do not call the received text a "reconstruction."** The slips confirm a thirteen-chapter Sunzi; they are not the source from which it was rebuilt. They also carry variants, several additional Sunzi-related 篇 outside the thirteen, and a chapter-directory tablet whose reconstructions do not all place 用間 last — some put 火攻 after it. This matters directly to §C's last line: 此兵之要 closes the **received** book, and calling it "the book's final sentence" without that qualification is exactly the kind of silent choice this site exists not to make. This is Chapter 13's own subject matter and belongs *in* Chapter 13, where a chapter about the reliability of sources is the right place to admit the reliability of ours. **Confidence: high** on the 13-chapter early-Han recension and on the extra 篇; **medium** on any specific variant reading or directory order, because the slips are fragmentary and scholars differ.
