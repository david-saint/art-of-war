## ACT-LEVEL NOTE (applies to all three chapters)

These three chapters open **Act III**, the longest act, whose ink ceiling is **mean page density 0.42**. Chapters 7–9 must therefore spend roughly the *first third* of that budget and no more: entering Chapter 10 the sheet should read as heavily worked but still hold reserved white in the upper register. Concretely — **飛白 (broken dry-brush) is introduced in Chapter 7 and never leaves the site again**, and the first *permanent* tide lines (水痕) of the whole experience are deposited by Chapter 7's forced-march node. Act III is where the reader stops being clever.

**Textual honesty, for the Historian.** The received thirteen-chapter *Sunzi* is a reconstruction. The Yinqueshan (銀雀山) Han-tomb bamboo slips at Linyi, Shandong — discovered and excavated in April 1972, Tomb 1 opened on 10 April, Tomb 2 on 18 April, salvage continuing through the month — are dated to the early Western Han. They attest **all thirteen chapter titles**, and where the slips survive (roughly a third of the received book's length) they carry a text close to the transmitted recension *but with real variants*; they also carry five further *Sunzi*-affiliated texts not in the received book at all. Chapter 8 is both the most visibly disordered chapter in the received book and among the worst-preserved sections in the slips (see §B8). The Historian says this out loud once, in Chapter 8, and does not repeat it.

# CHAPTER 7

## A. IDENTITY

| | |
|---|---|
| **Number** | 7 of 13 |
| **Chinese title** | 軍爭 |
| **Pinyin** | *Jūn zhēng* |
| **Literal** | "the army's contention" / "contending [with the army] for advantage" |
| **Standard English title** | *Manœuvring* (Giles, Luzac, 1910 — the American reprints respell it *Maneuvering*) — also rendered *Manoeuvring*, *Armed Contest*, *Military Combat* |
| **Sharper alternative (ours)** | **THE ARITHMETIC OF ARRIVING FIRST** |

*Why the alternative.* "Manœuvring" imports a romance of cleverness that the chapter does not contain. 軍爭 is a contest for a *position*, decided by who gets there in usable condition, and the chapter's centre of gravity is a costing sheet: march a hundred li and you arrive with one man in ten. The title should say *arithmetic*, because the chapter's real content is a rate of loss.

---

## B. THE CORE STRATEGIC DICTUM

> **The longer road, taken first, arrives first — and every hour you buy with speed is paid for in men who never arrive at all.**

**Unpacking (c. 190 words).** 以迂為直 is not a mystique of indirectness, but it is also not pure timekeeping: the received sentence continues 故迂其途，而誘之以利，後人發，先人至 — *so make his road circuitous, bait him with advantage, set out after him and arrive before him.* Deception is load-bearing in the mechanism. What is distinctive is that Sun Tzu then does something almost no strategic writer does — he immediately prices it. Force the pace a hundred li and your three commanders are taken and one man in ten arrives; fifty li and you lose your senior commander and half the force; thirty li and two-thirds get there. Then, three times in one breath: an army without its baggage train, without grain, without stockpiles, is dead. The chapter is a warning wearing a maxim's clothes.

**Where the popular reading is wrong.** 風林火山 — "swift as wind, still as forest, fierce as fire, immovable as mountain" — is quoted as a war cry. It is not one. It is a *gear list*: four tempos a single force must be able to change between, and the passage continues with two more terms (難知如陰、動如雷霆) that the famous version amputates. The four-character banner is not a Chinese reading of Sunzi at all: later Japanese tradition, first described in the early-Edo *Kōyō Gunkan* (甲陽軍鑑, completed 1616), attaches the four phrases to Takeda Shingen, and the compressed four-character nickname *Fūrinkazan* is popularly traced to Inoue Yasushi's 1953 novel — it is **not** a demonstrated sixteenth-century flag. Per our refusal list it does not enter this world as iconography; only as text doing work. Second error: readers take Chapter 7 as advocacy of speed, and correct themselves too far in the other direction. The chapter contains 其疾如風 and 後人發，先人至 and 兵以詐立; it **invoices** forced marches, it does not recant speed.

## D. THE VIGNETTE

### **FIVE HUNDRED LI OF NO ROAD**
**207 CE, the twelfth year of Jian'an · fifth month at Wuzhong, seventh month on the drowned coast, eighth month at White Wolf Mountain · the abandoned Lulong track, beyond the northeast frontier of Han**
**Cao Cao 曹操 · Guo Jia 郭嘉 · Tian Chou 田疇 · Zhang Liao 張遼 · Tadun 蹋頓, chanyu of the Liaoxi Wuhuan and the strongest of the three-commandery chieftains**

The plan is old by the time the water arrives. Before the column ever reaches the frontier Guo Jia has already made the argument that will be quoted for two thousand years, and he makes it as a logistics observation, not a poem: *speed is the essence of war* — 兵貴神速. We are striking at a thousand li. The baggage is heavy and will not let us take the advantage. And if he hears us coming, he will be ready. Leave the baggage. Light troops, double marches, come out where he is not looking.

Leave the baggage. Sun Tzu's own text says it three times in one breath — no train, no grain, no stores, dead. Cao Cao is going to do it anyway, and the difference between this and stupidity is not one man's nerve. It is two men's information.

The army reaches Wuzhong in the fifth month. In the seventh month the rain comes down and the coast road ceases to exist. Cao Cao stands at the edge of a trough of grey soup and watches a cart go to its axle. Tian Chou, who is local, puts the problem in one sentence: in summer and autumn this road always floods — **淺不通車馬，深不載舟船** — where it is shallow it will not pass carts and horses, where it is deep it will not float boats. It has been useless for a long time. And the Wuhuan are holding the passes on the far side of it anyway.

Then he offers the other road. There was a Han garrison at Pinggang once, and a road to it through the Lulong defile; it has been broken and cut for the better part of two hundred years, but a footpath survives.

So Cao Cao performs a departure. He takes the army back down the coast, and by the water at the roadside he has large wooden boards set up: *the summer heat is on us, the roads are impassable, we will come again in autumn or winter.* The enemy's scouts find the signs and report that the Han army has gone home. Then the column turns inland, quietly, and is not seen again.

They cut down ridges and fill in ravines for five hundred li. There is no road; they are making one and abandoning it behind them. They pass Baitan, they pass Pinggang, they cross open Xianbei country going east. Tian Chou walks in front. The column is stripped to what a man carries. There is no cart to sit on when the legs go, and no soup at the end of the day, and the men who fall behind are not deserters, they are just men who have stopped.

Two hundred li out from Liucheng, in the eighth month, the enemy finally sees them.

Tadun comes up fast with the Yuan brothers and tens of thousands of horse, and Cao Cao meets him at White Wolf Mountain with his own baggage still strung out somewhere behind him and not enough of his men in armour. His officers are frightened, and it is a reasonable thing to be. Cao Cao rides up the high ground and looks down and sees the one fact the whole march was a wager on: 望虜陣不整 — *the enemy's line is not dressed.* They have come at speed too, and they have arrived the way arriving at speed makes you arrive.

Zhang Liao asks for the attack. Cao Cao takes his own command banner — 麾 — out of his hand and gives it to him.

Tadun is cut down, and the named kings under him with him, and something over two hundred thousand people, Hu and Han, come in and submit.

Then the bill. The return is worse than the campaign: it turns cold and it turns dry, and there are two hundred li with no water at all. The grain is gone, so they kill several thousand horses for food, and they dig more than thirty *zhang* into the ground before the pits give water. Guo Jia does not recover from the road. He falls seriously ill on the way back from Liucheng and dies at thirty-eight.

Back inside the frontier, Cao Cao has a list drawn up of every officer who had argued against the campaign. They are summoned, and nobody knows why, and men who advised against a victory have reason to be careful. He rewards all of them, richly — 皆厚賞之 — and tells them what the reward is for: *I went out on a risk and I got away with it. Heaven was on my side. It cannot be made a rule. Your advice was the safe calculation, so I am paying for it — and next time, do not find it hard to speak.*

That is the chapter. Not the speed. The bill, itemised, paid, and read aloud afterwards.

---

**Record vs. legend, by source layer.**

*Chen Shou's main text, Sanguozhi Wei 1 (Annals of Emperor Wu, Jian'an 12):* arrival at Wuzhong in the fifth month; the great flood in the seventh month and the coast road closed; the march through Lulong, the cutting of ridges and filling of ravines for five hundred li past Baitan and Pinggang; White Wolf Mountain in the eighth month; Cao Cao ascending the height and seeing the enemy line undressed (望虜陣不整); Zhang Liao sent as vanguard; Tadun and the named kings killed; over 200,000 Hu and Han submitting.

*Chen Shou's main text, Sanguozhi Wei 11 (Tian Chou):* Tian Chou's guidance; the 淺不通車馬，深不載舟船 assessment; **and the wooden notice boards** — 乃引軍還，而署大木表於水側路傍 — which are Chen Shou's own narrative, not a commentary layer. The withdrawal is the ruse; the placards are left where the enemy's mounted scouts (候騎) will find them, and they do.

*Chen Shou's main text, Sanguozhi Wei 14 (Guo Jia):* the 兵貴神速 argument, given as the campaign is being decided rather than on the flooded coast; Guo Jia falling gravely ill on the return from Liucheng (自柳城還，疾篤) and dying at thirty-eight.

*Attribution the sources do not agree on:* **who killed Tadun.** Wei 1 names Zhang Liao as vanguard and leaves the killing unattributed to any individual. Wei 17 (Zhang Liao) credits Zhang Liao and supplies the detail of Cao Cao handing over his own 麾. Wei 9 (Cao Chun) credits the Tiger and Leopard cavalry under Cao Chun. Chen Shou records both attributions in different biographies and we do not collapse them.

*Pei Songzhi's fifth-century annotation on Wei 1, quoting the* Cao Man zhuan 曹瞞傳 *(a hostile, Wu-sourced anecdote collection):* the waterless two hundred li on the return, the several thousand horses eaten, the wells dug 30-plus *zhang*, and the whole reward scene with Cao Cao's speech. These are the most quotable lines in the vignette and they are the ones furthest from Chen Shou. The Historian marks them on screen.

*Sequenced narrative:* Zizhi Tongjian 65.

*Legend:* the idea, common in retellings, that Cao Cao foresaw the victory. Even the friendliest version of his recorded verdict is that he gambled and was lucky. Also *not* recorded: any duration for the fight at White Wolf Mountain, and any container for the Yuan brothers' heads (Gongsun Kang later sent them; 傳其首 says nothing about a box). The count of years since Guandu is our arithmetic and we do not put it in a source's mouth.

## F. TACTIC DECISION NODE — **"THE ROAD IS OUT"**

**Situation (Commander, 行草 on the operative term, tracked small-caps jade):**
> The coast road is under water and will be for a month. The prize is two hundred and fifty miles beyond it and it will not wait. There is a second road, broken for two centuries, that a local swears still carries a footpath — and your baggage train cannot use it.

**OPTION A — 卷甲而趨. Strip to what a man carries. Take the broken road. Double marches, day and night.**

*Simulated visual consequence:* The letterbox snaps to **2.76:1**. Time compresses; the column-stroke is laid at 4× brush velocity and **feibai initiates within the first second** — deposition density decays along the path, the stroke striates, then breaks into dry flecks. The rear of the march is *never deposited*: that ground stays raw substrate, unwetted from the beginning, and the reader's scroll keeps it that way permanently. What arrives is a small, very dense head — the highest local D the site has yet produced — landing on ground the enemy has not had time to occupy. A tide line crystallises where the wet section of the stroke pinned and stopped, and never leaves. Ink cost: **low total area, brutal local density, a permanent unmarked scar the reader will read as men.**

**OPTION B — 輜重俱行. Keep the train. Take the long road whole.**

*Simulated visual consequence:* No feibai. The column stays a continuous 濃墨 stroke at uniform width, and the reader watches it lay down cleanly for nine seconds — which is nine seconds of **drying**. M falls to ~0.1 across the whole objective *while you are still travelling*. When your stroke finally reaches the ground, the ground is dry, and Ink Law clause 1 does the rest: dry paper takes a hard, final edge. Your mark cannot bleed, cannot spread, cannot reach or overrun the enemy's mark already sitting there inside its own crystallised tide line. It simply stops where the brush stops. You arrive intact, and intact is a thing you now watch fail to matter. Ink cost: **high total area, no local dominance, a wide grey deposit that pushes the act's mean toward its ceiling for nothing.**

**HISTORICAL VERDICT (revealed only at M ≈ 0):**
Sun Tzu **refuses to resolve this**, and the refusal is the chapter. In the space of forty characters he says that racing a hundred li costs you your commanders and nine-tenths of your force, *and* that an army without baggage, grain and stores is destroyed. Both options are quoted from him. What he actually supplies is not a preference but a rate: know what the hurry costs before you buy it.

**What happened when a real commander chose A.** Cao Cao, 207 CE, on Guo Jia's argument and Tian Chou's road — neither man dispensable, and the campaign works only because both were listened to — took the abandoned Lulong track with light troops and no train and caught the Wuhuan with their line undressed at White Wolf Mountain in the eighth month. Then he came back through two hundred li of dry country killing horses for food, buried Guo Jia, and rewarded every officer who had told him not to go, saying that it had worked and that it must never be made a rule. *(The return and the reward speech come to us through Pei Songzhi quoting the* Cao Man zhuan*, not through Chen Shou. The Historian says so at the moment the line lands.)*

*Node cost to the sheet: A leaves a permanent unmarked break in the reader's scroll; B leaves a permanent grey field. Neither branch reconverges. (Manifesto §3, §8.)*

## G. CODEX MODE ENTRY — CH. 7 軍爭

**DICTUM** — The longer road, taken first, arrives first; and speed is bought with men who never arrive.

- **以迂為直** is a claim about *time* and *bait* together: you lengthen his road by putting something he wants at the end of the other one, then leave after him and arrive before him.
- **The chapter prices itself.** 100 li → one man in ten and your three commanders taken. 50 li → half. 30 li → two-thirds. No other classic invoices its own advice this precisely.
- **風林火山 is a gearbox, not a battle cry** — and the received text has six terms, not four. 難知如陰 means *unreadable*, not *legible*. The four-character banner is a later Japanese device from the *Kōyō Gunkan* tradition, nicknamed in the twentieth century.

**APPLY IT** — Before you commit to a pace you have not set yourself, write the list of what will be left on the road: the specific stores, the specific people, the specific things that will not arrive when you do. If the list cannot be written, speed was not chosen. Counting was simply refused.

# CHAPTER 8

## A. IDENTITY

| | |
|---|---|
| **Number** | 8 of 13 |
| **Chinese title** | 九變 |
| **Pinyin** | *Jiǔ biàn* |
| **Literal** | "the nine variations / nine changes" |
| **Standard English title** | *Variation in Tactics* (Giles) — also *The Nine Variables*, *Adaptations*, *Nine Contingencies* |
| **Sharper alternative (ours)** | **THE GENERAL'S FIVE HANDLES** |

*Why the alternative.* "Variation in Tactics" is a title for the chapter's first half; the chapter's *argument* lands in its last twenty-eight characters, where Sun Tzu stops discussing ground and starts discussing the commander's personality as an attack surface. A handle is the correct image: a thing on you that someone else can take hold of.

---

## B. THE CORE STRATEGIC DICTUM

> **Every rule in this book has a case where it must be broken — including the order you were given — and the qualities you are proudest of are the exact places the enemy will take hold of you.**

**Unpacking (c. 195 words).** Chapter 8 does two things and they are the same thing. First it enumerates refusals: there are roads not to travel, armies not to attack, cities not to besiege, ground not to contest, and **orders from the sovereign that are not to be accepted** (君命有所不受). This is not a general theory of flexibility; it is a specific and dangerous doctrine of licensed insubordination, written for a ruler's reading and asserting that the man on the ground owns the decision. Then it says the wise man's calculation always mixes advantage with harm — you count the gain inside the loss and the loss inside the gain — and it closes by naming five dispositions that get generals killed: determination to die, determination to live, quickness to anger, scrupulousness about honour, and love of one's people.

**Where the popular reading is wrong.** Two errors. First, the chapter is read as "be adaptable" — a virtue so bland it costs nothing. It is in fact a rule about *authority*: who may say no, and to whom. Second, and more interesting: the five 危 are usually glossed as simple vices, and they are not simple. Recklessness, caution, spirit, integrity and compassion are the standard virtues of a commander, and Sun Tzu's mechanism is that each becomes a *handle* — a competent enemy will not fight your army, he will pull the one you are known for. **But the text does not let us call them virtues and stop there:** it closes the list with 凡此五者，將之過也，用兵之災也 — *these five are the general's faults, and a calamity in the use of troops.* The correct statement is therefore not "virtues, not vices". It is: five virtues carried to the point where they become predictable, which the text itself scores as 過. Being sure of your best quality is what makes it targetable.

**Textual note (the Historian says this once, here).** 九變 is the most visibly disordered chapter in the received book. It opens with a list of grounds that duplicates material in Chapter 11 (九地), and it never plainly enumerates nine of anything — commentators from Cao Cao onward disagree about which items constitute the "nine". Reading 九 as an indefinitely large number ("many") rather than a count goes back at least to the Song commentator Wang Xi 王皙, and some moderns follow him. It is also among the worst-preserved chapters in the Yinqueshan slips, so the manuscript evidence does not adjudicate it either. This is stated on screen, once, because it is what an honest reconstruction looks like.

## D. THE VIGNETTE

### **THE GENTLEMAN AT THE FORD**
**Winter, eleventh month, first day (己巳朔), 638 BCE · the river Hong 泓, in the state of Song (near modern Zhecheng, Henan)**
**Duke Xiang of Song 宋襄公 · the Grand Marshal (大司馬固, identified by Du Yu as Gongsun Gu 公孫固) · the Master of Horse (司馬, identified by Du Yu as Ziyu 子魚 / Muyi 目夷, the duke's elder half-brother) · the army of Chu**

The Song army finishes forming on its own bank of the Hong before the Chu army has finished crossing. This is the last time today that anything goes right.

Duke Xiang has wanted this for years. Song is a small state with an enormous pedigree — the house of Song descends from the fallen Shang, and their duke is a man who has never once forgotten it. He has been trying to convene the great lords and be the one who presides: at Lushang in the spring, then at Yu in the autumn. At Yu, Chu seized him in front of everyone and held him until the winter meeting at Bo, and being let go was worse than being taken. That was last year, not two summers ago; the humiliation is fresh. Now Chu has crossed into Song territory to punish him for going after Zheng, and the two armies are a river apart, and the river is on the duke's side.

Before any of it, the Grand Marshal has already told him not to fight at all. *Heaven abandoned Shang a long time ago. My lord means to raise it up again. It cannot be pardoned.* The duke does not listen. That is the first refusal, and almost nobody remembers it, because the two that follow are more famous.

The Chu host comes down to the water. They are the larger army by a wide margin and they begin to cross.

The Master of Horse is at the duke's shoulder. He says: they are many and we are few. They are not yet across. Let us hit them now.

The duke says: no.

The crossing takes a long time. Men come up out of the shallows with their formations gone; the far bank feeds them into the near bank in a queue, and for a long stretch the whole Chu army exists as a column standing in cold water in no order at all.

The Master of Horse says it again — they are over, but they have not formed up. Now.

The duke says: not yet.

He is not a coward and he is not a fool. He is doing something he believes is more important than winning, and he says so afterwards, so we have it in his own reported words: *a gentleman does not wound a man twice, and does not take the grey-haired prisoner. When the ancients made war they did not press an enemy in a defile. I may be only the remnant of a fallen house, but I will not beat the drum on an army that has not formed its line.*

He waits until Chu is across, dressed, and ready. Then he gives the order.

What follows is not a battle, it is an arithmetic operation. The Song army is broken. The duke's own guard — the *menguan* 門官, the men whose entire function is that he does not get hurt — are annihilated to the last man around him. **公傷股.** He is wounded in the thigh.

Afterwards the state blames him, and he is unrepentant, and Ziyu says the sentence that ends the story: **君未知戰。** *My lord does not understand war.* A serious enemy caught in a defile and out of line is Heaven handing him to you. Beating the drum on him then is not dishonour; it is the thing you were given an army for. And even then, Ziyu adds, I would still be frightened.

The wound does not close. Duke Xiang of Song dies the following summer, in the fifth month of 637, of the injury he took at the Hong.

Here is the part that makes this a Chapter 8 story and not a joke. For the next thousand years, a serious tradition of Chinese moral scholarship *praised him*. The *Gongyang* commentary singles out precisely his refusal to drum an unformed enemy as the act of a great man who did not forget the great rites under pressure. He was, by the standards he was applying, correct. Sun Tzu's answer is not that Duke Xiang was insufficiently ruthless. It is 廉潔可辱: a man whose honour is a fixed rule is a man whose behaviour can be computed by anyone standing on the far bank, and computed behaviour is a road the enemy walks down. Chu did not defeat Song at the Hong. Chu crossed a river slowly, in front of a man they could predict.

---

**Record vs. legend.** *Recorded, Zuozhuan* Xi 22: the battle at the Hong on the first day of the eleventh month of the twenty-second year of Duke Xi (638 BCE); the Grand Marshal's prior remonstrance against fighting at all; the Master of Horse's two urgings — before the crossing was complete, and again before Chu had formed line — and the duke's two refusals; the defeat; 公傷股, the wound to the thigh; 門官殲焉, the destruction of the guard; the duke's own defence of his conduct; and Ziyu's 君未知戰. *Zuozhuan* Xi 23 records his death the following summer from the wound. *Zuozhuan* Xi 21 records the meetings at Lushang and Yu, Chu's seizure of the duke at Yu, and his release at Bo in the winter — one year before the battle, not two. The campaign also appears in *Shiji* 38 (Hereditary House of Song) and *Shiji* 40 (Hereditary House of Chu). The *Gongyang* praise of his refusal is likewise recorded.

*Recorded, but at one remove:* the reason he was seizable at Yu — that he insisted on a 乘車之會, a meeting attended with civil carriages and no war chariots, against advice — is in *Shiji* 38 and the *Gongyang* tradition, **not** in the bare *Zuozhuan*, which reports only the meeting and the seizure. We keep the motive and mark the layer. Likewise the identification of the Grand Marshal with Gongsun Gu and of the Master of Horse with Ziyu/Muyi rests on Du Yu's commentary, not on the *Zuozhuan* text; some readers take 大司馬固 adverbially ("the Grand Marshal firmly remonstrated") and get no name at all.

*Legend and editorial convenience:* listing Duke Xiang among the Five Hegemons is a later habit and the lists disagree. He convened assemblies at Lushang and at Yu and failed at both; he never held anything comparable to the hegemony of Duke Huan of Qi or Duke Wen of Jin. Also *not* in any source, and cut from our staging: what time the Chu army ate, and how many minutes the crossing took.

## F. TACTIC DECISION NODE — **"THE HANDLE"**

**Situation (Commander):**
> You hold the crest and he cannot take it from you. So he has stopped trying. Overnight he has burned the villages behind your line and broken open the grave-mounds of your own district — deliberately, in plain view, at a distance he knows you cannot cover and return from before dark. At noon a rider comes in from your ruler's court with a letter asking why you are still sitting down.

*(This is 愛民可煩 and 廉潔可辱 being pulled at the same time, by a man who could not beat your position and did not try.)*

**OPTION A — 出救. Go out. Cover the people, answer the insult.**

*Simulated visual consequence:* The court's letter goes **gold** — ratified, and now it can no longer bleed. You leave the crest, which is the only ground on the sheet where **S** is high and your mark cannot be entered, and you commit onto the unsized flat where S = 0 and every stroke you lay bleeds at full K∥ along **G**. You are legible from the instant you commit — the reader watches their own intention become the most readable thing in frame. Then the enemy's marks *part* before yours (no contact; we cut away at contact, every time) and close along the grain behind you. Two costs the sheet keeps: an irreversible casualty tide line where your own front pinned and stopped, and a mark that is not yours standing on the crest you left. Ink: **roughly +0.10 to this chapter's local mean density**, which does not exhaust Act III's 0.42 ceiling but puts the cumulative trajectory ahead of curve, and the bamboo-slip register shows the reader the overshoot.

**OPTION B — 不動. Do not move. Let it burn. Send the courier back with nothing.**

*Simulated visual consequence:* **Nothing marks on the field.** The brush lifts away and the crest is untouched — the only branch in Act III that spends no D on the ground, and the reader will feel it as a win for about two seconds. The order never goes gold; it stays 淡墨, an intention that was never ratified, and it dries there. Then the court's answer arrives: a censure, a gold-ruled slip in the Historian's margin with a **vermilion seal** pressed into it — cinnabar at ~2.5× viscosity, beaded proud, unblended, and permanent, because vermilion never blends and consequence never dissolves into the gloom. It is the first vermilion in the site that is neither the reader's own commitment nor blood. It is drawn in the margin band of every subsequent chapter. You keep the ground. You carry the seal.

**HISTORICAL VERDICT (revealed only at M ≈ 0):**
Sun Tzu wrote both halves of this trap himself. 愛民可煩 and 廉潔可辱 are his: the enemy is not attacking your army, he is attacking the two things about you that everyone already knows. And 君命有所不受 is also his — the licence to send the courier back is on the page in his own sentence. What the text will not do is tell you which handle to let him pull. It names the five, and then it calls them 過 — *faults* — without ranking them, and its only procedural instruction is the one nobody quotes: 智者之慮，必雜於利害 — count the harm inside the advantage and the advantage inside the harm, and then choose while holding both.

**Where the two branches lead, historically.**
*B, and its price:* Sima Rangju, given command of Qi's army, executed the ruler's own favourite Zhuang Jia for arriving late to muster; when a royal messenger galloped into the camp with a reprieve, he said **將在軍，君令有所不受** — a general in the field does not accept every command from his sovereign — and, ruling that the sovereign's envoy could not himself be killed, had the messenger's **charioteer beheaded** and the left side-fitting of the carriage and the left trace-horse cut down in front of the army. *Caveat the Historian states on screen:* this is *Shiji* 64, a literary set-piece of the same doubtful class as the concubine drill in *Shiji* 65 which this site elsewhere declines to narrate as fact. It is an exemplum, not a securely dated event, and we hold it to the same standard.
*A, and its price:* Duke Xiang of Song at the Hong, going out to meet the situation with a fixed rule already in his hand — the inverse posture, obedience to a code rather than to a court, and the same mechanism underneath it: **behaviour a rule can predict is behaviour an enemy can price.**

*Node cost to the sheet: A spends ink and gives up the only sized ground in the chapter; B leaves a vermilion censure seal in the margin for the rest of the site. Neither branch reconverges.*

## G. CODEX MODE ENTRY — CH. 8 九變

**DICTUM** — Every rule here has a case where it must be broken, including your orders; and the quality you are proudest of is the handle by which you will be moved.

- **Five refusals, and the fifth is the live wire.** Roads, armies, cities, ground — and 君命有所不受, the sovereign's order. This is a doctrine about who owns the decision, not a note about flexibility. Chapter 3 argues the *neighbouring* case in different words — 縻軍, the army crippled by a court ordering an advance or a retreat it cannot see, and 將能而君不御者勝, victory where the ruler does not manage a capable general — but the sentence 君命有所不受 is Chapter 8's own.
- **雜於利害.** The wise man's calculation always mixes the two: find the loss inside the gain and the gain inside the loss *before* choosing, not after.
- **The five 危 are virtues carried to the point of predictability** — courage, prudence, spirit, integrity, compassion — and the text still scores them as 將之過也, the general's faults. Both halves are true and the chapter needs both. If your enemy can name your best quality, he has your route.
- *Textual*: the chapter never clearly lists nine of anything, its opening duplicates Chapter 11, and reading 九 as "many" goes back to Wang Xi in the Song. The received text is a reconstruction and we say so.

**APPLY IT** — Name the virtue an opponent would attribute to you, and assume he already has. A standard of conduct you hold without exception is a fixed route, and a fixed route can be marched down without scouting. You are not required to abandon it. You are required to stop being certain that you will act on it at the moment he chooses.

# CHAPTER 9

## A. IDENTITY

| | |
|---|---|
| **Number** | 9 of 13 |
| **Chinese title** | 行軍 |
| **Pinyin** | *Xíng jūn* |
| **Literal** | "moving the army" / "marching the army" — 行 here is *xíng*, to move/march |
| **Standard English title** | *The Army on the March* (Giles) — also *Marches*, *Manoeuvring the Army*, *On the March* |
| **Sharper alternative (ours)** | **SIGNS WITHOUT SPIRITS** |

*Why the alternative.* The chapter's back half is a catalogue of inferences that gets described, constantly, as Sun Tzu reading omens. It is the exact opposite: the most materialist passage in the book, in which every visible sign is traced to a physical cause with nothing supernatural anywhere in it. The title should defend that.

---

## B. THE CORE STRATEGIC DICTUM

> **Everything the enemy is about to do is already visible in something he cannot help doing — and none of it is supernatural. It is physical cause and physical consequence, and you have not learned to look.**

**Unpacking (c. 190 words).** Chapter 9 has two halves and the join between them is the point. The first half is siting doctrine: mountains, rivers, marshes, level ground — camp high, camp facing the light, never fight upstream, never linger where you cannot get out, and above all **do not fight him while he is in the water; let him get half over, then hit that half**. The second half is a list of roughly thirty diagnostic readings. Trees stirring: he is coming. Birds getting up: an ambush. Startled animals: a sweep. Dust high and sharp: chariots. Low and broad: infantry. Thin and running in lines: they are cutting wood. Men leaning on their spears: they are hungry. The water-carrier drinking first: they are thirsty. Birds settling on the camp: it is empty. Shouting in the night: they are frightened. Humble words while defences increase: he is coming. Aggressive words and forward movement: he is leaving. **Suing for peace with no prior arrangement: he is buying time.**

**Where the popular reading is wrong.** Twice over. First, these are called *omens*, and they are the anti-omen: Chapter 13 states flatly that foreknowledge cannot be had from spirits, cannot be got by analogy, cannot be computed from the heavens — it must be taken from men. Chapter 9 is the practical proof of that sentence. Second, the chapter is read as reconnaissance technique. Its closing paragraphs are about *discipline*: punish men before they are attached to you and they resent it; fail to punish once they are attached and they are useless. Instruct them with words, level them with the axe. The catalogue only works for an army that will report what it saw.

## D. THE VIGNETTE

### **THE KING ON THE LADDER**
**Sixth month, last day of the month (甲午晦), 575 BCE · Yanling 鄢陵, in Zheng territory (near modern Yanling county, Henan)**
**King Gong of Chu 楚共王 · Bo Zhouli 伯州犁, a Jin exile in Chu service · Duke Li of Jin 晉厲公 · Miao Benhuang 苗賁皇, a Chu exile in Jin service · Fan Gai 范匄 of Jin · Zifan 子反, commanding the Chu centre**

Chu comes up in the dark and at first light is already drawn up hard against the Jin camp — 楚晨壓晉軍而陳 — close enough that Jin has no open ground to form on. The Jin officers are alarmed, and they are right to be: an army that cannot deploy is an army that is already losing. Fan Gai walks up and gives the answer in six characters. *Fill in the wells. Level the cooking pits. Form ranks inside the camp itself.* So the Jin army begins destroying its own water supply in order to have somewhere to stand.

Across the flat, King Gong wants to see it for himself. He has a 巢車 — a nest-chariot, a wheeled mast with a box on top and a ladder up to it — and he climbs it. Zizhong sends Bo Zhouli up to stand behind him. Bo Zhouli is a Jin man whose father was killed in Jin and who came south; he knows exactly what a Jin army looks like when it is doing each of the things a Jin army does. The king watches, and describes what he sees, and the man behind him names the cause. Nothing else happens for some minutes. It is the purest scene of its kind in the literature.

*Riders galloping out to left and right.* — Calling in the officers.
*They are all collecting at the centre.* — Taking counsel.
*They are putting a tent up.* — Divining, reverently, before the tablets of their former rulers.
*They have struck the tent.* — The orders are about to be given.
*There is a great deal of noise, and the dust is going up.* — Filling in the wells and levelling the stoves. They are making themselves room to form.
*They are all mounting; and now left and right have taken their weapons and got down again.* — Hearing the oath.
*Will they fight?* — Not yet known.
*They have mounted, and left and right have both got down again.* — A prayer before battle.

Look at what is and is not in that list. Every single item is a physical event with a physical cause: a horse moves because a man was sent, dust rises because earth is being moved, a tent goes up because something is being done that requires cover. Even the divination is read as *behaviour* — the fact that they are consulting the ancestors tells the king where the Jin command is in its sequence. It does not tell him what the ancestors said, and Bo Zhouli does not pretend it does. This is the difference between reading signs and reading omens, performed by two men on a ladder in 575 BCE, roughly two and a half centuries before anyone wrote this chapter down.

And it is happening in both directions at once. On the other side Duke Li of Jin has Miao Benhuang, a Chu exile, doing exactly the same office in reverse: the good troops of Chu, he says, are only the king's own household in the centre; put our best against their left and right wings and mass three armies on the king's guard, and they break. Each army has hired the other army's memory.

The battle, when it comes, is a long confused day around a bog in the middle of the field, and the chapter does not need it. Two facts from it are worth keeping, because both are read rather than fought. King Gong is shot in the eye. And at the end of the day, when he calls Zifan to plan the second morning, Zifan cannot come — his servant Gu Yang has been bringing him wine and he is too drunk to stand. The king says: *Heaven is defeating Chu. I cannot wait here.* And Chu goes out in the night — 楚師宵遁 — and Jin walks into the abandoned camp and lives three days on Chu's grain.

Nobody on that field learned anything from a spirit. The king learned where the Jin command was from dust. The Jin learned where Chu was weak from a man who used to be Chu. And Chu lost the campaign because at the one hour the whole thing turned on, the man commanding the centre could not be got out of his tent, which is a physical fact with a physical cause and was fully visible to anyone who had been watching him drink.

---

**Record vs. legend.** *Recorded, Zuozhuan* Cheng 16 (575 BCE): the sixth-month engagement at Yanling on 甲午晦; 楚晨壓晉軍而陳 and the Jin officers' alarm; Fan Gai's 塞井夷灶，陳於軍中; King Gong ascending the 巢車 with Bo Zhouli attending behind him at Zizhong's direction, and the whole question-and-answer sequence quoted above; Miao Benhuang's counter-reading and his advice to mass against the king's household troops; the bog in the field; Lü Qi shooting King Gong in the eye and Yang Youji killing Lü Qi in return; Gu Yang's wine and Zifan's incapacity; 楚師宵遁, the night withdrawal; 晉入楚軍，三日穀, Jin eating Chu's grain for three days; and Zifan's death. The battle is also carried in *Shiji* 39 (Hereditary House of Jin) and *Shiji* 40 (Hereditary House of Chu).

*What kind of record this is.* The nest-chariot dialogue is *Zuozhuan* set-piece composition, written to the standards of its genre, as all *Zuozhuan* speech is. The engagement is recorded; the stichomythia is literature. We stage it as literature that is telling the truth about a method, and the Historian says both halves of that sentence.

*Not used, and why:* Wuzhang Plain 234 CE is Chapter 4's vignette and stays there. It is referenced once, by name and as a cross-reference, in this chapter's node verdict — because Chapter 4 already told it — and it is not retold.

## F. TACTIC DECISION NODE — **"THE THIRD DUST"**

**Situation (Commander):**
> At first light an embassy comes up from his camp under no truce you agreed to, offering terms, and his commander's letter is unusually courteous. Over his camp the dust stands low and broad. A second dust, thin and drawn out in lines, is going up in the woods east of the ford — timber, or a screen. And far off your left, beyond the ford, there is a third dust that you have not been able to account for since yesterday afternoon. While the envoy is still speaking, a flock lifts out of the ford woods and does not settle.

**OPTION A — 半濟而擊. End the parley. Take the ford now, while his column is in the water.**

*Simulated visual consequence:* The letterbox is already at 2.76:1; the camera does **not** cut to the enemy (180mm is reserved, and you never share space with him). You watch your own stroke go: a short, extremely dense 濃墨 mark driven into the wettest ground on the sheet, where K∥ is at maximum, so it blooms fast and wide and anisotropically down the watercourse. The **water disturbance** runs and the crossing force does not darken — it *loses identity*, remixing to undifferentiated grey until D < 0.05 and the men become weather. This is the site's first full rout, rendered without a drop of vermilion and without a single blue pixel. Then the cost: your ink and your camera both went to the ford, and behind you the third dust closes. The wet trail your own stroke left across the valley reaches ∂M/∂t = 0 and pins into a hard, bright tide line — your road home, now a boundary nothing you lay can bleed back across. **You bought a rout and sold the return.**

**OPTION B — 據高而觀. Hold the crest. Refuse the crossing. Find out what the third dust is.**

*Simulated visual consequence:* **Nothing you do marks.** What happens instead is drying. The dilute 淡墨 environmental wash lying over the unread ground — the ground you never spent anything to clear, at D ≈ 0.03–0.05 — has its moisture front recede across the valley, and the pine soot suspended in it accumulates at the pinned boundary and crystallises a faint, hard tide line along the treeline. That ground now takes no further bleed: no sign can propagate through it for the rest of the chapter, and you carry the blind strip into Chapter 10. Meanwhile the crossing force gets across, and dries, and becomes a hard-edged mark on your own bank that nothing you lay can enter. **You keep the road and you have given him a lodgement.** Ink cost: almost nothing, and it is not cheap.

**HISTORICAL VERDICT (revealed only at M ≈ 0):**
Both signs are Sun Tzu's own, verbatim: 無約而請和者，謀也 — terms offered with no prior covenant, so he is buying time — and 鳥起者，伏也 — birds getting up, so there are men underneath. **What the text does not do is add them together for you.** The first tells you the embassy has a purpose; it does not tell you what the purpose is buying. The second tells you there are men in the ford woods; it does not tell you whether they are the ambush waiting for your charge or the screen for the movement on your left. 半濟而擊之，利 is conditional — it is advantageous to strike the half-crossed force *if the crossing is the thing that matters*, and the third dust is the reason you cannot yet know that. Sunzi supplies a reading method and stops.

He does supply one warning against treating A as a reflex: 兵非貴益多也，惟無武進 — numbers are not the virtue, and do not advance on aggression alone. That is a caution, not a licence for B. **The instruction is to read the signs, not to apply the rule** — and the thing the chapter actually forbids is choosing before you have named the third dust.

Two commanders mark the outer edges of this. **Duke Xiang of Song, 638 BCE, at the Hong** — Chapter 8's man — was handed the half-crossed enemy twice by an officer who told him exactly what he was looking at, and refused both times, because he was applying a rule instead of reading a river; he died of the wound. And at the other edge, **Wuzhang Plain, 234 CE**, where a commander read a hundred days of deliberate provocation as an enemy who *needed* a battle, and won a campaign by not moving. **That case belongs to Chapter 4 and is told there; this chapter only points at it.**

*Node cost to the sheet: A spends the chapter's remaining density in one stroke, shows the reader a rout, and pins their own retreat into a tide line; B spends nothing and permanently blinds the treeline while a hard-edged enemy mark establishes on the reader's bank. Neither branch reconverges.*

## G. CODEX MODE ENTRY — CH. 9 行軍

**DICTUM** — Everything he is about to do is already showing in something he cannot help doing; none of it is supernatural, and you have simply not learned to look.

- **These are not omens, they are mechanics.** Dust high and sharp is chariots because chariot wheels throw a narrow column. Birds rising is an ambush because men underneath put them up. Chapter 13 forbids foreknowledge from spirits, portents and calculation of the heavens — this chapter is what it permits instead.
- **The three-line register everyone should memorise.** Humble words while defences grow: attack coming. Loud words and forward movement: withdrawal coming. Terms offered that nobody asked for: he needs time.
- **The signs do not sum themselves.** Each clause is *X者，Y也* — one observation, one cause. The chapter never combines two of them into an instruction, and a reader who turns the catalogue into a decision procedure has stopped using it.
- **The chapter ends on discipline, not scouting.** 令之以文，齊之以武 — instruct with words, level with the axe. Punish men who are not yet yours and they resent you; fail to punish men who are and they are useless. A catalogue of signs is worthless in an army that will not report what it saw.

**APPLY IT** — When terms are offered that nobody asked for, do not weigh the terms. Observe what physical preparation was quietly doubled while they were being offered — what was moved, what was stockpiled, who was withdrawn from where. Read the cost of the movement, never the speech that accompanies it, and do not act on one sign in isolation: the chapter's whole discipline is holding two readings at once for longer than is comfortable.

## Editorial notes

Findings from the two adversarial passes that were **rejected or only partially accepted**, with reasons. Everything not listed here was accepted and applied.

1. **Rejected in part — "Duke Xiang seized at Yu 'having come without a guard' is a later retelling; delete the motive."** The interval error was real and is fixed (Yu is Xi 21, the year *before* the battle, not "two summers ago"). But the unguarded-meeting motive is not invented: the 乘車之會 — a covenant attended with civil carriages rather than war chariots, against advice — is recorded in *Shiji* 38 and in the *Gongyang* tradition. It is retained and its source layer is now marked, rather than deleted.

2. **Rejected — "bind Ch7 Option B's obstacle to an enemy-applied alum (S) barrier."** The reviewer is right that dry ink is not a hydrophobic resist and that the original text fabricated repulsion; that error is fixed. But having an army apply sizing to a battlefield perimeter is a stranger invention than the one it replaces, and S is Chapter 4's doctrinal channel. The obstacle is instead grounded in Ink Law clause 1: the enemy's ground is *dry* (M ≈ 0.1), and dry paper takes a hard, final edge, so the late stroke cannot bleed into or over anything. Same visual outcome, no new mechanism.

3. **Rejected in part — "replace Ch8's alum fleck with a water-spot."** The poaching concern is accepted and the alum fleck is gone. A water-spot on raw xuan alters very little, so the replacement is a **burnished (calendered) spot** — a real xuan defect that lowers absorbency through surface compaction rather than chemistry, implemented as a local M/bleed clamp and never touching the S channel.

4. **Rejected — placing Guo Jia's 兵貴神速 speech specifically at Yi 易.** The substantive point is accepted: the speech does not belong at the flooded coast and the packet's coughing-at-the-roadside staging was invented, so both are removed and Guo Jia's illness is now correctly placed on the return from Liucheng. But I could not verify the place-name 易 in *Sanguozhi* Wei 14, so the speech is sited at the campaign's decision rather than pinned to a location the packet cannot support.

5. **Rejected as stated — "the packet swapped shallow/deep in the flooded-road description."** The original phrasing ("neither deep enough to float a boat nor shallow enough to carry a wheel") was an idiomatic paraphrase with the same practical meaning, not an inversion. It has nonetheless been replaced with a close rendering of Tian Chou's actual words, 淺不通車馬，深不載舟船, because the source is more vivid than the paraphrase.

6. **Accepted with a note — the 動如雷霆 / 動如雷震 apparatus.** Added as flagged, but weighted as a genuine minority variant: 霆 is the dominant received reading and is what we use on screen.

7. **Note, not a rejection — the Ch7 "seven years" framing** was our own arithmetic from Guandu (200) to Liucheng (207) and has been cut entirely rather than kept and labelled, along with "in an afternoon", "in a box", and "silver", none of which any source supports.
