# CHAPTER 4 — 軍形

## A. IDENTITY

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Number | 4 (Act I → Act II hinge; first chapter of THE SHAPE) |
| Chinese title | 軍形 |
| Pinyin | **Jūn Xíng** |
| Literal | "army form" / "the disposition of forces" |
| Standard English | *Tactical Dispositions* (Giles) — also rendered *Formation*, *Dispositions*, *Form* |
| **Sharper alternative** | **ALREADY DECIDED** |

*Why the alternative.* "Tactical Dispositions" sounds like a chapter about where to put units. It is not. It is a chapter about *when the outcome happened* — which is before the units moved. The chapter's real subject is the epistemology of victory: victory is not produced by fighting, it is **recognised** in fighting. Titling it *Already Decided* puts the reader in the correct dread.

> **Title apparatus (applies to all three chapters of this act).** The Yinqueshan 篇題木牘 and slip titles run **形 · 勢 · 實虛** against the received **軍形 · 兵勢 · 虛實**: the older titles are the bare one-graph forms, and chapter 6's pair is in the reverse order. The site should set the short title (形 / 勢 / 實虛) in 篆書 at chapter-card scale and let the received forms sit small in the Historian's apparatus. *(Confidence: high that the Yinqueshan titles are shorter and that 實虛 is the slip order; verify graph-by-graph against Wu Jiulong's apparatus at build.)*

## B. THE CORE STRATEGIC DICTUM

> **You can make yourself impossible to beat; you cannot make him possible to beat — so build the first, and wait, and the outcome is sealed before a blade leaves the scabbard.**

Sun Tzu splits the outcome into two halves with different owners. **不可勝在己，可勝在敵** — *invincibility is in yourself, vulnerability is in the enemy.* Your own security is entirely a matter of work you can do today: ground, walls, stores, order, the fact that no route into you is cheap. His collapse is not a matter of your work at all; it is his to donate. So the competent general does the half he owns, completely, and then does the intolerable thing — nothing — until the other half arrives. Battle, when it finally happens, is bookkeeping: **勝兵先勝而後求戰** — *the winning army wins first and seeks battle afterwards.*

The popular reading gets this exactly backwards in two ways. First, it reads the chapter as a defensive doctrine, a counsel of turtling. It is not defensive; it is a claim about *sequence*. Defence is merely the half that is available early. Second — and this is the damaging one — the self-help tradition reads **無智名，無勇功** ("no reputation for wisdom, no merit for courage") as modest humility, a nice line about ego. It is not about ego. It is an **epistemological warning to the audience**: if a victory looked brilliant, it was late. Brilliance is the visible signature of a general who let the situation get close enough to require brilliance. The general you should be watching is the one nothing was ever written about, and the chapter is telling you that your instinct for spotting excellence is calibrated on exactly the wrong evidence.

There is a third misreading worth killing on the page: that this is a chapter about patience as a *virtue*. It is a chapter about patience as *arithmetic*. **勝可知，而不可為** — *victory can be foreseen; it cannot be forced.* Cao Cao's gloss is the operative one: you may see that a victory is available and still be unable to compel the enemy into it (不能使敵必可勝). Foreseeing is a measurement problem, and the chapter ends by giving you the instrument: 度、量、數、稱、勝 — measure the ground, estimate the yield, count the men, weigh the two sides, and the result falls out. Nothing in that list is a feeling.

## C. THREE KEY LINES

### C1 — the ownership split

- **Chinese (trad.):** 昔之善戰者，先為不可勝，以待敵之可勝。不可勝在己，可勝在敵。
- **Pinyin:** *Xī zhī shàn zhàn zhě, xiān wéi bù kě shèng, yǐ dài dí zhī kě shèng. Bù kě shèng zài jǐ, kě shèng zài dí.*
- **Literal:** "The good fighters of old first made themselves not-conquerable, and thereby waited for the enemy's conquerability. Not-conquerable lies in oneself; conquerable lies in the enemy."
- **Modern:** "The old masters made themselves unbeatable first, then waited for the other side to become beatable. Your safety is your job. His collapse is his."
- **Confidence:** High. Received text secure in both Song lineages; the Yinqueshan 形 slips support the opening where the slips survive — they are fragmentary, and "stable across the manuscript tradition" would overstate what the apparatus can carry.

### C2 — the sequence

- **Chinese (trad.):** 是故勝兵先勝而後求戰，敗兵先戰而後求勝。
- **Pinyin:** *Shì gù shèng bīng xiān shèng ér hòu qiú zhàn, bài bīng xiān zhàn ér hòu qiú shèng.*
- **Literal:** "Therefore the victorious army first wins and afterwards seeks battle; the defeated army first fights and afterwards seeks victory."
- **Modern:** "Winners settle it, then fight. Losers fight, then go looking for a way to win."
- **Confidence:** High.

### C3 — the un-famous general

- **Chinese (trad.):** 故善戰者之勝也，無智名，無勇功。
- **Pinyin:** *Gù shàn zhàn zhě zhī shèng yě, wú zhì míng, wú yǒng gōng.*
- **Literal:** "Thus the victories of one skilled at war have no name for wisdom, no merit for courage."
- **Modern:** "When the good ones win, nobody calls it clever and nobody calls it brave. There is nothing to call it."
- **Confidence:** High. Giles renders it "his victories bring him neither reputation for wisdom nor credit for courage," which is the phrasing in circulation.

**Bench (not for display, for the Historian's margin):**
善守者藏於九地之下，善攻者動於九天之上 — *the good defender hides beneath the ninth earth, the good attacker moves above the ninth heaven* (i.e. neither is findable). And the closing image, which is the bridge into Chapter 5: 勝者之戰民也，若決積水於千仞之谿者，形也 — *the victor commits his men the way one releases pent water down a thousand-fathom gorge: that is form.*

**HISTORIAN'S NOTE (textual, must be said aloud on the page).** The received text reads **守則不足，攻則有餘** — "defend when strength is insufficient, attack when it is in surplus." The **Yinqueshan** slips reverse it: **守則有餘，攻則不足** — "in defending you have more than enough; in attacking you have too little." The reversed form is not a manuscript oddity: it is the form Han writers quote as *bingfa*. *Hanshu*, Zhao Chongguo's memorial: 「攻不足者守有餘」. *Hou Hanshu*, Feng Yi: 「攻者不足，守者有餘」. Two independent Han citations and the Han slips point one way; the Song recensions the other.

**This is a live crux, not a solved text, and the page must say so.** Cao Cao already read the received line the received way (力不足則守), so the Song reading has a third-century commentator behind it and is not a scribal accident. We **print both lemmas** on the page. The Codex *foregrounds* the reversed reading, and states plainly that this is an editorial choice made because the reversed reading is the older attested one and because it is the more interesting argument — not because the Song line is an error and not because we have solved the crux. **The honesty test is printing both, not picking the romantic one.** *(Confidence: high on the crux and on the two Han citations; the site should not cite Tongdian as a witness for the reversed Sunzi sentence — see Editorial notes.)*

## D. THE VIGNETTE

### ONE HUNDRED DAYS AT WUZHANG
**Spring to the eighth month, 234 AD (建興十二年 / 青龍二年) · the Wuzhang Plains (五丈原), south bank of the Wei River, Fufeng commandery · Sanguozhi; Zizhi Tongjian; Jin Shu, Xuandi ji; Pei Songzhi's citations of Weishi Chunqiu and Han Jin Chunqiu**

In the spring, Zhuge Liang brings a hundred thousand men out through the Xie Valley — 十二年春，亮悉大眾由斜谷出 — and by summer he is on the terrace at Wuzhang, south of the river.

Sima Yi does not wait to be told what that means. The people and the grain of this commandery are all on the south bank; whoever stands there owns the campaign. So he takes the army across the Wei and builds his camp with the water at his back — **背水為壘** — which is the posture of a man who has removed his own retreat on purpose, and his officers assume it is a prelude.

Guo Huai reads the map and says the northern heights, Beiyuan: if Shu takes those they cut the road and turn the flank. Take them first. Sima Yi takes them first, and the palisade there is still going in when the Shu vanguard arrives. There is fighting at Beiyuan. There is fighting later along the Wugong water, at Jishi and Yangsui, and the Wei records count heads and prisoners for it. What there is not, and will not be, is a battle.

Because now Zhuge Liang does the thing an invader is not supposed to be able to do. He divides his army and puts it into the ground — 分兵屯田 — Shu soldiers working the Wei valley strips beside Wei peasants, a fair share taken, no requisitions, no burning; the record says the inhabitants were undisturbed and the army took nothing privately (百姓安堵，軍無私焉). An army that plants is an army that intends to stay past winter, and the clock Sima Yi was counting on — the clock that killed all four previous expeditions, the clock made of grain carts and mountain roads — has just stopped.

So he stops too.

**相持百餘日.** More than a hundred days. The two camps sit within sight across the flat, close enough that each side's watch-drums arrive slightly late, out of time with the beat you can see being struck. Zhuge Liang offers battle in the morning. Sima Yi does not come out. Zhuge Liang offers battle at midday. Sima Yi does not come out. Shu riders walk their horses along the palisade line shouting things about Wei mothers, and the Wei line stands and takes it, and the officers' faces go tighter every week — because three years ago at Mount Qi men in this same army said it out loud to his face. *You fear Shu the way a man fears a tiger; what will the empire say?* He had let himself be pushed. He had gone out. He had lost the engagement, and the enemy had counted three thousand sets of armour off his dead.

He does not intend to buy that again.

Then the parcel arrives. A gift, formally presented, from the Prime Minister of Han to the Grand General of Wei: a woman's headdress and a woman's clothes.

The tent goes silent in the specific way tents do when a hundred officers are waiting to see whether their commander is a man. Sima Yi turns the cloth over. Then he does something that no one in the tent can immediately classify: he writes east, a thousand li east, to a court that is not even sitting still — the Emperor is on campaign against Wu this season — and requests permission to give battle.

The answer comes back as a man. Xin Pi, **使持節**, bearing the imperial staff, plants himself in the gateway of the camp and forbids it. **六軍皆肅** — the six armies fall in behind the staff, and nobody crosses it. The insult is answered. Not one man has left the palisade.

Across the flat, Jiang Wei brings the news to Zhuge Liang and says: he has been handed an imperial refusal, he will never come out now.

Zhuge Liang does not look up. *He never had any intention of fighting. He asked for permission so his own men would see him ask. A general in the field is not bound by his sovereign's orders — if he could actually control us, would he send a thousand li for leave to try?*

Both men are right, and only one of them has time.

A Shu rider crosses the shallows on an errand between the camps. Sima Yi receives him in the outer tent and asks nothing about troop numbers, grain stores or order of battle. He asks what the Prime Minister eats, and how he sleeps, and how much he decides himself. The envoy, flattered, is expansive: he rises before dawn and retires late, he personally reviews every punishment above twenty strokes, and he eats — the envoy laughs — very little.

When the man has gone, Sima Yi says to his staff: *he eats little and handles much. How long can he last?*

Eighth month. The Shu camp changes its noise. Then it changes its smoke. Then, one night, the cooking fires along the terrace do not light. Zhuge Liang is dead in his camp at fifty-four, and by morning the army is gone southward, in good order and without panic, with a sealed cart somewhere in the column going home through the defiles.

At noon Sima Yi walks the abandoned works: the ditch profiles cut to standard depth, the latrines limed, the stove pits in their exact grid, every trench line squared as though the army had expected to be inspected rather than to leave. He says the only complimentary sentence anyone records from him about his enemy. **天下奇才也** — *a genius of the age.*

He had never beaten him. He had simply been unbeatable for longer than Zhuge Liang could be alive.

> **Record vs. legend.** *Sanguozhi* and *Zizhi Tongjian* carry the spine: the fifth expedition out of the Xie Valley in the spring of 建興十二年; Sima Yi's crossing and 背水為壘; Guo Huai's pre-emption of Beiyuan; the military farms among the Wei-valley households; the standoff of 相持百餘日; Xin Pi despatched 使持節 with the result 六軍皆肅; Zhuge Liang's death in the eighth month at fifty-four; and Sima Yi's 天下奇才也 on inspecting the abandoned camp, which stands in the main text of the Zhuge Liang biography, not in the commentary. *Jin Shu*, Xuandi ji, records the fighting at Jishi and Yangsui with a count of heads and prisoners, and the *Shuijing zhu* has Sima Yi attacking Meng Yan on the Wugong water and being shot off a bamboo bridge — **there were clashes; there was no general engagement, and the two claims are not the same claim.** The women's clothing and the "he eats little and handles much" exchange survive in *Weishi Chunqiu* and *Han Jin Chunqiu*, quoted by Pei Songzhi and reused by Sima Guang — later, partisan, plausible, not primary. Zhuge Liang's 千里請戰 riposte is *Han Jin Chunqiu*.
>
> **Two things this page refuses to narrate as fact.** (1) The pursuit episode — Sima Yi presses the withdrawal, the Shu rearguard turns its drums and banners on him, he pulls back, and the countryside coins **死諸葛走生仲達**, "dead Zhuge routs living Zhongda." That is *Han Jin Chunqiu* and the *Jin Shu*, i.e. the same partisan stratum, and it is folklore. It is not the ending of our vignette; it lives here, in the apparatus, where it belongs. (2) 234 ends the *fifth northern expedition*, not the war: Jiang Wei went on campaigning north for another three decades.
>
> **The 231 counter-case, kept in its two separate pieces.** (A) In the fourth expedition, before the engagement of the fifth month, Wei officers — Jia Xu and Wei Ping in *Han Jin Chunqiu* — taunt Sima Yi with 「畏蜀如虎」. He goes out, and he *loses*: the Shu account claims three thousand 甲首. (B) Separately, in the sixth month, Zhuge Liang withdraws for want of supply. *Wei Lue* records Zhang He objecting — 「歸軍勿追」, do not pursue an army that is going home — being overruled, and dying in the pursuit at the Mumen defile. **The taunt did not kill Zhang He. The forced pursuit did.** On the wound itself the sources differ and we say so: *Sanguozhi* has 「飛矢中郃右膝」, a flying arrow in the right knee; *Wei Lue* has 「弓弩亂發，矢中郃髀」, massed bow-and-crossbow fire and a hit in the thigh. Do not merge them into one crossbow bolt in one knee.

## E. VISUAL CUE — "THE FORTRESS MADE OF REFUSAL"

- **Environment.** A flat river terrace at the hour when there is no sun to locate. Two camps on one sheet, and the sheet is the emptiest it will be after Act I: mean density ~0.09. Foreground, the near palisade as dry contour. Midground, four hundred paces of stubble, irrigation cuts, one ox. Background, the Qinling foothills as a single compressed horizon wash. **The Wei River is not drawn in ink**: it is an un-inked saturation lane — M ≈ 0.85, D ≤ 0.02 — running between the terrace banks, and it is legible because saturated xuan swells along θ and returns ambient value differently from the dry terrace at M ≈ 0.15. A seam in the fibre direction alone would be invisible under this chapter's lighting; the river reads as *wetness*, not as line and not as shadow.
- **Dominant ink behaviour — S, SIZING (this is the chapter that owns it).** Fortification renders as **nothing**. The alum-sized region has no line, no colour, no edge, no highlight; it is identical to unread paper until ink is spent against it. But where Chapter 3 sized a single object — one city wall, one white refusal-halo — Chapter 4 sizes **a territory**. S = 1.0 is scored across the whole terrace as a cadastral geometry: ditch lines, farm boundaries, camp perimeters, the road embankments. Ink run across this flat does not simply stop at a castle silhouette; it *channels* down the unsized irrigation ruts and halts against the sized berms, so what emerges is not a building but a survey map of refusal covering the entire sheet. **Negative-space architecture: the chapter's thesis is a shader feature, not a metaphor.**
- **Particle system.** Two, both sheet-bound, no airborne smoke and **no particle-driven deposits** — chaff does not write: (1) chaff and threshing dust off the military farms, InstancedMesh, N < 800, drifting along θ at ground level to make the wind direction legible without adding a texel of D; (2) crystallisation ticks along the drying front where ∂M/∂t crosses zero. Per Ink Law rule 6 the tide line is bright, hard-edged and permanent, so the accumulated tide lines of every failed probe do exactly what the chapter wants: over the chapter they close into a **bright, hard-edged outline of the fortification the reader was never shown**, drawn by drying rather than by ink.
- **Lighting.** Overcast, keyless, **shadowless**. The only value in the frame is ink density and moisture. This is the one chapter in the thirteen with no directional light at all — it is what "no reputation for brilliance" looks like as a lighting design — and every cue in the frame must therefore be carried by D or M, never by relief.
- **Camera move.** The chapter is one shot. 85mm, a lateral pan-and-scan at roughly two degrees per ten seconds, tracking while the reader thinks and refusing to cut — because a cut is a decision and this chapter's argument is the withheld decision. Exactly **one** insert is permitted: a 180mm compression across the flat onto the enemy camp, held far too long, with heat shimmer expressed as a moisture gradient in the paper rather than as an optical effect. You never share space with him. That is the whole point of the lens rule and this is where the rule is taught.
- **The one image they will remember.** *He* floods the sheet — and a fortress appears in white, drawn entirely by where his ink stopped. You built nothing that could be seen. He paid to find out it was there.

## F. TACTIC DECISION NODE — "THE PARCEL"

**Situation (Commander, 行草 on the operative term):**
> A hundred days. His army is in your fields, planting your ground, and the harvest that ripens out there is the only clock you had. This morning he sends you a woman's headdress, and every officer in the tent is watching your hands.

**Option A — GO OUT.** Not a fit of temper: a reason. The standing grain on the south bank is what has stopped your clock, and it is four hundred paces from your gate. Take the army out, burn the crop, break the farms, and put him back on a supply line through the mountains — with an army that is angry and therefore fast, on the flattest ground in the commandery.

**Option B — STAY IN.** Answer the insult with paperwork: request permission to fight from a court a thousand li east, and let the staff at your gate say no for you. The farms stay. The clock stays stopped. You are betting that his body runs out before your discipline does.

**Simulated visual consequence — A (LOAD → MARK → BLOOM → DRY → READ).**
The aperture closes to 2.76:1 and the gate opens as a **cut**, the chapter's first — the reader caused it, and the reader will feel that they caused it. 濃墨 leaves the terrace in one committed stroke, and the sizing field becomes irrelevant the instant you are outside it: on open ground there is no **S**, so bleed runs unopposed at full K∥ along the valley grain. Your mark reaches the fields, is heavy, is impressive, and is *permanent*: page density jumps ~0.07 in four seconds, more than a third of Act II's entire budget spent in one node. The farms burn — and the burn is your ink, not his. Then the enemy mark does not break. It parts, closes, and the anisotropy field carries your own bloom back along your line of advance, and where it crosses the sized cadastral geometry it **covers it**, because D is monotonic and the survey map of refusal you spent a season building is now under your own ink and can never be recovered. At READ the aperture opens back to 2.39:1 on a dark sheet. Chapter 5 opens on a page that has already spent its reserve.

**Simulated visual consequence — B.**
Nothing of yours marks. The aperture holds at 2.76:1 through the whole node — the bars stay grown, the frame stays tight, and the reader is held inside a decision they are not allowed to spend — and it eases back to 2.39:1 only at READ, when the verdict is legal. One vermilion event: the seal press of the imperial refusal, laid at the gate per the colour doctrine and never blending. The drum does not sound.

Then **he** floods the sheet. This is the chapter's promised image and this branch is where it is delivered: over four scroll-beats the enemy commits a broad, multi-pronged 濃墨 wash across the terrace at full K∥, and it runs, and it finds the sized geometry. It channels down the irrigation ruts. It stops dead along the berms. It stops again at the camp perimeter. His moisture crosses zero at every halted front and the tide lines crystallise bright along all of it — and the fortress, the farm grid, the whole cadastral survey of refusal, is carved out of the sheet in white by **his** ink and **his** drying. Your page density rises by ~0.004: dust, not decision. His rises by four times that.

The reward for choosing B is that you are given **nothing of your own to look at**, and the reader's boredom is the mechanic. Act II proceeds with its ink budget effectively intact, and the reader will find out in Chapter 12 what that reserve was worth.

**HISTORICAL VERDICT (revealed at M ≈ 0, never before).**
*What follows is the reading the chapter's doctrine draws from 234, stated as such. It is not a claim that the campaign was a controlled demonstration of the chapter.*

Sun Tzu: **勝兵先勝而後求戰，敗兵先戰而後求勝**, and **故善戰者之勝也，無智名，無勇功**. B is the chapter. At Wuzhang in 234 Sima Yi refused contact for over a hundred days, absorbed a public humiliation designed by the best mind of the age specifically to make refusal impossible, and finished the campaign without ever accepting a general engagement. **He did not "never fight":** Guo Huai fought at Beiyuan, the Jin Shu counts heads at Jishi and Yangsui, and the Shuijing zhu has Sima Yi himself shot off a bridge on the Wugong water. What he refused was the *decisive* battle, which is the only thing the chapter asks him to refuse.

And 無智名，無勇功 is a maxim about how victory is *judged*, not a description of this man's press. The *Jin Shu* praises the Wuzhang stand outright and the later tradition made it the type-case of 持重, weighted steadiness. The line is not "history forgot Sima Yi." The line is: *the kind of win he took is the kind that generates no anecdote*, and our instinct for spotting excellence is trained on the anecdote.

The counter-case is the same man three years earlier, and it is two events, not one. In 231 his officers pushed him out with 「畏蜀如虎」 and the sortie *failed* — Shu claimed three thousand sets of armour. Separately, weeks later, he forced a pursuit of an army that was already leaving over Zhang He's explicit objection 「歸軍勿追」, and Zhang He died for it in the Mumen defile. Going out cost him an engagement; forcing the pursuit cost him a general. **The purest form of A is Xiahou Yuan at Mount Dingjun in 219**, who was *contesting* the mountain rather than holding it (定軍山 was Liu Bei's camp; 淵將兵來爭其地), who split his force with Zhang He and then went out with a few hundred men to mend an abatis fifteen li from camp — the detail is in Cao Cao's 軍策令, preserved in Pei Songzhi's annotation — and was killed there. Huang Zhong's downhill charge (乘高鼓噪) is a separate set-piece in the Liu Bei and Fa Zheng biographies, not the same continuous action, and should not be spliced to the abatis. Cao Cao's admonition — 「為將當有怯弱時，不可但恃勇也」, a general must know how to be timid and must not rely on courage alone — is in the main Xiahou Yuan biography; the 白地將軍 verdict and the abatis complaint are in the annotated 軍策令. Two citations, kept apart. **Going out is not the brave choice. It is the choice made by a man who has decided that being watched is more dangerous than being killed.**

## G. CODEX MODE ENTRY — 4 軍形

> **勝兵先勝而後求戰。** The winning army wins first and seeks battle afterwards.

- **The halves have different owners.** Your unbeatability is work you can do this week. His collapse is not yours to schedule. Do the first completely; wait for the second without decorating the wait.
- **Two readings of the same line, and we print both.** Received: *defend when strength is insufficient, attack when it is in surplus.* Yinqueshan and the Han citations: *in defending you have more than enough; in attacking you have too little.* We foreground the second because it is older and because it is the harder claim — the attacker is the one who is short — and we tell you that is an editorial choice.
- **Fame is a symptom of lateness.** No reputation for wisdom, no merit for courage: a victory that looked brilliant is a victory that was nearly lost. Your instinct for spotting excellence is trained on the wrong evidence.

**Apply it:** Audit the sequence before you audit the action. Measure the ground, estimate what it yields, count what is committed, weigh the two sides — 度、量、數、稱 — and the fifth term falls out of the first four. If your plan requires him to blunder or you to be brilliant, you are fighting in order to find a victory rather than fighting because you already have one. That is not a courage problem and no amount of resolve will fix it. Go back and rebuild the four measurements.

# CHAPTER 5 — 兵勢

## A. IDENTITY

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Number | 5 (centre of THE SHAPE) |
| Chinese title | 兵勢 |
| Pinyin | **Bīng Shì** |
| Literal | "the shi of troops" — 勢 is disposition-as-stored-energy: leverage, momentum, positional potential |
| Standard English | *Energy* (Giles) — also *Force*, *Strategic Power*, *Momentum*, *Configuration of Power* |
| **Sharper alternative** | **CONFIGURATION, NOT COURAGE** |

*Why the alternative.* Every English title for this chapter names the output (energy, force, momentum) and hides the argument, which is about the **input**: that the output is a property of an arrangement, not of the people inside it. The chapter says so flatly — 勇怯，勢也, *bravery and cowardice are matters of shi*. Same men, different configuration, opposite behaviour. Title it *Configuration, Not Courage* and the chapter stops being a hype speech.

> **Title apparatus.** Received 兵勢; the Yinqueshan title is the bare **勢**. Same system as chapters 4 and 6 — see the note under Chapter 4A, which governs all three.

> Note: 形 (Ch4) and 勢 (Ch5) are a matched pair and must be built as one system. 形 is the *arrangement at rest*; 勢 is *what that arrangement will do when released*. Ch4 is the boulder's position on the mountain; Ch5 is the mountain's height. Neither is a quality of the boulder.

## B. THE CORE STRATEGIC DICTUM

> **Force is not something your men have; it is something your arrangement does to them — so stop demanding valour of men and build the gradient that produces it.**

Sun Tzu's move here is to relocate military virtue out of persons and into geometry. Torrential water rolls boulders — that is 勢, the accumulated potential of height and mass. A bird of prey shatters its quarry in one strike — that is 節, *jié*, the timing and shortness of release. The pair is the whole chapter: **其勢險，其節短**, the potential steep, the release brief. Then the mechanism: **凡戰者，以正合，以奇勝** — you engage with 正 (*zhèng*, the ordinary, the force he can see and must answer) and you decide with 奇 (*qí*, the extraordinary, the force his answer has just made room for). They are not two kinds of troops. They are two *roles*, and they trade places continuously — 奇正相生，如循環之無端, generating each other like a circle with no starting point. A flank that is committed and fixed has become 正; the reserve you just released is 奇 only until he turns to face it.

Where the popular reading fails: it translates 奇 as "surprise" or "the unconventional," which turns the chapter into a licence for cleverness — the trick attack, the unexpected angle, war as improv. That is a misreading with a body count. 奇 has no meaning without 正: it is defined *relationally*, as the force that acts on the commitment the ordinary force has extracted. Surprise with nothing fixing the enemy is not 奇, it is a raid. And the second failure is worse, because it is moral: the chapter's real punchline is **求之於勢，不責於人** — *seek it in the configuration, do not demand it of individuals.* Sun Tzu is telling commanders that when their men break, the men are not the fact; the arrangement is the fact. Rendering this chapter as motivational — "unleash your energy" — inverts a doctrine that exists specifically to stop leaders from blaming people for the geometry those leaders built.

## C. THREE KEY LINES

### C1 — the engine

- **Chinese (trad.):** 凡戰者，以正合，以奇勝。
- **Pinyin:** *Fán zhàn zhě, yǐ zhèng hé, yǐ qí shèng.*
- **Literal:** "In all battle, join with the straight/ordinary, win with the odd/extraordinary."
- **Modern:** "You make contact with the force he expects, and you settle it with the force his answer just made room for."
- **Confidence:** High. Note 奇 here is read *qí* ("odd, unpaired, extraordinary"), not *jī*.

### C2 — potential and release

- **Chinese (trad.):** 激水之疾，至於漂石者，勢也；鷙鳥之疾，至於毀折者，節也。⋯是故善戰者，其勢險，其節短。勢如彍弩，節如發機。
- **Pinyin:** *Jī shuǐ zhī jí, zhì yú piāo shí zhě, shì yě; zhì niǎo zhī jí, zhì yú huǐ zhé zhě, jié yě. … Shì gù shàn zhàn zhě, qí shì xiǎn, qí jié duǎn. Shì rú kuò nǔ, jié rú fā jī.*
- **Literal:** "That torrential water in its speed can float stones — that is shi. That a bird of prey in its speed can shatter — that is jie. … So the good fighter: his shi is steep, his jie is short. Shi is like a drawn crossbow; jie is like the tripping of the trigger."
- **Modern:** "Water that can roll rock got that from the drop, not the water. A hawk breaks a back in one beat because of when it stops falling. Build a steeper drop; make the release shorter."
- **Confidence:** High on the passage, **medium on three graphs**, and the divergences run along the Song stemmatic split, so name the edition when quoting. (a) 鷙鳥之**疾** in our base text against 鷙鳥之**擊** elsewhere — "the swiftness of the bird of prey" versus "the strike of the bird of prey"; the parallelism with 激水之疾 favours 疾, the sense favours 擊, and the tradition is genuinely divided. (b) 勢如**彍**弩 against 擴 and 張 (all giving "drawn/spanned crossbow"). (c) 節如**發機** against 節如**機發** — "like tripping the trigger" versus "like the trigger going off." Use 彍 / 疾 / 發機 in the body with all three flagged in the apparatus. 鷙鳥 = bird of prey; Giles's English here is a falcon's well-timed **swoop**, so do not attribute "the falling hawk" to Giles — it is loose popular quotation with no owner.

### C3 — the moral inversion

- **Chinese (trad.):** 故善戰者，求之於勢，不責於人，故能擇人而任勢。
- **Pinyin:** *Gù shàn zhàn zhě, qiú zhī yú shì, bù zé yú rén, gù néng zé rén ér rèn shì.*
- **Literal:** "Thus one skilled at war seeks it in the shi and does not demand it of individuals; therefore he can select men and entrust matters to the shi."
- **Modern:** "The good ones look for the answer in the arrangement and stop blaming people. That is what lets them place a man and then trust the situation instead of the man."
- **Confidence:** High.

**Bench:** 治亂，數也；勇怯，勢也；強弱，形也 — *order and disorder are a matter of organisation; courage and cowardice, of shi; strength and weakness, of form.* And the closing image, which is the load-bearing one for the shader: 故善戰人之勢，如轉圓石於千仞之山者，勢也 — *the shi of the good commander is like rolling a round stone down a mountain a thousand fathoms high.* Round, not sharp; the stone is chosen for its indifference.

**HISTORIAN'S NOTE.** The classical extended treatment of 奇正 is the Tang dialogue *Questions and Replies between Tang Taizong and Li Weigong* (唐太宗李衛公問對), which insists the two are one thing viewed from two sides and that a commander's whole art is making the enemy read the wrong one as the wrong one. Its authorship is disputed — traditionally Tang, argued by a number of scholars to be a Northern Song compilation. Say so; do not cite it as Sun Tzu.

## D. THE VIGNETTE

### THE THOUSAND OXEN OF JIMO
**Conventionally dated 279 BC · Jimo (即墨), one of the last two free cities of Qi, on the Shandong coast · Shiji 82, 田單列傳 (Sima Qian); Shiji 80, 樂毅列傳**

Years earlier, on the road out of Anping, Tian Dan had won an argument with a chariot axle.

Everyone else fled with carts as built, long axle-ends jutting past the hubs. At the choke point the axle-ends fouled, the carts broke, and the people in them were taken by Yan. The Tian clan's carts had been prepared in advance on the instruction of a minor market official nobody had thought about twice — ends sawn short, iron caps fitted. His carts went through the gap. That is who is now commanding at Jimo.

Yue Yi has been in Qi for five years — 留徇齊五歲，下齊七十餘城, five years of campaigning and occupation, seventy-odd cities taken, the capital Linzi gone in the first of them. Two cities have never come in: Ju in the south, and this one. The Yan siege lines have been out there long enough to have become a suburb.

Then the King of Yan dies and his son succeeds him, and Tian Dan sends words out through the lines the way you would seed a field.

*The only thing Yue Yi is really doing is stalling. He wants to be King of Qi himself. If Yan ever sent an ordinary general, this city would fall in a month.*

The new king recalls Yue Yi. Yue Yi, who is not a fool, does not go home; he goes to Zhao. And Qi Jie arrives to take command of the siege — competent, orthodox, and holding a job he got because a rumour was cheaper than a war.

Now Tian Dan begins to build the slope, and none of it looks like fighting.

First the food. He has every household in Jimo set out an offering to its ancestors in the courtyard before every meal — 食必祭其先祖於庭 — and within days the birds of the coast are coming down over the walls in numbers, wheeling and dropping into the streets all day for the food. The Yan sentries watch them come and do not like it: 燕人怪之. *Only then* does Tian Dan supply the explanation. **神來下教我** — a divine teacher is coming down to instruct me. A soldier, joking, asks whether it could be him, and finds himself installed, facing east, in robes, receiving Tian Dan's bows; every order in Jimo from then on is issued as the teacher's.

Then he lets it be known — carefully, in the way things leak — that there is only one thing he fears: that Yan will cut the noses off the Qi prisoners and set them in the front rank.

Yan does it.

The men on the wall watch it done. And a city that had been quietly rehearsing the words of surrender now understands with total clarity that the gate can never be opened.

He lets it be known that there is one other thing: that if Yan were to dig up the ancestral graves in the ground outside the walls and burn what is in them, it would break the heart of every man in Jimo.

Yan does that too.

From the parapet they can see the mounds opened and the smoke. Sima Qian says the men of Jimo wept, and asked to be let out to fight, and their fury multiplied tenfold. **勇怯，勢也.** These are the same men who have been behind this wall since the siege closed. Nothing has been added to them. Something has been taken away from behind them.

Now Tian Dan spends three days making Yan comfortable. Elders and women on the walls; the fighting men off them, out of sight. Envoys go out to discuss the terms of surrender. The rich families of Jimo send a thousand *yi* of gold to the Yan officers with a small polite request — when the city falls, spare our households, do not take our women. The Yan camp is delighted. The siege lines relax the way besiegers relax when the negotiation has started.

The crossbow is at full draw. Every day since the succession has been spent cranking it.

Night. In the wall, dozens of holes, dug through from the inside and not opened until now.

They have collected over a thousand oxen. Each is dressed in crimson silk painted with five-coloured dragon patterns — 被以絳繒衣，畫以五彩龍文, which is what the *Shiji* says and is therefore what the Historian says. Blades are lashed to the horns. Reeds soaked in fat are bound to the tails. Five thousand picked men follow behind with their mouths bitted for silence — 銜枚 — and behind those, every old man, woman and child in Jimo with a bronze vessel and something to hit it with.

The tails are lit. The walls are opened.

**節如發機.**

What comes out of the wall of Jimo is not an army, and the Yan camp has no category for it: a thousand burning things running in the dark with metal on their heads, and everything they touch dies. Then five thousand silent men in the smoke behind them. Then, from the whole circuit of the city, a sound like the sky being beaten with a hammer — the entire population striking bronze, the noise arriving from every direction at once, so that the Yan army, already running, cannot find the direction they are running from.

Qi Jie is killed in the rout.

The pursuit does not stop at the siege lines. City after city that had gone over to Yan turns as Tian Dan comes up the road, and by the end of it Qi has all seventy-odd back, and King Xiang is brought from Ju to Linzi and put on the throne, and Tian Dan is made Lord Anping.

He never won an engagement he had not already finished building.

> **Record vs. legend.** *Shiji* 82 is the source for the whole sequence: the sawn axles at Anping (the text does not date the modification to any particular night, and says only 為燕所虜, taken by Yan — not cavalry); the reverse-espionage that removed Yue Yi; the courtyard offerings, then the birds, then 燕人怪之, then the claim 神來下教我 and the installation of the soldier as oracle — **in that order**, the wonder first and the explanation after; the nose-cutting; the graves; the bribes; the fire-oxen; Qi Jie's death; the recovery of the seventy-odd cities; the title Lord Anping. The five-year figure is *Shiji* 80's 留徇齊五歲 and belongs to Yue Yi's occupation of Qi as a whole; Ju and Jimo held out for years, but the *Shiji* nowhere gives the length of the close siege of Jimo, and this page does not invent one. **279 BC is a conventional reconstruction**, synchronised with the death of King Zhao of Yan; the *liezhuan* states no year, and the *Shiji* gives no location for the king's death. The counts — a thousand-odd oxen, five thousand picked men, a thousand *yi* of gold — are Sima Qian's and are unverified elsewhere.
>
> **What the chapter is, honestly.** *Shiji* 82 is a compiled heroic account, and the dragon-painted silk in particular has the shape of folk memory. Treat the *sequence* as recorded, the *numbers* as reported, and the whole as a story that had already been shaped for telling before Sima Qian received it. Nothing on this page claims that Tian Dan executed a 奇正 doctrine or that 279 BC "happened" the way a controlled demonstration happens. What the page claims is narrower and survivable: **this is the episode the chapter's doctrine reads best against, and the reading is ours, not the record's.**

*Boundary with Chapter 12:* this is fire as one night's release mechanism, not 火攻 doctrine. Chapter 12 keeps Red Cliffs, keeps the ember system, and keeps vermilion. Chapter 5 renders the oxen **in ink only** — no flame sprites, no embers, no dragon motif anywhere in frame. The fire here is 節, not 火, and the costume stays in the Historian's prose where the *Shiji* put it.

## E. VISUAL CUE — "THE CITY THAT HAS NOT LET GO"

- **Environment.** Jimo, and the only **vertically composed** chapter in the thirteen. The city is drawn as a pressurised reservoir seen from an impossible angle: a tight dense enclosure of high density (D ≈ 0.60) held on an elevated contour in the upper third of a 2.39:1 frame that has been rotated in the reader's mind, and below it the Yan siege flats — wide, loose, low, D ≤ 0.05 — occupying the lower two-thirds as **reserved white**, an untouched fall. No horizon. The enemy is present but formless: siege lines as the faintest possible dry contour, never a mass. Mean density opens at ~0.11 and does not move for the entire accumulation phase. *(This is the chapter's own image — 轉圓石於千仞之山, the round stone at the top of the mountain — with the stone made of the thing the vignette is actually about. The compressed city is the boulder.)*
- **Dominant ink behaviour — G, THE GRAIN FIELD (this chapter owns it).** The anisotropy vector field is made *visible* for the first time: fibre direction θ is rendered as the faintest possible tooth in the paper, and the reader can read the slope by reading the grain. The dense enclosure sits at the top at maximum load, **not moving** — held by nothing but the fact that it has not been released. Bleed is at zero because there is no channel out of it: the walls are the sizing, S = 1.0 all the way round, and the dozens of breaches cut from the inside are microscopic incisions in that line that the reader can find only by looking for them. Everything in this chapter is the ink that has not run yet. The dread is entirely a dread of gradient.
- **Particle system.** **Precursor motion**: dry grit, paper lint and single fibres sliding down the gradient *before* any ink does — the sheet telling you which way it will fail, three seconds before it fails. Then, at release only, a burst of 飛白 flecks shed where velocity outruns the brush's reserve. No smoke, no embers, no sparks: those are Chapter 12's and they are not lent out.
- **Lighting.** A single hard raking light, near-parallel to the sheet, from screen left. It exists to make the *slope* legible — ink sitting proud of the fibre throws a one-pixel contact shadow, so mass and height are readable as physical facts. This is the opposite of Chapter 4's shadowless flat, and the two chapters should be identifiable from a single frame at thumbnail size.
- **Camera move.** A slow crane **up** the scarp of the city — the only vertical camera move in the site — 85mm, accumulating height for the whole chapter, so the reader spends the chapter's runtime *building the thing that is about to be spent*. The chapter contains exactly **one** cut, and it is the release. It fires on the reader's action, at 2.76:1, and it is the hardest cut in the thirteen chapters: one frame of drum, ≤3px punch, and the metal (金) is conspicuously absent, because nothing is coming back.
- **The one image they will remember.** The whole dark mass of the city held above two-thirds of untouched paper — and then that entire white spent in eleven frames as the breaches open and the stored load discharges down the steepest line of G, tearing into 飛白 as it goes, arriving at the bottom of the sheet and running out across the loose Yan lines before the reader's hand has left the input. **You cannot buy the white back. You had it for the whole chapter and you were looking at the city.**

## F. TACTIC DECISION NODE — "THE DRAW"

**Situation (Commander):**
> Their new general is careless and their lines have gone soft, and tonight you could hurt them. Every extra day you spend making them softer is a day the men on your wall spend deciding you are afraid. Say when.

**Option A — RELEASE NOW.** The enemy is complacent enough. Strike tonight, into an army that is merely relaxed, with men who are merely willing, and out of a city that has not yet been welded shut behind them.

**Option B — CRANK IT FURTHER.** Spend three more provocations — invite the atrocity that will weld your own people to the wall, open the surrender negotiation, take his officers' attention with gold — and release into an army that has stopped being an army, with men who cannot be stopped. Risk: any one of the three, read correctly, tells him exactly what you are doing.

**Simulated visual consequence — A.**
The release is granted immediately and the fall *starts beautifully* — down the grain, the sound is right, the drum lands. But the load behind the gate is what it was three days ago and the Yan perimeter below is still closed and dense. Insufficient mass, insufficient gradient: the stroke goes out thin (D ≈ 0.20), tears into 飛白 within its first third because velocity has outrun a reserve that was never built up, and one third of the way down the sheet it runs into the intact enemy line and **stops**. Along the whole halted front, ∂M/∂t crosses zero and a **tide line** crystallises: bright, sharp, permanent, at exactly the height the reader chose. Page density rises ~0.05 for nothing. The remaining two-thirds of white are still there — but the load is gone, the crane cannot re-accumulate, and the city above is now a spent enclosure. The chapter's last shot is that stop-line held for nine seconds at 85mm with no music, and the reader is made to read the height they settled for. **The white they kept is not a saving. It is a distance they failed to cross.**

**Simulated visual consequence — B.**
Three sub-beats, and none of them mark your page. What changes is *below*: with each provocation the Yan lines spread and thin — the perimeter widens to hold a negotiation, to work the graves, to take the gold — and the reader watches channels open along G where the enemy contour has gone slack. Above, the crane climbs, the raking light steepens, the precursor grit runs faster and starts earlier before each beat, and under it the room tone thins.

Each provocation is stamped, and none of them is stamped the same way, because they are not the same act: a single hairline vermilion incision across the wall line for the mutilated prisoners; a dull oxidised smear of cinnabar over the grave mounds; a hard stamped seal on the Yan officers' gold tally. Three separate forms of expenditure, at the colour doctrine's ~2.5× viscosity, none of them blending into the grey, because consequence never dissolves into the gloom and the reader is buying this with somebody. Density gain across all three: ~0.006.

Then the release, and the fall runs the entire sheet, edge to edge, bottom-out — a heavy concentrated 濃墨 surge down the steepest descent of G, straight through the channels the three provocations opened — page density +0.13 in under a second, and the frame is unreadable for four frames before the bloom resolves. **You spent all of it. It worked. Three of the marks on this page are not the enemy's.**

**HISTORICAL VERDICT (revealed at M ≈ 0).**
*Again: this is the reading the doctrine draws from the episode. Shiji 82 is a compiled heroic account and the verdict does not upgrade it to a controlled experiment.*

Sun Tzu: **其勢險，其節短。勢如彍弩，節如發機** — and the reason B is the chapter is **勇怯，勢也**. The fury Tian Dan releases was not in his men in the spring. On the *Shiji*'s account he manufactured it, by advertising two fears in order to have an enemy realise them, and the account is explicit that this was the intention. **What the chapter does not let you off:** the three vermilion marks on your sheet are Qi prisoners' faces and Qi families' graves. The Historian says that plainly and does not resolve it, and does not let the word 勢 do the resolving either.

A has its own anchor at the opposite end of the same principle — Duke Xiang of Song at the Hong River in 638 BC. *Zuozhuan*, Xi 22: the Song were formed up and the Chu army was still crossing the water; the 司馬 said *they are many and we are few — strike them before they are across*, and the duke said 不可. Chu finished crossing and had not yet formed; the 司馬 said it again, and the duke said 未可. Only after the crossing and the forming did Song attack, and Song was routed and the duke wounded; he died of it the next year. The famous chivalry — 不鼓不成列, one does not drum against a line that has not formed — is his *retrospective* justification for the defeat, delivered afterwards, not a lecture staged in advance. (The adviser is named in the text as 司馬; the tradition divides over whether this is 司馬固 or 子魚 / 公子目夷, and we leave that open rather than pick one.) **The trigger is a moment, not a mood: too early is a tide line, too late is a corpse, and neither is a failure of courage.**

## G. CODEX MODE ENTRY — 5 兵勢

> **求之於勢，不責於人。** Look for it in the arrangement. Stop demanding it of people.

- **勢 is height; 節 is when you stop holding it.** Rolling water moves rock because of the drop. A hawk breaks a spine because of the instant. Steep potential, short release — the two are one specification.
- **奇 has no meaning alone.** The extraordinary force acts only on the commitment the ordinary force extracted. Surprise with nothing fixed in place is not 奇, it is a raid, and it is how clever people lose.
- **Courage is an output.** Order and disorder come from organisation; bravery and cowardice come from configuration. The same men, differently arranged, are different men.

**Apply it:** When a line gives way, do not begin with the man who gave way. Find the gradient he was standing on — what was behind him, what was open to his flank, how far the nearest support was, what his best possible effort would have bought — and find out who built it that way. Where the geometry dictates the breaking point, the individual only registers it. Then note that you are usually the one who built it.

# CHAPTER 6 — 虛實

## A. IDENTITY

| Field | Value |
|---|---|
| Number | 6 (close of THE SHAPE; the act's payoff) |
| Chinese title | 虛實 |
| Pinyin | **Xū Shí** |
| Literal | "empty / full" — void and substance, hollow and solid |
| Standard English | *Weak Points and Strong* (Giles) — also *Emptiness and Fullness*, *Void and Actuality* |
| **Sharper alternative** | **MAKING HIM MOVE** |

*Why the alternative.* "Weak Points and Strong" implies the weak points are out there, waiting to be found, like a search problem. The chapter's actual argument is that weak points are **manufactured**: you do not locate his emptiness, you *summon* it, by making him answer things — and every answer thins him somewhere. The chapter is not about targeting. It is about **who is compelling whose movement**. Hence: *Making Him Move.*

> **Title apparatus.** Received title **虛實**; the Yinqueshan title is **實虛**, in the reverse order, and shows the same one-graph-pair pattern as 形 and 勢. See the note under Chapter 4A.

## B. THE CORE STRATEGIC DICTUM

> **Do not go looking for his weak point — summon one, by compelling him to answer somewhere he cannot afford to ignore, and then arrive at the place his answer just emptied.**

Sun Tzu opens with the sentence the whole chapter hangs from: **致人而不致於人** — *the good commander summons the enemy to the field and is never summoned by him.* Everything after is machinery for that. Be first on the ground and rested; make him come. Attack **其所必救**, the thing he *must* rescue, and he will leave a strong position without being pushed out of it. Then the arithmetic: **形人而我無形，則我專而敵分** — force him into a visible shape while you have none, and you concentrate while he divides. A defender who does not know where the blow lands must be strong everywhere, and being strong everywhere is the definition of being weak: **無所不備，則無所不寡.** Concentrate as one against a tenth, and superiority is manufactured out of his uncertainty rather than out of your recruitment.

Then the image everybody quotes and almost nobody reads correctly: **夫兵形象水**, the army is shaped like water. The popular reading takes this as a poem about flexibility — be fluid, be adaptable, go with the flow, and it turns up on posters. But the sentence has a second half that is doing all the work: water *avoids the high and hurries to the low*; the army *avoids the full and strikes the empty*. Water is not being praised for being adaptable. It is being cited as a thing that is **utterly predictable about where it will go** — it takes the path of least resistance, always, without exception, without courage, without preference. Sun Tzu is not telling you to be free-flowing. He is telling you to be as unsentimental as gravity about where you spend force, and to stop attacking things because they are important, prestigious, or where the enemy is.

Second misreading: 無形, "formlessness," is not mysticism. It is an information state. It means *he cannot derive your dispositions*, and it is achieved by ordinary work — dispersion, feints, silence, tempo — not by transcendence. The chapter's own gloss is unromantic: **微乎微乎，至於無形；神乎神乎，至於無聲** — subtle to the point of having no shape, quiet to the point of no sound — *and therefore* you become the arbiter of the enemy's fate. It is an intelligence differential, expressed as poetry.

## C. THREE KEY LINES

### C1 — the initiative

- **Chinese (trad.):** 故善戰者，致人而不致於人。
- **Pinyin:** *Gù shàn zhàn zhě, zhì rén ér bù zhì yú rén.*
- **Literal:** "Thus one skilled at war brings others [to him], and is not brought by others."
- **Modern:** "The good ones summon the enemy to the ground of their choosing. They never march to his."
- **Confidence:** High.

### C2 — the arithmetic of dispersion

- **Chinese (trad.):** 故形人而我無形，則我專而敵分。我專為一，敵分為十，是以十攻其一也。
- **Pinyin:** *Gù xíng rén ér wǒ wú xíng, zé wǒ zhuān ér dí fēn. Wǒ zhuān wéi yī, dí fēn wéi shí, shì yǐ shí gōng qí yī yě.*
- **Literal:** "So if I give the enemy a form while I have none, then I am concentrated and he is divided. I concentrate as one; he divides into ten; thus it is ten attacking his one."
- **Modern:** "Make him legible and stay unreadable, and he splits while you gather. He becomes ten pieces. You are one. Every fight after that is ten against one."
- **Confidence:** High.

### C3 — water

- **Chinese (trad.):** 夫兵形象水，水之形，避高而趨下；兵之形，避實而擊虛。
- **Pinyin:** *Fū bīng xíng xiàng shuǐ, shuǐ zhī xíng, bì gāo ér qū xià; bīng zhī xíng, bì shí ér jī xū.*
- **Literal:** "Now the form of an army resembles water. Water's form avoids the high and runs to the low; an army's form avoids the full and strikes the empty."
- **Modern:** "An army moves like water. Water refuses height and takes the low ground, every time, without exception. So do not attack what is strong. Attack what is not there."
- **Confidence:** High on the sense; **medium on the graphs**, and the apparatus must be printed rather than assumed. Our base text has 水之**形** where part of the tradition reads 水之**行** ("water's *course*" rather than "water's *form*") — a one-graph difference that changes the sentence from a statement about shape to a statement about movement. The chapter's closing line has the same problem: received **故兵無常勢，水無常形** — *no constant configuration, no constant shape* — against a reported Yinqueshan reading along the lines of **兵無成勢，無恆形** (no completed 勢, no fixed 形, and without the 水), with early citation traditions (*Qunshu zhiyao*, *Taiping yulan*) pointing at further variants including 無定勢. **This is a crux of the same order as the 守則 crux in chapter 4 and the page must print both readings rather than presenting the received line as settled.** *(Confidence: high that the crux exists and that Yinqueshan diverges here; medium on the exact slip wording — verify against Wu Jiulong's apparatus at build.)* Followed by 能因敵變化而取勝者，謂之神.

**Bench:** 攻而必取者，攻其所不守也；守而必固者，守其所不攻也 — *attack where he does not defend; defend where he will not attack.* And the operational key: 故我欲戰，敵雖高壘深溝，不得不與我戰者，攻其所必救也 — *if I want battle, then though he sits behind high ramparts and deep ditches he must come out and fight me — because I attack what he cannot fail to rescue.*

**HISTORIAN'S NOTE.** This chapter contains what is usually offered as one of the text's few concrete geopolitical asides: 以吾度之，越人之兵雖多，亦奚益於勝敗哉 — *by my reckoning, though the men of Yue are numerous, what does it add to victory or defeat?* Read straight, the speaker is positioned in Wu, looking at Yue, and the traditional dating (Sun Wu at the court of King Helü of Wu, late 6th c. BC) leans on it. **We do not let it carry that weight.** The line has been suspected in the commentarial tradition — including the proposal that 吾 is an error for 吳, which would remove the first-person Wu speaker entirely — and the commentary tradition contains readings of 越 that are not geographically anchored at all. The Yinqueshan slips do not confirm a Helü-court origin for the sentence. Treat it as **traditional Wu–Yue colouring, possibly interpolated, and not a secure dating anchor.** *(Confidence: medium.)*

Many modern scholars date composition of the received text to the Warring States, roughly the 4th century BC. The load-bearing arguments are vocabulary and syntax, the chapter architecture, and the scale and institutional character of the armies described — mass infantry, sustained campaigns, a bureaucratised state behind them. Weapon references are a *weak* lever and should be demoted accordingly: crossbows are attested well before the 4th century and the cavalry references in the received text are themselves contested. The Yinqueshan find in 1972 did not settle the date; it settled a *different* question — it recovered both a *Sunzi* and a separate *Sun Bin Bingfa* in the same tomb, ending centuries of argument over whether Sun Wu and Sun Bin were one person. The Historian states the open question as open.

## D. THE VIGNETTE

### THE ROAD TO DALIANG
**353 BC · from Linzi in Qi to the Wei heartland, and a place called Guiling · Shiji 65 (孫子吳起列傳); Zhanguo Ce, Qi strategies; Zizhi Tongjian; the Yinqueshan Sun Bin Bingfa, chapter 擒龐涓**

The man with the strategy cannot stand up.

He rides in a covered supply cart, out of sight, because in the state of Wei both his feet were cut off and his face was tattooed — 以法刑斷其兩足而黥之 — on the recommendation of a fellow student who had studied the same books beside him and understood early that there was only room for one of them. That student's name is Pang Juan. He is currently in the west, finishing the siege of Handan.

Zhao's envoys have been in Linzi begging for weeks. King Wei of Qi has been in no hurry — the advice at court was to let the two of them grind each other first, and take the field only when both are worn — and now the moment has ripened. Tian Ji has the army. Sun Bin has the cart.

Tian Ji's plan is the plan any competent man makes: march west to Handan, relieve the siege, fight Wei's main force in front of the city it is besieging.

From inside the cart:

*A man untangling a knot does not haul on the cord. A man breaking up a fight does not step in swinging.*

Then, flatly, the sentence that is this chapter of Sun Tzu with the poetry taken out — **批亢搗虛**: strike at the throat, and pound into the void.

The reasoning takes ten seconds and is entirely about accounting. Wei and Zhao have been at each other for a year. Everything sharp Wei owns is in the west; what is left at home is the old and the tired. Daliang — the capital — is a city full of the state's treasury, the state's granaries, the king's household and no field army. So: do not go to Handan. Go and stand on the roads of Daliang. Take up position across his throat, in the place he is hollow. He will come back to us, and he will come back *fast*, which means he will come back strung out, and he will arrive having marched instead of having chosen.

Tian Ji does it.

And the Qi army spends the first phase of the campaign deliberately looking incompetent. Sun Bin throws the opening move away on purpose: he sends the army at Pingling, a place he has told Tian Ji plainly is hard to take, with hostile districts on either flank and a supply line that can be cut. Two city commanders — Qicheng and Gaotang, each a 都大夫 — are sent in against it and are destroyed. The slaughter is public, clumsy, and impossible to mistake.

That is the message. It is written in the only ink Pang Juan will believe: Qi's dead.

Then light chariots go west and rush the outskirts of Daliang, insolently, in the open. And behind them the Qi main body *thins itself* on purpose, sends detachments away, presents to every Wei scout the picture of a small, badly-led, overconfident force poking at a capital it cannot possibly hold.

In the west, Pang Juan is handed all of it at once: the capital threatened, the enemy contemptible, the enemy's generals apparently fools.

He does the thing he must do and the thing he wants to do, and they are the same thing, and that is how you know he has been shaped. He abandons his heavy baggage. He takes the fast column. He runs for home by forced marches, and every hour of that run he is converting his army from a besieging force into a queue.

They are waiting at Guiling.

The sources give the battle no tactical detail — 大破梁軍, the Wei army was heavily broken — but they are not ambiguous that it was a defeat, and it was a real one. What the sources also give is a piece of arithmetic that has been running since a cart in Linzi. Pang Juan brought his best men to a battlefield chosen by a man he had crippled, at a time chosen by that man, on legs that had been marching for days, to defend a city that was never actually assaulted, having left the prize he had spent a year taking.

Qi did not out-fight Wei at Guiling. Qi arrived as one thing and Wei arrived as several, and then Qi broke it.

> **Record vs. legend.** *Shiji* 65 records: Sun Bin's mutilation in Wei (斷其兩足 — **both feet**, not kneecaps; the punishment named 臏 is conventionally kneecap-removal and is also his cognomen, but the sentence describing what was done to him says feet, and the terminological tension should be printed rather than smoothed); his concealment in a covered cart; Tian Ji as commander with Sun Bin as strategist; the 批亢搗虛 speech; the march on Daliang instead of Handan; Wei's departure from Handan (魏果去邯鄲); and the defeat at Guiling.
>
> **Handan was not saved.** *Zizhi Tongjian* has the city surrender to Wei first — 「十月，邯鄲降魏」 — and only then 「魏師還，與齊戰於桂陵，魏師大敗」. The Qi manoeuvre did not lift a siege; it caught an army going home from a siege it had already won. Handan returned to Zhao in 351 BC under the covenant on the Zhang River, two years later and by treaty. Say this plainly wherever the vignette's own rhetoric ("one movement lifts the siege") is quoted: that is Sun Bin's *pitch*, not the outcome.
>
> **The excavated layer.** The Pingling feint, the two sacrificed 都大夫 of Qicheng and Gaotang, the light chariots at Daliang and the deliberate thinning of the force come from **擒龐涓**, the opening chapter of the *Sun Bin Bingfa* excavated at Yinqueshan in 1972 and unread for two thousand years before that — an early and independent witness. The slips record Sun Bin proposing to the commander that the two 都大夫 be spent in order to make the army look incompetent. **They do not say whether the two men were told.** (Note also that 齊城 is a place-name, not the state of Qi; rendering it "the magistrate of Qi" implies the capital or the state and is wrong.) The title of that chapter claims Pang Juan was *captured* at Guiling, which cannot be squared with the *Shiji*'s account of his death years later at Maling. **The conflict is unresolved in the scholarship and we present it as unresolved**, including in the verdict box. Whether Pang Juan personally commanded at Guiling is likewise attested by the excavated text and not by the *Shiji*. Note finally that stitching the excavated Pingling campaign onto the *Shiji*'s Handan campaign is a modern reconstruction joining two textual traditions, not two witnesses to one battle.
>
> **The idiom.** 圍魏救趙, "besiege Wei to rescue Zhao," does not appear in the *Shiji*. It is a later crystallisation — the form in circulation belongs to the 三十六計 tradition — conventionally traced back to this campaign. The campaign is Warring States; the four-character phrase is not.

## F. TACTIC DECISION NODE — "WHERE HE IS NOT"

**Situation (Commander):**
> Your ally's capital has been under siege all year. His envoys are in your hall every morning, weeping, and this morning they say that if you do not march *at the siege* they will make terms and hand the city over — and the whole of the empire will watch you decline to save an ally. Your king is wavering. The enemy's entire striking force is parked in front of that city, in the west. Which means the enemy's own capital currently contains a treasury, a granary, a king, and no army. Choose the road.

**Option A — RELIEVE THE SIEGE.** March west to the besieged city and fight the enemy's main force where it stands: on ground it has held and prepared for a year, in front of the walls, in front of the empire, with your ally's survivors joining you from inside. It is what you were asked for and it is what will be remembered if you refuse it.

**Option B — MARCH ON HIS CAPITAL.** Ignore the siege entirely. Stand on the roads of the city he cannot lose, look weaker than you are, and make him come the whole way east to you. Your ally is not told. Your ally may not last.

**Simulated visual consequence — A.**
The crane finally stops, and that stillness is the tell. Your stroke travels west across moving water toward the densest mark on the sheet, widening as it goes, and arrives at his siege works — where the ground has been prepared for a year and reads S = 1.0 all along the line. Your ink does not penetrate. It **pools outside the ramparts**, and while it is pooling his interior reserve comes out and takes it against the walls. What forms there is the ugliest object in Act II: a dark, jagged, bleeding blotch of static attrition (D ≈ 0.22) that neither side can move, sitting on ground you did not choose. Page density jumps ~0.09 — the act's remaining budget in one exchange, and the act closes over its ceiling, which the Act III opening frame will show. And the ally's city is still under enemy ink, because it fell weeks ago and nothing in this stroke reached it. **You marched to a battle he had prepared. He did not have to move at all.**

**Simulated visual consequence — B.**
You make one small **淡墨** mark — pale, high-water, deliberately contingent — on the road outside his capital, and the ink budget barely registers it (+0.004). Then the camera goes to 180mm and **stays there**, and for eleven seconds nothing happens except what the enemy does to himself. His siege deposit in the west stays dark and stays where it is — it is the one thing on this sheet that will never move again, and it is now a hundred miles from the war. Out of its eastern edge a stroke is drawn: the fast column, and it elongates along the road, and as it elongates its reserve runs out. It goes 飛白. It breaks into segments as the fast elements outrun the slow. Ten marks strung along one line where there had been one mass, every one of them thinner than the source, none of them able to re-concentrate because overlapping pale marks make a wider stain and never a denser one.

The only place the sheet darkens is at **Guiling** — a small, dense, terminal deposit where the head of that column meets a mark that has been sitting there, dry and concentrated, since before he started walking. Total cost to your page: less than a fifth of option A. **The ink that was spent was his, and he spent it on his own legs.**

**HISTORICAL VERDICT (revealed at M ≈ 0).**
*This is the reading the chapter's doctrine draws from 353 BC. The narrative below is assembled from three traditions — Shiji 65, the Zhanguo Ce, and the excavated 擒龐涓 — that do not entirely agree, and the disagreements are named rather than resolved.*

Sun Tzu: **致人而不致於人**; **故我欲戰，敵雖高壘深溝，不得不與我戰者，攻其所必救也**; **我專為一，敵分為十，是以十攻其一也**. B is the chapter, and B is 353 BC. Tian Ji proposed A. Sun Bin, from inside the cart, vetoed it in one sentence — *do not haul on the knot; strike the throat and pound the void* — and sent the Qi army at Daliang instead. Wei left Handan, force-marched east, and was broken at Guiling by an army that had chosen the ground, the hour, and the enemy's condition on arrival.

**Three things the verdict does not get to round off.** First: **Handan was not rescued.** It had already surrendered to Wei; it came back to Zhao two years later by treaty, on the Zhang River. The manoeuvre saved an ally's *state*, not his capital, and the ally paid for the interval. Second: the price of the picture is on the excavated slips and not in Sima Qian — Sun Bin proposed spending two of his own city commanders at Pingling, a position he had already told Tian Ji was not takeable, in order to look like a fool worth chasing. Whether those men knew is not recorded, and inventing their ignorance would make the moment worse in a way the sources do not support; what is recorded is that a strategist proposed them as expenditure and a commander agreed. **Formlessness is not free and it is not clean.** Third: **we do not know what happened to Pang Juan.** The excavated chapter is titled 擒龐涓 — *the capture of Pang Juan* — and the *Shiji* has him alive for another thirteen years and dying at Maling. The two cannot both be right and this page does not pick.

So the sequel is offered as a sequel *in the sources*, not as a fact about a man: **the *Shiji* says 後十三歲, thirteen years later, and puts Pang Juan on the road again — conventionally dated 342 or 341 BC, and the date is contested.** That second campaign is a different war (Wei against Han, with Qi again striking at Daliang and the pursuit ending in a defile) and it is not a repeat of this road. And its famous mechanism — the diminishing cooking fires, the message cut into the tree, the massed crossbows in the dark — belongs to the same *Shiji* 65 narrative stratum as the concubine-drill anecdote that Hong Mai already dismissed in the Song as the work of 好事者. It is a story about arithmetic. It is not independently corroborated arithmetic, and the Historian says so before anyone quotes it.

## G. CODEX MODE ENTRY — 6 虛實

> **致人而不致於人。** Summon the enemy. Never answer his summons.

- **Weak points are made, not found.** Attack what he cannot fail to rescue, and he will leave a position no assault could have taken. Every answer he gives thins him somewhere else.
- **Uncertainty is a force multiplier you do not have to fund.** Give him a shape, keep none yourself, and he must be strong everywhere — which is the technical definition of being weak everywhere. He divides into ten and you are still one.
- **Water is not a poem about flexibility.** Water is cited because it is *pitiless and predictable*: it refuses height, always, and takes the low ground, always. Be that unsentimental about where you spend force. Do not attack a thing because it is important.

**Apply it:** Audit who is initiating movement and who is answering it. If you are dispatching force to hold positions he chose, you have already lost the initiative, whatever the map says — and reinforcing them will not get it back. Stop feeding the perimeter. Threaten the one thing he cannot afford to leave unguarded, and let him do the marching.
